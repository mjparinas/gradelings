PREFACE
Many people assume that learning more than one language is confusing and, 
in fact, detrimental to the intellectual development of the child. Those who 
can speak two or even more languages would completely disagree with such 
an assessment, no matter what languages they speak. 
This is because the language instinct that we are all born with does not 
behave and is not structured like a computer memory. Our brains and 
our abilities to think about and manipulate ideas (for amusement, to solve 
problems, etc.) just do not have the same limitations as our technologies do. 
Humans, to be sure, cannot make instantaneous calculations as computers 
can, but we can achieve and do so much more. We are human. 
The language a child is exposed to first is the language they will acquire 
first. An Inuit child is more likely to develop linguistic competence and 
performance, initiated since birth, in a predominantly Inuktut-enriched 
environment. The form of Inuktut—in other words, the dialect the child is 
exposed to—is what they will use to think human thoughts, revel in human 
ideas, and so much more.
A child’s first language actually forms the foundation for learning another 
language, which is a crucial skill and way of being in Nunavut that our 
children need in order to thrive and prosper; we should never forget this as 
parents, teachers, institutions, and caregivers. This is a fact borne out by 
study after study, and it is a philosophy that informs the long-standing policy 
of the Government of Nunavut’s Department of Education to educate Inuit 
children in the Inuktut stream up to Grade 3.
2.1 Preface

The Inuktut Language Arts 1st Language Curriculum (IL1), the Inuktut 
Language Arts 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced (IL1e), and its sister 
document, the Inuktut 2nd Language Curriculum (IL2), are designed to expand 
on and extend this vision by insisting on describing Inuktut Language Arts 
in terms of their formal linguistic principles, such as those that govern 
grammatical, syntactical, phonological, and lexical considerations in 
relation to phrase-formation rules. This necessarily goes beyond teaching 
orthography (syllabics and roman) to a grammar-based, narrative-directed 
Inuit language instruction on a formalized, graduated scale intended to gauge 
degrees of the increasingly emergent creative and spontaneous novel usage of 
language on the part of the child.
This document is designed to use the ever-expanding library of levelled 
readers that emphasize vocabulary- and phrase-decoding and building rules 
of thumb for the students, and it relies on the lesson planning, rollout ideas, 
and suggestions in the IL1 Educator Support Handbook.
And, oh, we must never forget that language arts, especially Inuktut 
Language Arts, should be fun and interesting to draw on and keep the 
attention of these young, developing, growing minds—ideally for life.
Jaypeetee Arnakak 
Iqaluit, Nunavut

As stated in the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for Nunavut 
Curriculum, “Schooling in Nunavut should provide support to students in all 
areas of their development so that they can achieve personal goals, become 
well-equipped to contribute and serve their families and communities, 
demonstrate leadership and healthy attitudes, and be able to actively 
participate and contribute as Nunavut takes on new roles in the global 
community.”¹ To achieve this vision for education in Nunavut, it is imperative 
that the curriculum in all subject areas promote holistic critical thinking 
and encourage continuous learning that can be transferred across contexts. 
It must also promote inclusion and provide opportunities to differentiate 
learning experiences to meet the needs of all students. The updated Nunavut 
Curriculum Framework will provide a common structure for the curriculum 
in each subject area and support this holistic, cross-curricular approach to 
teaching and learning.

The Curriculum Foundations—Nunavusiutit, Iqqaqqaukkaringniq, 
Uqausiliriniq, and Aulajaaqtut—are at the centre of the curriculum in each 
subject area. According to the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework, 
the foundations “are generic in nature, transcend subject-specific content 
areas, and result in the application and transfer of specific learning to new 
contexts and situations. This approach to learning is designed to focus on the 
development of complex intellectual (metacognitive) skills and lead students 
to transformational ways of thinking and processing. This type of approach 
helps students understand the connections between various learnings and 
the strategies that lead to successful application of learning in new contexts.”² 
Within the Curriculum Foundations are the subject areas. These are areas 
of learning organized as discrete learning disciplines and taught in systemic 
ways over time.

The aims and principles of the Curriculum Foundations inform the Major 
Understandings in each subject area. Major Understandings are broad 
statements that reflect the essential principles and core ideas within a 
subject area that can be transferred across different areas of learning.³ These 
Major Understandings anchor the curriculum in the purpose for learning 
in each subject area and the values of Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ). They 
act as a lens through which teachers can make decisions about planning, 
instruction, and assessment. 
The Major Understandings are explored through the Learning Strands, 
which represent the key concepts and processes within each subject area 
and the relationships among them. The Learning Strands remain consistent 
across grades and will encourage a richer exploration of how these concepts 
and processes work together over time. Within each Learning Strand, 
there are Learning Concepts and grade-specific Learning Outcomes. The 
Learning Concepts outline the constructs, processes, and patterns within 
each Learning Strand. These concepts provide a structure through which 
students can organize and connect the Learning Outcomes. Learning 
Outcomes define the key knowledge, skills, and understandings students will 
develop at each grade level. These become the building blocks that support 
deeper conceptual understanding.
In addition, each grade level has Guiding Questions. The Guiding Questions
will help link the Learning Concepts and the Learning Outcomes back to 
the Curriculum Foundations. By building connections among the knowledge, 
skills, and understandings they have acquired, students will be able to explore 
the principles of the Curriculum Foundations in age-appropriate ways.
Teachers can use the Guiding Questions:
• In daily classroom activities and discussions
• As part of assessment tasks
• To plan units or themes
• To inspire inquiry-based projects

ARTS 1ST LANGUAGE CURRICULUM (IL1)
An Inuit view of language learning can be compared to a child learning how 
to compose, sing, and perform a drum dance. At first, the child may not even 
be able to hold up the drum. However, the child will progress from imitating 
songs modelled by the teacher to creating and performing a song and drum 
dance independently, expressing themselves through voice and movement.
ELDER’S STORY
“Drum dancing and sharing songs 
and learning the songs of others was 
a way of bonding with each other 
and expressing deep emotions. This 
was very powerful in our camps. 
Young people used to have songs as 
well and these could be very powerful. 
I used to have a song about working 
and earning a dollar a day and being 
able to buy bullets. This song was 
made by my mother, but I was being 
taught about the terminology and she helped with the tune. Even little 
children who had trouble holding up a drum were assisted to dance 
and sing. The song writing relationship was two-way, reinforcing the 
respect and relationship between teacher and learner.” 
Donald Uluadluak (June 2010)

Inuktut Language Arts 1st Language Curriculum 3.7
Inunnguiniq
Inunnguiniq is a process within Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ) that intends 
to make a child a capable human being. Collectively, it unites families, 
schools, and communities to work together and care for, raise, and teach 
our students. Inunnguiniq is grounded in the strengths of relationships 
and effective communication. Learning Inuktut in an Inunnguiniq way 
is founded on and supported by IQ Principles and Inuit ways of knowing. 
Inuktut is a nature-embedded language and inherently expresses the 
relationships between people and the environment. 
Inunnguiniq develops interconnectedness and requires language and 
respectful interaction. There is an emphasis on oral fluency and visualization 
for both verbal and memory development. The reliance on strong language 
and communication skills provides a foundation for developing the profound 
conceptual functioning required in the way Inuit describe “deep thinking” 
and the ability to become effective in seeking solutions. Inunnguiniq ensures 
a solid foundation for the future of every student and future generations. 
The study of Inuktut as an additional language enables each student 
to understand and appreciate Inuktut and to use it confidently and 
competently in a variety of situations for communication, personal 
satisfaction, and learning. Research has shown that emphasizing oral 
communication and providing a language-rich environment that stresses 
purpose and understanding from the start is a crucial factor in language 
learning.

3.8 Introduction
Instruction of Inuktut Language Arts requires collaboration with Inuit Elders 
in order to infuse core IQ values and beliefs into teaching and learning 
methods. Language learning is a process and requires the active and ongoing 
involvement of Elders and other community members to ensure that IQ lives 
in all our schools. 
Our ultimate responsibility as educators and partners in education is to 
instill in our students the value of Inuit culture and language. By studying 
Inuktut Language Arts, we will teach the importance of understanding, 
compassion, harmony, and unity among Nunavummiut and the world. 
This living curriculum contributes to the process of reconciliation. Through 
teaching and learning, we serve as bridges to heal past hurts and injustices. 
Teach with Inunnguiniq as our foundation, and our children will develop into 
capable human beings.
The iglu is a symbol of survival and 
has become a symbol for K–12 education 
in Nunavut. The one-block-at-a-time 
metaphor embodies the stages of 
development children pass through 
as they progress through public 
schooling. Upon graduation, the 
child will have constructed an iglu 
strong enough to withstand their 
own weight and will be able to stand 
upon the iglu to see what other 
opportunities they can build in the 
community and beyond. 
The children of Nunavut must be equipped to survive in this ever-changing 
world. The age-old tools an Inuit child was given outfitted that child to 
survive the challenges of the land as well as life within a community of 
people. The caribou antler in the child’s hand represents the important 
connection to the environment. The environment has always supplied the 
natural resources required for success in the world. The antler is also a 
reminder of the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ) value of practise and the 
importance of continual improvement in order to become successful. 

Inuktut Language Arts 1st Language Curriculum 3.9
Inuktut Language Arts curricula
In 2018, the Inuktut Language Arts 1st Language Foundation Curriculum
(IL1-F) was completed. This is a foundational research document that 
uncovered contexts and assumptions to be considered when creating a firstlanguage curriculum and formed the basis of subsequent language curricula.
The research and development used to write the IL1-F has guided the 
creation of a full complement of Inuktut Language Arts and English language 
curricula, including: 
• IL1e: Inuktut Language Arts 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced
• IL1: Inuktut Language Arts 1st Language Curriculum
• IL2: Inuktut 2nd Language Curriculum
• EL1: English Language Arts 1st Language Curriculum
• EL2: English 2nd Language Curriculum
About the IL1
The IL1 was researched, written, and reviewed by a working group of educational 
and Inuktut-language experts from across Nunavut. The working group 
carefully created a curriculum that is accessible, balanced, and reflective 
of the unique nature of Inuktut as a language and the culture of Nunavut. 
The IL1 outlines grade-level Learning Outcomes for Inuktut first-language 
students in communities where there may be some language erosion. 
The IL1 provides inclusive entry points for students with varying levels of 
Inuktut language development entering the school system. Implementing 
the IL1 allows schools to meet students where they are in their language 
development and provides additional space and time for all students to 
strengthen their oral language skills in the early elementary years, as needed. 
The IL1 also provides a framework for engaging students in learning that 
is accessible, flexible, and reflective of the unique nature of Inuktut within 
a Nunavut-specific context. Schools should implement the IL1 to support 
students in strengthening their oral language development. This helps 
support schools in their implementation of the Qulliq Model. If students 
exceed the expectations of the IL1, schools have the option to shift to the IL1e
to further support the language needs of their students.

3.12 Introduction
Organization of the IL1 Curriculum Foundation
The IL1 is part of the Uqausiliriniq Curriculum Foundation. The principles 
of the Uqausiliriniq Curriculum Foundation focus on relationships in 
communication and language development, creative and artistic expression, 
and reflective and critical thinking.
Major Understandings
The principles of Uqausiliriniq inform the three Major Understandings 
within the IL1 that continue from Kindergarten to Grade 6. These Major 
Understandings reflect the essential learning within the subject area and can 
act as a lens through which teachers can make decisions about planning, 
instruction, and assessment. The Major Understandings for the IL1 are: 
1. Learning and valuing Inuktut is essential for the preservation of Inuit 
identity, culture, values, and worldview.
2. Communicating effectively helps us to build relationships and participate 
more meaningfully in our communities. 
3. Making meaning in Inuktut encourages us to think critically and 
creatively and helps us come to common understandings. 

Inuktut Language Arts 1st Language Curriculum 3.13
Learning Strands and Concepts 
The IL1 has been organized by grade and then further broken down into 
three Learning Strands: Oral Communication, Reading, and Writing. The 
grade-specific Learning Outcomes within each Learning Strand have been 
organized according to their Learning Concept so that there is a clear 
progression of student learning from Kindergarten to Grade 6.
Oral Communication 
Oral communication includes speaking and listening skills, which are 
essential for thinking, learning, and developing social skills. Listening 
and speaking enable students to explore ideas and concepts as well as 
understand and organize their experiences and knowledge. As students 
progress through the grades, they will communicate their thoughts, 
feelings, experiences, information, and opinions and learn to understand 
themselves and others. Oral language carries a community's stories, values, 
beliefs, and traditions.
The Learning Concepts in the Oral Communication Learning Strand:
OC.1 Listening to understand
Students show their understanding of Inuktut through the use of verbal 
and non-verbal responses. From Kindergarten to Grade 3, students 
recognize familiar, grade-appropriate vocabulary in classroom routines, 
games, and activities and listen for different purposes. From Grades 4 to 
6, students demonstrate an understanding of increasingly complex oral 
language and use a variety of verbal and non-verbal cues and strategies 
to engage physically and mentally with speakers and oral text. 
OC.2 Speaking to communicate
Students use expressive language processes in order to be understood 
by others. The Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept focus on 
the knowledge and skills students need to organize their thoughts 
and make appropriate language choices in order to communicate 
effectively for specific purposes and audiences. Students will incorporate 
new vocabulary into their growing linguistic tool kit to speak with 
confidence and ease in a variety of contexts. 

3.14 Introduction
OC.3 Interacting with others 
Students engage in listening and speaking when interacting with 
others. The Learning Outcomes in this concept focus on promoting 
Tunnganarniq, which encompasses inclusive and open ways of 
communication, and Inuuqatigiitsiarniq, which encompasses the 
understanding, cooperation, and mutual caring that build relationships 
between people. Students first learn how to respond to greetings and 
engage in simple exchanges. As students progress through the grades, 
they begin to participate in conversations and collaborate with others to 
complete tasks and solve problems. 
OC.4 Traditional stories and oral history 
Sharing and discussing traditional stories and oral history is an 
important part of cultural heritage. The Learning Outcomes in this 
Learning Concept are the same in grades 5 and 6. The progression is 
connected to the length and complexity of the traditional stories the 
students listen to and retell.
Reading
Reading involves making meaning from texts. Students begin to develop 
lifelong reading habits and attitudes toward reading in the early years of 
school. Supporting students' reading skills in a nurturing and engaging 
learning environment encourages students to develop an interest in and 
love of reading. Students will demonstrate Pilimmaksarniq as they develop 
their reading skills through continued practise. Students become more 
independent as readers by developing and applying skills and strategies 
for decoding, comprehension, and fluency. They will explore a variety of 
texts with themes related to Nunavut and IQ and engage with different 
perspectives to gain new understandings of the world around them. 

Inuktut Language Arts 1st Language Curriculum 3.15
The Learning Concepts in the Reading Learning Strand are:
R.1 Comprehension 
Comprehension refers to processes students need to interact with and 
make meaning from texts. The Learning Outcomes in this concept focus on helping students develop comprehension strategies and respond 
to texts through creative and critical means. To support their reading, 
students first learn about comprehension strategies through teacher 
modelling and direct instruction and then learn to select appropriate 
strategies independently as they read. From grades 3 to 6, students develop and apply research skills to use sources, locate information, and 
answer self-generated questions. 
R.2 Fluency
Students develop the ability to read continuous text with accuracy, 
good momentum, and phrasing, and appropriate pausing, intonation, 
and stress. Fluency supports comprehension and is an essential 
element of both reading out loud and reading silently. In the early 
stages of reading development, students listen to oral texts that 
model appropriate pace, expression, and intonation. As students 
acquire decoding and other word-solving strategies, their reading 
process will become more automatic and focused on making 
meaning. 
Throughout the IL1, reference is made to grade-level texts. Please see 
the IL1 Educator Support Handbook for more information about 
grade-level texts and Inuktut reading levels. 

3.16 Introduction
R.3 Text forms and purposes
Students learn how the elements of text forms (or types of texts) 
determine the ways readers interact with them. In this Learning 
Concept, students will come to an understanding that text forms 
are connected to the different purposes of reading, such as to be 
entertained, to locate specific information, or to expand vocabulary. 
Learning about the different purposes of reading and the text forms 
that serve those purposes is an important part of becoming a skilled 
reader. Students gradually become capable and independent readers 
as they make informed decisions about how to select and use texts for 
various purposes. 
Writing
Writing is a way to communicate and learn. To write effectively, students 
must build on the knowledge and skills that they have acquired through 
oral communication and reading. Writing should be connected to 
listening and speaking opportunities that reinforce and extend students’

The Learning Concepts in the Writing Learning Strand are:
W.1 Purpose, form, and audience
Students learn that writing is done for a purpose. Some common purposes for writing include to narrate, entertain, describe, inform, inquire,

in a variety of text forms. Examples of text forms include stories, letters, 
reports, and poetry.

audiences. As students develop their writing skills over time, they will 
develop their own voice in writing. 
W.2 Writing process
Students develop the ability to create and produce a finished text. 
They learn to plan for writing and organize their thoughts and ideas.

and ideas, and they learn to revise, edit, and proofread their writing 
before sharing it with others. Students also learn about consulting 
reference tools and knowledge holders (including parents and family, 
Elders, skilled artisans, storytellers, and other school staff) as an 
important part of the writing process. 
W.3 Conventions 
Students develop an understanding of how to use the elements and 
mechanics of writing (for example, spelling, grammar, and punctuation) 
to make their writing clear and comprehensible to others.

Grammar and Vocabulary
The IL1 Learning Outcomes include grade-appropriate vocabulary and 
grammatical elements necessary for students to listen, speak, read, and 
write effectively. The IL1 Educator Support Handbook provides grammar 
and vocabulary lists developed by Inuktut language experts, linguists, and 
educators to support the use of the IL1. These lists reflect the vocabulary and 
syntactic elements that students will encounter when using books in the 
Inuktut guided reading program and other Inuktut Titiqqiriniq resources. 
These lists are intimately connected to the learning of developmentally 
appropriate language structures.
Vocabulary lists and syntactic elements
The Inuktut vocabulary lists include roots (bare verbs and nouns) that are 
completed by pronominal endings that function as subjects/objects of the 
predicate (for example, I am...; am I..?; and let me...; and other pronouns) 
along with other argument clauses of the predicate as necessary. These 
surrounding grammatical elements complete the meaning of the predicate. 
Tenses and other modifiers between the root and subject pronoun may 
be inserted and varied depending on the focus of a lesson. Incorporating 
vocabulary with syntactic elements will ensure that students are connecting 
language use to real-life purposes.
Inuktut Titiqqiriniq is a comprehensive Inuktut literacy program that 
was created in Nunavut. Inuktut Titiqqiriniq was developed by Nunavut 
educators, linguists, and language consultants, with constant testing 
and input by Nunavut classroom teachers. Inuktut Titiqqiriniq provides 
instructional tools and resources to help students develop strong 
Inuktut Language Arts skills. For more information about the Inuktut 
Titiqqiriniq program, speak to your school’s literacy coach.

are based on what terms the working group of Inuktut language experts from 
across Nunavut considered to be the most accessible for students learning to 
read and write. 
Social Emotional Literacy Terminology vocabulary lists
The Social Emotional Literacy Terminology vocabulary lists provide 
words and phrases for each grade connected to the development of social 
emotional expression. When selecting the words and phrases for each grade, 
consideration was given to which words and phrases would be most useful

The Traditional Terminology vocabulary lists provide traditional Inuktut 
words and phrases for each grade. When selecting the words and phrases for

The lists provided in the IL1 are just a starting point, and teachers 
should not feel limited to using only the terms provided in the lists.

The following definitions and examples are intended to help teachers use

adjective
A word element added to describe a noun root (for example, -ᕐᔪᐊᖅ-/

adverb
A word element added to describe a verb root (for example, -ᓕᖅ-/-liq-). 
audience
The intended readers, listeners, or viewers of a particular text. 
basic punctuation
The punctuation used to end sentences. Periods, question marks, and 
exclamation marks are examples of basic punctuation. 
character
An imaginary person that inhabits a literary work. Literary characters may 
be major or minor, static (unchanging) or dynamic (capable of change). 
clause
A group of words that consists of a subject and a verb and provides 
information about what the subject is doing. 
complete sentence
A sentence that expresses a complete idea that makes sense on its own. Also 
called a well-formed sentence. 
complex sentence
A sentence consisting of a main clause and one or more subordinate 
clauses.

5.2 Glossary
complex punctuation 
The punctuation used in compound and complex sentences. Commas, 
colons, and semicolons are examples of complex punctuation. 
comprehension strategies
The strategies that students use before, during, and after listening, reading, 
and viewing to make meaning from texts. Examples include:
• Making connections from prior knowledge and experience to 
familiar texts
• Visualizing to clarify or deepen understanding of a text
• Predicting
• Finding important ideas
• Asking questions
• Summarizing information 
• Inferring

• Skimming text for information or details
• Scanning to determine the purpose or type of the text 
• Adjusting reading speed according to the level of difficulty of the 
text or the kind of reading (for example, a simple story or more 
complicated factual text)
concepts of print
The awareness of how print works of the purposes for reading. Print 
concepts include directionality (Inuktut is read from left to right, top to 
bottom), the difference between syllabics/letters and words (syllabics/
letters and finals represent sounds; words are made of roots and 
morphemes [chunks and affixes]; there are spaces between words), the use 
of punctuation, and common characteristics of books (for example, title, 
author, and front and back covers). 
conjunctions
Word chunks (morphemes) used to connect clauses or sentences. 
For example, -ᒐᒪ-/-ᑲᒪ-/-ᓇᒪ-/-ᕋᒪ-/-gama-/-kama-/-nama-/-rama-. 
decoding
Using knowledge of the relationships between sounds and syllabics/letters 
to read words.
