NALUNAIQSIJJUT
PREFACE
The purpose of schooling in Nunavut is to develop able human beings.
1
 The 
goal of science education is to create informed citizens capable of problemsolving and critical thinking who understand the interplay between 
science, society, climate, and the land in our ever-changing world.
Science is the body of knowledge and understanding about the natural 
and physical world, as well as the systematic process that humans use to 
create this knowledge. It is central to Inuit history. Traditional knowledge 
is scientific knowledge, as evidenced by innovations such as the qajaq, the 
qamutiiq, the qulliq, and the iglu. Problem-solving and perseverance were 
valued elements of the scientific approach that sustained life and survival 
on the land.
The cultural and contemporary significance of science, along with children’s 
innate sense of wonder about the natural and physical world around them, 
have merited this subject’s inclusion in Nunavut’s core curriculum. The 
pedagogy and the content of the Kindergarten–Grade 6 Science Curriculum
are informed by the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ) Principles, including 
Pilimmaksarniq (hands-on learning), Qanuqtuurunnarniq (problemsolving), Piliriqatigiingniq (group work), Aajiiqatigiingniq (consensus decisionmaking), and Avatittinnik Kamatsiarniq (environmental stewardship). This 
ensures that students not only learn science but also understand its cultural 
relevance and its role in environmental stewardship.
With support from Elders and the community, the Science Curriculum
provides an opportunity to explore topics of local and cultural interest. 
Incorporating traditional Inuit knowledge into the classroom makes science 
more relatable to Nunavut students and their communities. In fact, the 
classroom itself should be expanded to include the land through placebased investigations.

Science Curriculum 2.2
In the curriculum, Learning Outcomes are gradually sequenced to guide 
students through the Learning Strands of The Material World, Physics, 
Life Sciences, and Earth and Space. The topics increase in complexity 
from one grade level to the next to reinforce the major understandings of 
interdependence, energy, atomic structure, stewardship, and exploration. 
The structure of the curriculum directs and supports educators in 
translating Learning Outcomes into classroom experiences. Through group 
work and hands-on inquiry, students build a solid foundation of scientific 
understanding and apply their developing skills in observation, reasoning, 
and clear communication to real-life situations and other subject areas.
The importance of science today has not diminished as our world, 
including Nunavut, becomes more dependent on technology and concerned 
about sustainability. An aim of science education in Nunavut is for 
students to gain a deeper appreciation of their cultural heritage and the 
interconnectedness of all living things. As they learn the importance of 
stewardship, collaboration, and the value of traditional knowledge, the 
Kindergarten–Grade 6 Science Curriculum provides guidance for teachers to 
help students achieve this goal.

As stated in the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for Nunavut 
Curriculum, “schooling in Nunavut should provide support to students in all 
areas of their development so that they can achieve personal goals, become 
well-equipped to contribute and serve their families and communities, 
demonstrate leadership and healthy attitudes, and be able to actively 
participate and contribute as Nunavut takes on new roles in the global 
community.”¹ To achieve this vision for education in Nunavut, it is imperative 
that the curriculum in all subject areas promotes holistic critical thinking 
and encourages continuous learning that can be transferred across contexts. 
It must also promote inclusion and provide opportunities to differentiate 
learning experiences to meet the needs of all students. The updated Nunavut 
Curriculum Framework will provide a common structure for the curriculum 
in each subject area and support this holistic, cross-curricular approach to 
teaching and learning.

The aims and principles of the Curriculum Foundations inform the Major 
Understandings in each subject area. Major Understandings are broad statements 
that reflect the essential principles and core ideas within a subject area that can 
be transferred across different areas of learning.³ These Major Understandings 
anchor the curriculum in the purpose of learning for each subject area and 
the values of Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ). They act as a lens through which 
teachers can make decisions about planning, instruction, and assessment. 
The Major Understandings are explored through the Learning Strands, which 
represent the key concepts and processes within each subject area and the 
relationships among them. The Learning Strands remain consistent across grades 
and will encourage a richer exploration of how these concepts and processes 
work together over time. Within each Learning Strand, there are Learning 
Concepts and grade-specific Learning Outcomes. The Learning Concepts outline 
the constructs, processes, and patterns within each Learning Strand. These 
concepts provide a structure through which students can organize and connect 
the Learning Outcomes. Learning Outcomes define the key knowledge, skills, 
and understandings students will develop at each grade level. These become 
the building blocks that support deeper conceptual understanding.
In addition, each grade level has Guiding Questions. The Guiding Questions 
will help link the Learning Concepts and the Learning Outcomes back to 
the Curriculum Foundations. By building connections among the knowledge, 
skills, and understandings they have acquired, students will be able to explore 
the principles of the Curriculum Foundations in age-appropriate ways.
Teachers can use the Guiding Questions:
• In daily classroom activities and discussions
• As part of assessment tasks
• To plan units or themes
• To inspire inquiry-based projects

INTRODUCTION TO THE SCIENCE CURRICULUM
Science is the observation, study, and exploration of the natural and physical 
world. It is “a rational, empirically based description-explanation of nature,”4
 
and is also a “social, cultural, as well as an individual process of thought that 
has been utilized in some form by every human cultural group.”5
Rationale
When students understand the scientific structures of the physical and natural 
world, it helps connect them to a sense of place and encourages them to 
consider their impact on the world around them. Through learning science 
skills and knowledge, students build their understanding of how to ask 
questions, solve problems, make informed decisions, and innovate for the future. 
—
Notes
4. G. S. Aikenhead, “Towards Decolonizing the Pan-Canadian Science 
Framework,” Canadian Journal of Science, Mathematics and Technology Education 6, no.

5. G. Cajete, “On Science, Culture, and Curriculum: Enhancing Native American 
Participation in Science-Related Fields,” Tribal College 31, no. 3 (2020): 40–44.

Science Curriculum 3.7
Goals
The Nunavut Science Curriculum encourages exploration, curiosity, 
problem-solving, and sharing of traditional knowledge as students build 
their understanding of the world and the universe. The Nunavut Science 
Curriculum is designed to cumulatively build on students’ prior knowledge 
through the grades. Students may come with varying knowledge and 
understanding of science, and they are encouraged to discover through 
inquiry and explore through questioning. The Science Curriculum is designed 
using two-eyed seeing. Two-eyed seeing is described as viewing the world 
through one eye using the strengths of Indigenous knowledge and ways of 
knowing and from the other eye with the strengths of Western knowledge 
and ways of knowing.6
 Using both eyes together will lead to the gift of 
multiple perspectives.
Science skills
A key factor in science learning is developing the skills needed to “think like 
a scientist.” The Nunavut Science Curriculum is designed for students to 
develop science literacy: the understanding of science concepts and processes. 
Science skills are what allow students to engage with the scientific world. The 
skills in the Nunavut Science Curriculum were created using national and 
international standards, as well as the Iqqaqqaukkaringniq principles of IQ, and 
are embedded into the curriculum. These skills are:
• Curiosity
• Planning and conducting
• Analyzing and interpreting
• Evaluating and engaging
• Applying
• Communicating

3.8 Introduction
Curiosity: Students show an interest in observing the natural world. 
They are curious about their sense of place and they ask questions, make 
predictions and hypotheses, and notice problems.
Planning and conducting: Students are resourceful in trying to answer 
questions and solve problems. They research, consider variables, and gather 
and record information. They create plans, such as steps for experiments, 
diagrams, charts, and research notes. Students experiment, manipulating 
materials, testing, documenting, and recording what they see, including 
measurements and observations. 
Analyzing and interpreting: Students use their sense of place to help 
interpret findings. They work together with their peers and their greater 
community to discuss and analyze their findings. They sort, classify, and 
display data they have collected to see patterns and present their results in 
a variety of ways (diagrams, graphs, tables, songs, and stories).
Evaluating and engaging: Students think critically about what they have 
seen and learned and make inferences. They learn from their mistakes, retry 
or retest theories, persevere through tough questions, continue to ask new 
questions, and plan for next steps. They work to come to consensus about 
their hypotheses with peers and draw conclusions from their findings.
Applying: Students engage in heavy thinking and apply their knowledge to 
solve problems and design solutions. They demonstrate leadership, creating, 
innovating, and applying their understandings, solutions, and knowledge for 
the betterment of their communities and the land.
Communicating: Students share their prior and newly acquired knowledge. 
They express their personal and shared understandings of place and work 
together to share their science knowledge with their communities.

3.12 Introduction
Curriculum Foundation
Science is part of the Iqqaqqaukkaringniq Curriculum Foundation. The 
principles of the Iqqaqqaukkaringniq Curriculum Foundation focus on heavy 
thinking, curiosity, experimentation, leadership, and learning from mistakes.
Major Understandings
The Science Curriculum informs the Major Understandings within the 
curriculum that continue from Kindergarten to Grade 6. These Major 
Understandings reflect the essential learning within the subject area and can 
act as a lens through which teachers can make decisions about planning, 
instruction, and assessment. The Major Understandings for the Science 
Curriculum are: 
1. We are dependent on relationships and survive by adapting and 
interacting with living and non-living elements of the environment.
2. Energy is an essential component of all processes on Earth. 
3. All materials on Earth are made of tiny moving particles.
4. Building connections to our environment helps us protect our natural 
resources and gain respect for the land. 
5. Exploring and asking questions about our natural world leads to a greater 
understanding of ourselves and our future.

Science Curriculum 3.13
Learning Strands
The Science Curriculum has been organized by grade, and then further 
broken down into four Learning Strands: The Material World, Physics, 
Earth and Space, and Life Sciences. Unlike in other curricula, the Learning 
Outcomes within each Learning Strand have not been further organized 
into Learning Concepts. Science is the study of our world and beyond: a 
world in which everything is interconnected. Interconnectedness is key to 
understanding science, and so to categorize outcomes by concepts does a 
disservice to the integrated reality of all science and traditional knowledge. 
Due to the holistic nature of science and its associated skills, the outcomes 
are not further divided, which encourages an integrated approach to 
exploring the outcomes and finding cross-strand connections. 
The Material World
This strand explores matter, including its properties, diversity, and 
composition. Students will explore the properties of matter through 
observation of and experimentation with familiar materials. They will 
understand the states of matter and the role energy plays in these 
transformations. An understanding of The Material World strand will 
enable students to appreciate the world’s great diversity of materials, their 
properties, and their uses. 
Physics
This strand explores motion and its relationship with force and energy. The 
flow of energy in our physical world is discovered through investigations 
into motion, force, magnetism, gravity, electricity, light, sound, heat, flight, 
and machines. Understanding the connections between energy, force, and 
movement enables students to consider ways energy is used in their own lives 
and to reflect on ways they can solve problems and innovate using physics.

3.14 Introduction
Earth and Space
This strand explores Planet Earth and its place in the universe. Students 
will investigate the natural cycles and systems of Earth, including 
seasons, weather, the water cycle, the rock cycle, the carbon cycle, and 
climate change, as well as the solar system in which Earth is found. An 
understanding of Earth and Space will enable students to appreciate the 
complexities of Earth, the human impact on the environment, and our 
responsibility to care for it.
Life Sciences
This strand explores the living world and our place in it. Students will 
investigate the needs and uses of plants and animals and how they adapt 
to their environment and change environments. An understanding of Life 
Sciences will enable students to appreciate the interconnectedness of the 
natural world and the complexities of the cycles and systems of plants, 
animals, and humans.
The Learning Outcomes in the Kindergarten–Grade 6 Science Curriculum
build each year in order of increasing complexity within each grade. 
Connections between the outcomes encourage teachers to teach across 
strands and grades.

NUNAVUT SCIENCE CURRICULUM CONSIDERATIONS
Science and Ways of Knowing
The Nunavut Science Curriculum aims to honour two-eyed seeing: the concept 
of viewing the world through one eye using Indigenous ways of knowing and 
through the other eye using Western ways of knowing and learning, seeing 
with both of these together.7
 Two-eyed seeing creates space for students 
to learn together, sharing knowledge from multiple understandings and 
recognizing the value of these perspectives. Being able to use both eyes, 
while also calling upon the strengths of one eye in certain circumstances, 
gives space for both ways of knowing.8
 In this way, traditional knowledge and 
Western science do not replace one another; they work together. Traditional 
knowledge is integrated and looks at connections between spiritual 
relationships, places, and history through storytelling and experiences. It sees 
humans as part of the environment, not separate from it, and understands 
the importance of human impact.9
 Western science focuses more on formal 
analytical evaluations of the natural world, often categorizing and separating 
the parts of the world. Both ways of knowing are encouraged through 
the Nunavut Science Curriculum. Students focus on sharing and working 
together, experiencing by doing, and seeing science as a holistic, integrated 
system. They also learn to ask questions, remain curious about the world, 
and analyze and make conclusions based on predictions and deductions.

Land-Based Education
“For Inuit, being on the land can be the ethical space between Inuit 
communities and the education system, with Inuit children at the centre 
of that space so that new equitable learning opportunities are created. 
New opportunities can manifest as new curriculum, new outcomes, new 
understandings about teaching and learning, new ways of working together.”10 
Land-based education has been described and implemented in a variety of 
ways. The Nunavut Science Curriculum approaches land-based education 
through the IQ Principles. It recognizes the deep spiritual, physical, and 
mental connections that Inuit have to the land and the passing of knowledge 
from Elders and other knowledge holders to students. It values teaching 
the history of the land, using local language, and interacting daily with 
the outdoor environment. The goal is not simply outdoor education; it is a 
holistic approach that shows how our understandings of place connect to our 
understandings of science and its systems.11 Being outside on the land is an 
important part of land-based education. The ability to do this will fluctuate 
depending on many factors, including the seasons, weather, community, 
and the availability and involvement of Elders and other knowledge holders. 
While the ability to be physically outside for lessons may fluctuate, the values 
of land-based education continue indoors.

TEACHING FROM A CROSS-CURRICULAR 
PERSPECTIVE
According to the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for 
Nunavut Curriculum, the four Curriculum Foundations—Nunavusiutit, 
Iqqaqqaukkaringniq, Uqausiliriniq, and Aulajaaqtut—were designed to 
promote a holistic approach to education that encourages students to 
transfer learning across subject areas and develop deeper, transformative 
ways of thinking and learning. Planning learning experiences around 
thematic units or integrated projects can provide students with opportunities 
to apply their learning in different contexts. 
Within the Science Curriculum, there are many outcomes and skills that 
connect to other curricula. The Physical Education, Health, and Wellness 
Curriculum explores how our bodies move, how we can notice signals from 
our bodies, and how we can monitor the health of our bodies. This connects 
and builds to the learning of body systems in Grade 5 and 6 Science. The 
Nunavut Social Studies Curriculum makes exceptional connections to the 
Nunavut Science Curriculum, including a strand entitled The Land. In each 
grade of this strand, the content directly connects to Science, including 
what the land provides for us, the human impact on the land, impacts of 
industry on the land, changes (and predicting future changes) we observe on 
the land, and the movement of air, water, and animals. The cartography and 
environmental observation skills included in the Social Studies outcomes 
will also play a strong role in science learning. Cross-curricular teaching 
of Science and Social Studies can strengthen land-based education and 
exploring science through students’ connection to where they live, including 
the land and how they care for it. 

Science Curriculum 3.23
Science and Mathematics also share many skills and concepts. Students can 
record and display scientific data and information in graphs, charts, and 
tables, make connections by noticing patterns in data, and make predictions. 
They can refer to statistics, sort and classify animals, and use measurement 
units such as mass, length, and time. They can also use knowledge of shapes, 
angles, and measurement in their explorations of machines. Teachers are 
encouraged to look for these cross-curricular connections and opportunities 
to deepen students’ learning.
Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts, and Mathematics (STEAM) learning 
encourages students to use integrative approaches by combining knowledge 
from different subject areas to problem-solve, innovate, and create. 
When students are actively encouraged to see the value in combining 
skills and understandings from many different disciplines, they see 
that while subjects are divided, they all support one another toward our 
understanding of the world. 
For more information on STEAM learning, refer to the Science Educator 
Handbook.

USE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATION 
TECHNOLOGY
The use of information and communication technology is an important 
part of science learning, and a growing part of global science education. 
Forms of technology such as the internet, GIS systems, social media, digital 
devices, robotics, and media (podcasts and videos) can be excellent tools 
for engaging with science. Tools available for science learning can help 
spark curiosity, give students access to worldwide research, in-depth views 
of scientific concepts, and use of technology that enhances their ability 
to interact with different environments. Students may use these tools to 
search for scientific research and information and connect with science 
communities. Teachers should closely monitor students’ use of technology, 
ensuring that it is being used for appropriate means. Students also need to 
be taught how to safely use technology, keeping themselves and others safe 
from harm. It is also vitally important for students to learn to differentiate 
between reliable and unreliable scientific sources. Teachers are encouraged 
to see the Science Educator Handbook for more information on discussing 
reliable sources.

Additional notes:
The rock cycle is the process of rocks forming and changing. It begins with molten 
rock or magma that cools into igneous rock. The igneous rock then gets broken 
down due to factors such as weather and erosion and becomes sediment. The 
sediment builds up into layers over time and hardens, continuing to be covered by 
more sediment over time. Heat and pressure transform the sedimentary rock to 
metamorphic rock. Metamorphic rock will eventually reach the mantle where it 
will be melted, and the cycle will begin again.
Igneous rock is rock formed from the cooling of magma or lava (molten rock). 
Basalt and granite are examples of igneous rock.
Metamorphic rock is rock formed from heat and high pressure. Soapstone, gneiss, 
marble, and slate are examples of metamorphic rock.
Sedimentary rock is rock that is formed through the hardening of layers of 
sediment, such as, clay, sand, and mud. They are formed mostly underwater. Coal, 
claystone, limestone, and shale are examples of sedimentary rock.

Additional notes:
A simple machine is a simple device used to reduce the amount of force or change 
the direction of a force.
Work refers to the energy or force needed to move an object.
A wedge is a device used to break things apart or keep them together. Knives, teeth, 
and staples are examples of wedges.
An inclined plane is a sloped surface (ramp) used to move objects up or down.
A pulley is a wheel and axle with a rope looped around it that is used to lift and 
lower objects.
A screw is an inclined plane that is wrapped around a post and used to lift objects 
or keep them together.
A wheel and axle is a circle with a rod through the centre.
The fulcrum is the support on a lever on which the beam pivots.
The load is the weight that needs to be moved.
Effort refers to the force needed to move a load.
A lever is a beam on a pivot that is used to lift or lower objects.
A first-class lever is a lever where the fulcrum is in between the effort and the 
load. Scissors and crowbars are examples of first-class levers.
A second-class lever is a lever where the load is between the effort and the 
fulcrum. Wheelbarrows are examples of second-class levers.
A third-class lever is a lever where the effort is between the load and the fulcrum. 
Tweezers are an example of a third-class lever. 
Educators can refer to the Science Educator Handbook for more information on 
simple machines, including diagrams.

Explain Newton’s three laws of motion and their application in

Notice if the student:

A balanced force is when force is applied in opposite directions, and the forces are

An unbalanced force is when force is applied in opposite directions, and one force is

The three laws of motion are three rules that explain the relationship between 
forces and motion:
• First law of motion (Law of Inertia): An object at rest will stay at rest unless 
 acted upon by a force, and an object in motion will stay in motion unless acted 
 upon by a force.
• Second law of motion (Law of Force and Acceleration): The mass of an object 
 determines how much force is needed to accelerate it (the greater the mass, the 
 more force required).
• Third law of motion (Law of Action and Reaction): Forces come in pairs. For 
 every action there is a reaction of equal strength from the opposite direction.
In Grade 6, these laws will be explored in hands-on, experimental, and visual 
contexts. Students in this grade are not expected to learn the formulas of motion.

sublimation
Sublimation is the process where a solid changes directly into a gas 
without becoming a liquid first.
suspension
A mixture containing a liquid and particles of a solid that do not dissolve. 
Particles can be mixed into the liquid but over time will always separate 
and settle at the bottom. Sand in water is an example of a suspension.
tectonic process
When the sections of plates that make up the Earth’s crust and mantle 
shift or move.
temperature
A measurement used to determine how hot or cold something is.
thermal energy (heat energy)
The movement of particles. As the temperature rises, particles move 
faster and bump into each other more frequently until they eventually 
break free. Particles slow and settle down as the temperature cools.
thrust
The force (push) that accelerates an object.
tone
The quality of a sound (for example, strong or weak).
transfer of energy
The movement of energy from one place to another.
transformation
A major change.
unbalanced force
When force is applied in opposite directions and one force is stronger 
than the other. 

5.22 Glossary
universe
Everything that exists on Earth, in the solar system, and beyond.
vertebrate
An animal with a backbone.
vibration
The action of moving back and forth quickly.
viruses
Small particles that can travel through air, water, or surfaces. Some 
viruses reproduce in the body and make us sick.
viscosity
The properties of a liquid that determine how well it will flow. Liquids 
with high viscosity flow slowly, and liquids with low viscosity flow quickly.
volume (liquid)
The amount of space a substance or object takes up. It is sometimes 
measured in millilitres and litres.
volume (sound)
The loudness of a sound.
water cycle
How water moves around Earth. It includes evaporation, condensation, 
precipitation, and collection..
weather
The state of the atmosphere (including air, temperature, precipitation, 
and pressure).
weathering
The breaking down of rocks and minerals through weather.
