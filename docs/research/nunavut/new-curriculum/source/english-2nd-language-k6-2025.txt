have helped to fulfill one of Nunavut’s fundamental goals as a territory: to 
provide our students with a unique Nunavut-made curriculum – one in 
which our learners can see themselves and their communities and which 
will influence them long into the future.

NALUNAIQSIJJUT
PREFACE
The kindergarten to Grade 6 English 2nd Language Curriculum guides 
educators in teaching English as a second language to young learners in 
Nunavut. It provides a structured framework that engages students in 
authentic and relevant second-language learning and progressively develops 
their English competencies in oral communication, reading, and writing.
To create a curriculum that ensures students are represented and that 
their learning is meaningful, the English 2nd Language Curriculum is 
guided by foundational documents such as the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit 
Education Framework for Nunavut Curriculum and Inuuqatigiit: The 
Curriculum from the Inuit Perspective. Inuit knowledge forms the basis of 
the curriculum, and Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Principles are prioritized and 
reflected within its Learning Outcomes.
Instilling the curriculum with Inuit values is a reclamation of Inuit identity 
that ensures our culture and language will thrive in modern society. Inuit 
teachings were traditionally imperative for our survival and harmonious 
living, and they are still important in modern day Nunavut. Privileging 
Inuit knowledge in education safeguards the unique perspectives that make 
us Inuit. 
Speaking a second languages offers numerous professional and personal 
advantages as well as increased cultural sensitivity. Insight into other 
cultures increases opportunities for success in our careers and allows us to 
navigate the world with confidence and to coexist with others.
2.1 Preface

English 2nd Language Curriculum 2.2
Learning a second language offers multiple benefits for students. It supports 
cognitive development, enhances conflict resolution skills, and improves 
academic performance through enhanced multitasking skills, memory, and 
creativity.1
 These skills benefit students in all academic areas. As they apply 
their first-language knowledge to new second-language contexts, students 
strengthen literacy in their first language, extend their comprehension 
abilities, and develop their reasoning and analytical skills.
For Nunavut students, English proficiency is both a skill and a gateway to 
the broader global community. It exposes students to diverse content and 
contexts and allows them to make new connections between language and 
culture. As they navigate between their first language and English, students 
develop a deeper appreciation for the linguistic and cultural diversity of 
Canadian and global communities while also strengthening their personal 
cultural identity.
While revitalizing the Inuktut language remains a central priority in 
Nunavut’s educational landscape, English proficiency prepares students to 
engage confidently in an interconnected world and expands their academic 
and professional opportunities. By learning English alongside their first 
language, students are empowered to share their rich Inuit culture and 
experiences with a broader audience and access a wealth of post-secondary 
education options and economic opportunities. 
The English 2nd Language Curriculum builds on the wisdom of Elders and 
Inuit educators, who emphasize the vital connection between language 
and cultural identity. Its success relies on the combined efforts of school 
teams, parents, and Elders to expand students’ opportunities in Englishspeaking communities and support them in achieving their personal and 
academic goals.

As stated in the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for Nunavut 
Curriculum, “schooling in Nunavut should provide support to students in all 
areas of their development so that they can achieve personal goals, become 
well-equipped to contribute and serve their families and communities, 
demonstrate leadership and healthy attitudes, and be able to actively 
participate and contribute as Nunavut takes on new roles in the global 
community.”¹ To achieve this vision for education in Nunavut, it is imperative 
that the curriculum in all subject areas promotes holistic critical thinking 
and encourages continuous learning that can be transferred across contexts. 
It must also promote inclusion and provide opportunities to differentiate 
learning experiences to meet the needs of all students. The updated Nunavut 
Curriculum Framework will provide a common structure for the curriculum 
in each subject area and support this holistic, cross-curricular approach to 
teaching and learning.

The aims and principles of the Curriculum Foundations inform the Major 
Understandings in each subject area. Major Understandings are broad 
statements that reflect the essential principles and core ideas within a 
subject area that can be transferred across different areas of learning.³ These 
Major Understandings anchor the curriculum in the purpose for learning 
in each subject area and the values of Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ). They 
act as a lens through which teachers can make decisions about planning, 
instruction, and assessment.
The Major Understandings are explored through the Learning Strands, 
which represent the key concepts and processes within each subject area 
and the relationships among them. The Learning Strands remain consistent 
across grades and will encourage a richer exploration of how these concepts 
and processes work together over time. Within each Learning Strand, there 
are Learning Concepts and grade-specific Learning Outcomes. The 
Learning Concepts outline the constructs, processes, and patterns within 
each Learning Strand. These concepts provide a structure through which 
students can organize and connect the Learning Outcomes. Learning 
Outcomes define the key knowledge, skills, and understandings students will 
develop at each grade level. These become the building blocks that support 
deeper conceptual understanding.
In addition, each grade level has Guiding Questions. The Guiding Questions 
will help link the Learning Concepts and Learning Outcomes back to the 
Curriculum Foundations. By building connections among the knowledge, 
skills, and understandings they have acquired, students will be able to explore 
the principles of the Curriculum Foundations in age-appropriate ways.
Teachers can use the Guiding Questions:
• In daily classroom activities and discussions
• As part of assessment tasks
• To plan units or themes
• To inspire inquiry-based projects

CURRICULUM (EL2)
What is the English 2nd Language Curriculum (EL2)?
The EL2 is designed to be used with students whose first language is a 
language other than English. The curriculum is cumulative and progresses 
consistently, allowing students’ English language skills to grow each year. 
Students entering Kindergarten will have varying knowledge of English 
depending on the level of exposure to English within their households and 
communities. 
Language of Instruction Model
The EL2 has been developed to support the Qulliq Language of Instruction 
model. The Qulliq model supports Inuktut as the main language of 
instruction in all subject areas while helping students acquire English in 
a second-language context as they progress from Kindergarten to Grade 
6. This approach helps to ground students in the knowledge, skills, and 
understandings of their first language while gradually acquiring the second 
language.4

English 2nd Language Curriculum 3.7
Rationale
Acquiring a second language provides cognitive, social, and academic benefits 
and enhances first-language and literacy skills. Students draw from the 
knowledge, skills, and understandings of their first language and apply them 
to second-language learning contexts. As students acquire a second language, 
they can think more critically and creatively in all aspects of their lives. 
Communicating in more than one language enhances students’ senses of 
belonging to the wider community and provides pathways for students to access 
post-secondary and economic opportunities in an English-speaking context. 
The Nunavut English 2nd Language Curriculum: Kindergarten to Grade 
6 provides a framework for engaging students in authentic and relevant 
language-learning experiences. These experiences help students become 
independent users of English, develop skills for further knowledge seeking, 
and build relationships in a wider language community that are based on 
mutual respect and personal empathy. 
Goals 
The goal of the EL2 is to provide Nunavut students with accessible, flexible, 
and supportive second-language learning experiences. Students will develop 
their ability to communicate effectively in a second language and build 
essential English literacy skills. As students progress through the grades, 
they will develop the knowledge, skills, and understandings necessary for 
independent English language use.
The EL2 aims for students to:
• Achieve levels of comprehension and competency to be able to 
communicate effectively and build positive relationships with 
others
• Use effective language strategies in a variety of contexts
• Make personal connections to language learning and develop a 
sense of belonging in a language-learning community
• Develop essential literacy skills in a second-language context

3.10 Introduction
Curriculum Foundation
The EL2 is part of the Uqausiliriniq Curriculum Foundation. The Uqausiliriniq 
Curriculum Foundation focuses on relationships in communication and 
language development, creative and artistic expression, and reflective and 
critical thinking. The principles of the Uqausiliriniq Curriculum Foundation 
include building relationships, respecting others, thinking creatively and 
seeking to understand, and improving on the present and building strength 
for the future.
Major Understandings
The principles of Uqausiliriniq inform the three Major Understandings 
within the EL2 that continue from Kindergarten to Grade 6. These Major 
Understandings reflect essential learning within the subject area and can 
act as a lens through which teachers can make decisions about planning, 
instruction, and assessment. The Major Understandings for the EL2 are: 
1. Learning language helps us build deeper relationships based on mutual 
respect and empathy. 
2. Making meaning from another language helps us think more critically 
and creatively in all aspects of our lives. 
3. The ability to communicate in more than one language enhances our 
sense of belonging to the wider community. 

English 2nd Language Curriculum 3.11
Learning Strands and Learning Concepts 
The EL2 has been organized by grade and then further broken down into 
three Learning Strands: Oral Communication, Reading, and Writing. The 
grade-specific Learning Outcomes within each Learning Strand have been 
organized according to their Learning Concept so that there is a clear 
progression of student learning from Kindergarten to Grade 6.
Oral Communication 
Oral Communication includes listening and speaking skills. Students 
develop oral communication skills through authentic and purposeful 
interactions and activities that are relevant to their daily lives, including 
play. Language strategies are modelled and taught explicitly to help 
students understand and communicate effectively in a variety of contexts 
in and outside of the classroom. As students practise and develop oral 
communication skills, they extend and enhance their knowledge and 
skills from their first language to build relationships with others based on 
mutual respect and understanding.
The EL2 emphasizes English as the language of instruction. However, 
students may feel the need to use their first language for reassurance, to 
confirm or clarify meaning, or to support their peers in a second-language 
environment. Students can also be observed interacting with Elders or 
other adults who speak Inuktut. Communicating openly to everyone 
demonstrates respect and the IQ principle of Tunnganarniq (fostering 
good spirits by being open, welcoming, and inclusive), which promote 
socialization in an inclusive way.

3.12 Introduction
The Learning Concepts in the Oral Communication Learning Strand of the 
EL2 are:
OC.1 Listening to Understand
Students will develop the ability to engage in purposeful listening in 
a second language. The Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept 
focus on students demonstrating active listening and observational 
skills, or purposeful listening, by using a variety of verbal and nonverbal cues and strategies to engage physically and mentally with a 
speaker or oral text. 
OC.2 Speaking to Communicate
Students will use expressive language processes to be understood by 
others. The Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept focus on 
the knowledge and skills students need to organize their thoughts 
and make appropriate language choices to communicate effectively 
for specific purposes and audiences. Students will incorporate new 
vocabulary and grammatical elements into their growing linguistic 
toolkit as they use the language of instruction in a variety of contexts. 
OC.3 Interacting with Others 
Students will engage in the process of building relationships in the 
classroom community using the language of instruction for socialization 
and self-expression. The Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept 
focus on promoting Inuuqatigiitsiarniq, which encompasses the 
understanding, cooperation, and mutual caring that builds relationships 
between people. Students first learn how to respond to greetings and 
engage in simple exchanges. As students progress through the grades, 
they begin to participate in conversations and collaborate with others in 
the language of instruction to complete tasks and solve problems. 
Reading
Reading involves making meaning from text. Most of the reading skills 
and strategies students have learned in their first language can be applied 
to reading in English. Students will develop a strong understanding of the 
English alphabet, including letter–sound relationships, as they learn to 
read from a second writing system. Within this strand, students will gain 
the ability over time to read English texts independently, using reading 
strategies to monitor and deepen their understanding of the text. 

English 2nd Language Curriculum 3.13
They will demonstrate the IQ principle of Pilimmaksarniq (development of 
skills through observation, mentoring, practise, and effort) as they develop their 
reading skills through continued practise. They will also explore a variety of 
texts with themes related to Nunavut and IQ that allow them to engage with 
different perspectives and gain new understandings of the world around them. 
The Learning Concepts in the Reading Learning Strand of the EL2 are:
R.1 Comprehension 
Students will develop the processes they need to interact with and 
make meaning of English texts read out loud and independently. The 
Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept focus on helping students develop comprehension strategies and respond to texts through 
creative and critical means. As students listen to and read a variety of 
texts, they begin to build their vocabulary by using modelled strategies 
to determine the meaning of unfamiliar words they encounter during 
reading. 
R.2 Fluency
Students will develop the ability to read smoothly, accurately, and 
with appropriate expression. Fluency supports comprehension and 
is an essential element of reading, both out loud and silently. In the 
early stages of reading development, students listen to oral texts that 
model appropriate pace, expression, and intonation. As students acquire 
decoding and other word-solving strategies in English, their reading 
process can become more automatic and focused on meaning-making.
R.3 Purpose and Form
Students learn how the elements of text forms (or types of texts) 
determine the ways readers interact with them. In this Learning 
Concept, students will come to an understanding that text forms 
are connected to the different purposes for reading, such as to be 
entertained, to locate specific information, or to expand vocabulary. 
Learning about the different purposes for reading and the text forms 
that serve those purposes is an important part of becoming a skilled 
reader in any language. Students will gradually become capable and 
independent readers in English as they make informed decisions about 
how to select and use texts for various purposes. 

3.14 Introduction
Writing
To write effectively, students must build on the knowledge and skills they 
have acquired through oral communication and reading. Writing should 
be connected to listening and speaking opportunities that reinforce 
and extend students’ knowledge of themes, topics, language structures, 
and vocabulary. Students will begin writing in English by learning 
basic letter formation of the alphabet (for example, printing upper- and 
lowercase letters). As students develop their writing skills in English, 
they will become more independent in the writing process and write for 
various purposes and audiences. They will connect to the IQ principle of 
Qanuqtuurunnarniq (being innovative and resourceful) by demonstrating 
resourcefulness and creative thinking as they engage in the writing 
process to write their own texts. Students will also develop an awareness 
of writing conventions and how they are necessary to deliver effective 
writing in English.
The Learning Concepts in the Writing Learning Strand of the EL2 are:
W.1 Purpose, Form, and Audience 
The Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept focus on helping students develop the skills to write meaningful texts for a variety of purposes and audiences. Students will use critical and creative thinking to 
make informed decisions about how to create and organize their texts 
in English, with teacher support. As students develop their writing skills 
over time, they will begin to explore creative use of language to develop 
their own voice in writing. 
W.2 Writing Process
Students develop the ability to create and produce a finished text in 
English. Students will develop a variety of strategies to support their 
writing throughout the writing process and use elements of effective 
presentation to publish and share their finished products with others. 
The Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept start at Grade 
3; however, students from Kindergarten to Grade 2 should be given 
frequent opportunities to participate in writing activities that model 
the writing process.

English 2nd Language Curriculum 3.15
W.3 Writing Conventions 
Students will develop an understanding of how to use the elements 
and mechanics of writing (such as letter formation, capitalization, 
punctuation, spelling, and grammar). Students will be able to use 
increasingly complex writing conventions in their written work to make 
clear and comprehensible texts in English.

INUIT QAUJIMAJATUQANGIT (IQ) IN THE EL2 
The nature of language acquisition draws on all IQ Principles in a holistic way. 
Learning a second language reinforces IQ through transformative ways of thinking 
and building relationships within language communities. Based on the core 
principles of Uqausiliriniq, the EL2 connects deeply to the following IQ Principles: 
• Inuuqatigiitsiarniq is “showing respect and a caring attitude for 
others.” Students will build relationships within language communities 
by using language in purposeful ways to establish respectful social 
interactions in the language of instruction. 
• Tunnganarniq is “being welcoming to others, open in communications, 
and inclusive in the ways people interact.” Students will demonstrate 
respect through inclusive language use, including non-verbal 
communication and gestures. While the EL2 uses English as the 
language of instruction, teachers can observe students using their first 
language to support their peers and interact with adults, Elders, and 
other community members. 
• Piliriqatigiingniq is “to develop a collaborative relationship and work 
together for the common good.” Students will extend their ability to 
collaborate with others to a second-language context. Through purposeful 
activities and tasks, students will gain a deeper understanding of different 
roles within a group, how to support each other in language learning, 
and how their contributions make a group and community stronger. 
• Qanuqtuurunnarniq is “to be resourceful and seek solutions through 
creativity, adaptability, and flexibility.” Students will engage in 
problem-solving and creative thinking as they work to understand 
and communicate with others in a second language. Students will use 
strategies they learn to apply their language skills in purposeful ways 
in a variety of social and academic contexts.

ENGLISH 2ND LANGUAGE CONSIDERATIONS
Planning for student success in a second-language context requires teachers 
to carefully plan interactive language-learning opportunities that promote 
purposeful and meaningful uses of language. Teachers can help students 
make connections to how English is used in their daily lives and draw from 
their prior knowledge and experience to expand their learning. 
This section provides essential information to inform planning and 
instruction in the EL2 classroom. Please refer to the EL2 Educator Handbook
for further support and resources. 
Creating an inclusive learning environment
Inuglugijaittuq: Foundation for Inclusive Education in Nunavut Schools (2008) 
notes that the way to start building an inclusive classroom is to nurture each 
student’s sense of belonging in the daily life of the classroom. Teachers in 
the EL2 classroom can foster this sense of belonging by presenting frequent 
opportunities for students to:
• Develop language skills to communicate their needs
• Use self-expression to share with others about themselves, their 
interests, and their personal experiences
• Be acknowledged for who they are as individuals and what they need

English 2nd Language Curriculum 3.19
When students have developed a sense of individual inclusion over time, 
the teacher can put more emphasis on group learning. Students will learn 
to use the language of instruction to solve problems, make group decisions, 
recognize and respect differences, and contribute to common goals. Providing 
opportunities for various groupings where students begin to share leadership 
roles to complete tasks builds a classroom community of collaborative 
learning over time. When an inclusive learning environment exists, students 
can explore new ways of thinking as they learn from others and take risks 
in the language-learning process. Establishing and maintaining an inclusive 
classroom is a key part of motivating and engaging students in secondlanguage learning.
Refer to the EL2 Educator Handbook for further information on creating an 
inclusive learning environment in the EL2 classroom. 
Using authentic texts 
Authentic texts are texts that are not specifically created for language 
learning purposes. These texts reinforce the direct relationship between 
learning the language in the classroom and using the language in the 
outside world. In the EL2 classroom, students should have opportunities to 
interact with a range of authentic texts they are likely to encounter in their 
daily lives. Authentic texts include:
• Visual texts (for example, pictures, photographs, and wordless books)
• Printed texts (for example, local newspapers, menus, flyers, maps, 
books, signs, and labels)
• Digital texts (for example, websites, TV shows, movies, radio, and 
social media)
• Physical texts (for example, land, structures, and objects)
These texts provide students with more exposure to language that is used 
outside the classroom and help them respond to and use texts for real-life 
purposes. Engaging with authentic texts and learning how to use them is an 
introduction to critical thinking. 

3.20 Introduction
Writing systems
In the EL2 classroom, students will learn to read and write with the English 
alphabet. Although English (sometimes bilingual) media may be found 
in the home or community, students need support and guidance as they 
develop their ability to read and write in a second-language writing system.
Teachers should consider providing additional instruction and support for 
the following:
• Alphabet knowledge: Recognizing and naming all the letters of the 
alphabet quickly and accurately is an essential building block for early 
reading and writing development. Games, songs, chants, and other playbased activities help young students effectively learn second-language 
print and begin to recognize and name the letters of the alphabet. 
• Alphabetic principle: The alphabetic principle is the understanding 
that there are predictable relationships between letters and sounds. While 
there are 26 letters in the English alphabet, some sounds can be represented 
by more than one letter, and some letters represent more than one 
sound. Students may need more explicit instruction and frequent 
opportunities to practise identifying letter–sound relationships through 
explicit phonics instruction and whole-class reading and writing activities. 
• Uppercase and lowercase letters: The concept of capital letters and 
identifying two different letter forms in English may be challenging 
for some students. Teachers should provide students with many 
opportunities to explore, identify, compare, write, and represent 
uppercase and lowercase letter forms, with support. 
• Punctuation: Students may need additional instructional support and 
guidance for the uses of apostrophes. In the English writing system, 
apostrophes are used to indicate possessives (for example, “Keith’s bag”) 
and contractions (for example, “do not” is also expressed as “don’t”). 
Teachers should provide students with many opportunities to explore, 
identify, compare, write, and represent the proper use of punctuation 
marks in context at the appropriate grade level.

Instructional approaches to second-language learning
Instructional approaches and teaching methods for second-language learning 
have shifted over the years to reflect ongoing research and the changing 
needs of society.6
 Language teaching has transitioned from a focus on rote 
memorization and grammar competency to a communicative approach that 
prioritizes using language in purposeful ways for real-life situations.7
Flexible instructional approaches support the diverse needs of each student at 
all levels of language development. Second-language instructional approaches 
must develop a foundation for social relationships so that students can 
engage with the second language in meaningful ways. Research suggests 
that communicative and action-oriented approaches help students develop 
effective and functional language skills and second-language competency.8
Listed below are suggested instructional approaches that are flexible and 
adaptive. These approaches encourage EL2 teachers to base language on 
tasks and activities that are reflective of students’ lived experiences. 
Communicative approach 
In the communicative approach, meaningful and authentic communication 
is central to all language-learning activities. Language conventions, such 
as knowledge of word order (syntax) and sentence structure, are modelled 
by the teacher and embedded into purposeful activities and tasks. 
Meaningful communication goes beyond learning isolated vocabulary 
and sentence structures by applying language to real-life contexts and 
interactions. Classroom activities are organized around communication 
tasks such as gathering information, expressing personal needs and 
interests, and interacting with others while using the language of 
instruction. Contextual cues, props, visuals, and gestures are used to 
support communication and make meaning.

The communicative approach recognizes that making linguistic errors is

themselves creatively and spontaneously. Attempting to communicate a 
message that is understood by others, even if not well-formed, is the main

Action-oriented approach 
The action-oriented approach focuses on learning language in real-world 
settings and is related to accomplishing real-life tasks. Action-oriented tasks 
are reflective of situations and contexts that are part of students’ everyday 
lives, including their world of play and imagination. To accomplish language 
tasks, students require ample opportunity to apply what they have learned in 
developmentally appropriate ways and within familiar situations and social 
contexts (for example, responding to questions and/or asking questions, etc.). 
In the action-oriented approach, assigned tasks follow lessons, discussion, 
and verbal instructions. Authentic texts, various instructional groupings, 
and guided reading and writing all play an important role in accomplishing 
tasks in second-language learning. The action-oriented approach is central 
to the IQ pedagogical principle of Inunnguiniq, which empowers students to 
shape the nature of their language interactions.

Balanced literacy approach
Balanced literacy is a standard approach to literacy instruction across 
all languages in Nunavut.10 In the EL2 classroom, an emphasis must 
be placed on the development of oral communication skills, which are 
the foundation for all language learning. Teachers should integrate 
developmentally appropriate reading and writing activities that build 
on what students can communicate orally. Texts that reflect students’ 
interests and use repetitive language and supportive visuals help students 
to make meaning and personal connections in a second language. 
Reading should be framed by authentic oral interactions so that 
students can consolidate their learning and reuse familiar vocabulary 
and sentence structures in purposeful and meaningful ways. Writing 
should be an extension of listening, speaking, and reading. Writing 
provides opportunities for students to consolidate their learning and to 
communicate their messages in a variety of ways while building on the 
skills they have learned from their first language. 
The balanced literacy approach empowers students to gradually become 
more capable and independent in understanding and using the language 
in meaningful ways. Teachers should model new language structures and 
expressions and create a supportive environment in which students can 
use and apply the language in structured tasks and activities. 
Refer to the EL2 Educator Handbook for further information on how to 
use a balanced literacy approach in the EL2 classroom.

Differentiated instruction in EL2 
Students in the EL2 classroom have varying interests, learning styles, 
cognitive abilities, and levels of exposure to English. According to the 
Nunavut Literacy Framework K–12, differentiated instruction is “provided to 
accommodate different learners, taking into consideration their strengths, 
challenges, interests, skills, learning preferences, and abilities.”11 It is 
important to consider the social, cultural, and linguistic knowledge that 
students bring to the EL2 classroom and how that shapes their interactions 
within the classroom. 
To ensure all students are engaged and supported in the language learning 
process, EL2 teachers should provide:
• Flexible groupings to maximize purposeful oral communication
• Activities and tasks that are relevant and appropriate for students 
in Nunavut
• Scaffolding through modelling, visual supports, non-verbal cues, and 
gestures 
• Ample opportunities for spontaneous interaction and selfexpression, where students can apply language skills in new 
contexts
• An inclusive environment that promotes open communication in 
Inuktut and English
• Appropriate error correction in a way that promotes self-correction 
and does not stop the flow of communication 
• Rich tasks that include choice and opportunities for critical and 
creative thinking

CULTURALLY RESPONSIVE TEACHING
Culturally responsive teaching is a student-centred approach to teaching 
and learning that recognizes and nurtures the background knowledge and 
experiences of each learner. Rather than being the keepers of knowledge, 
teachers are facilitators of learning and guide students in the learning 
process. Foundation for Languages of Instruction in Nunavut Schools12 notes 
that successful teachers are lifelong learners and take a genuine interest 
in learning about the community and lives of their students outside of the 
classroom.
Teachers in the EL2 classroom will hold positive views of their students 
and their ability to achieve success in a second-language environment. It is 
important that teachers maintain high expectations for the contribution 
that each learner brings to the group. Lessons should be based on relevant, 
authentic, and locally based topics and themes so that students can see 
themselves in the learning and apply their background knowledge as they 
build their second-language skills. Another important aspect is recognizing 
that students’ families know their children best and that building strong 
relationships with students’ families will promote mutual respect and 
collaboration in the teaching and learning process.

GLOSSARY 
The following definitions and examples are intended to help teachers use 
this document. The examples provided are suggestions and are not meant to 
be exhaustive. 
active listening
A communication strategy listeners use to demonstrate that they are 
engaged physically and mentally with a speaker or oral text. 
antonym 
A word that has the opposite meaning of another word. For example, 
cold and hot are antonyms. 
audience
The intended readers, listeners, or viewers of a particular text.
authentic texts
Texts that are not specifically created for language learning purposes. They 
can include radio programs, flyers, newspapers, magazines, TV shows, songs, 
movies, posters, etc.
basic punctuation
The punctuation used to end sentences (periods, exclamation marks, and 
question marks).
comparative adjectives
Adjectives used to compare two nouns (people, places, or things). Regular 
comparatives add an -er to the base word (for example, taller, smaller, 
and faster). 
complete sentence
A sentence that expresses a complete idea, makes sense on its own and 
includes a subject and a verb. It is also called a well-formed sentence.

5.2 Glossary
comprehension strategies
A variety of techniques that students use before, during, and after 
listening to, reading, and viewing to construct meaning from texts. 
compound sentence
A sentence with two or more independent clauses joined by a 
conjunction. For example, “She went for a walk, and she saw many birds.”
concepts of print
The awareness of how print works for the purposes of reading. Print 
concepts include directionality (English text is read from left to right, top 
to bottom); the difference between letters, words, and sentences; the use of 
punctuation; and common characteristics of books (for example, a title, an 
author, and front and back covers). 
concept words
Words that signify locations, numbers, settings, times, and feelings. 
Concept words can include vocabulary such as up, down, one, two, three,
morning, and night.
consonant blend
A combination of consonant letters in which each sound can be heard 
(for example, tr, pl, and bl). These are also referred to as consonant 
clusters.
conventional spelling 
The Canadian standard spelling of words.
CVC, CCVC, CVVC, and CVCC
Words that follow common spelling patterns. For example: 
• CVC: mat, top, and sit
• CCVC: chat, shop, and trip
• CVVC: wood and look
• CVCC: will, gill, and fill 
decoding
Using knowledge of letter–sound relationships to read words.
digraph
A combination of two letters that represent a single sound (for example, 
sh, ch, ay, ow, ee).

English 2nd Language Curriculum 5.3
draft
A first attempt at writing thoughts and ideas down in an organized way. 
Some tasks involve creating more than one draft, before creating a final 
draft.
echo reading 
An activity where the teacher reads a sentence slowly and the students 
repeat, or echo, it back.
environmental print
Print that can be seen every day in our surroundings, such as street signs, 
logos, labels, and food packaging.
familiar contexts
Environments or situations that are understood by the student through 
repeated experience.
familiar phrases
Language that students have regularly encountered in their everyday 
lives, both inside and outside of the classroom (for example, phrases 
related to daily routines). 
familiar vocabulary 
Language that students have regularly encountered in their everyday lives, 
both inside and outside of the classroom (for example, language related to 
daily routines).
figurative language 
A way of using words to express something beyond their literal meaning, 
often to create vivid imagery or convey complex ideas in an imaginative 
way.
fluency
The ability to read continuous text out loud or silently with accuracy, 
good momentum and phrasing, and appropriate pausing, intonation, 
and stress.

5.4 Glossary
graphic organizer
A visual framework (such as a T-chart, story map, or Venn diagram) that 
helps students organize, synthesize, and assess information and ideas.
high-frequency words
The words that appear most frequently in printed text. Students are 
encouraged to read these words automatically, without having to sound 
them out.
homograph 
Words that are spelled the same but have different meanings. For 
example, ring (the sound) and ring (a piece of jewellery) are homographs. 
homophone
Words that sound the same but have different meanings and spellings. 
For example, ate and eight are homophones. 
inclusive language
Knowing when to switch between first and second language, depending 
on the situation or with whom the student is speaking.
inventive spelling
Writing unfamiliar words based on phonetic knowledge.
legible writing
Writing that is clear and easy to read by the teacher or other adults. 
mentor texts 
Texts that are useful for demonstrating effective writing strategies, such 
as including introductions, setting, and descriptive language. Students 
can use mentor texts as tools to help influence and guide their own 
writing.
modelled questions 
Simple, patterned questions that students have regularly encountered in 
the classroom (for example, “What’s this?”).
modelled writing 
An instructional tool that a teacher uses to demonstrate a writing concept 
or skill. The teacher clearly communicates the purpose and type of writing 
to the students. 

English 2nd Language Curriculum 5.5
non-verbal communication and gestures
Actions that communicate meaning without using verbal language, such 
as shaking or nodding one’s head, raising one’s eyebrows, and scrunching 
one’s nose.
onset
The initial consonant(s) sound of a syllable (for example, c- is the onset 
in cat; l- is the onset in land).
oral texts
Texts that are delivered through oral language, including oral stories, audio 
recordings, short dialogues, role plays, chants, and songs.
paragraph 
A group of sentences that contain related information. When a text has 
more than one paragraph, the paragraphs are visually distinct from one 
another. 
personification 
a figure of speech where human qualities or characteristics are given to 
animals, objects, or ideas. It is used to create more relatable and vivid 
imagery. or example, "the wind whispered through the trees" or "he clock 
stared at me, ticking away the minutes."
planning 
Often one of the steps in the writing process, planning involves 
organizing one’s thoughts about what one is going to write. This may 
include brainstorming ideas, deciding what to write first, second, etc., and 
considering the purpose and audience.
phoneme
The smallest unit of sound that can change the meaning of a word (for 
example, the first letter of bat, rat, and cat).
prefix
A group of letters, such as un- or re-, that is added to the beginning of a 
word to change its meaning.

5.6 Glossary
publishing
Sharing a final revised, edited, and proofread piece of writing with others. 
Sharing writing can be done by reading the writing out loud, by letting 
someone else read the writing, or by talking about the final product and its 
purpose and audience. Not all writing is published.
purpose for reading
The objective a reader is trying to achieve by reading a particular piece of 
text (for example, to be informed or to be entertained).
purpose for writing 
The objective a writer is trying to achieve with a particular piece of writing.
read-aloud
An instructional tool that a teacher uses to model expressive and fluent 
reading and introduce new vocabulary. In a read-aloud, the teacher reads to 
the whole class while students listen. 
reference tools
Supportive materials that students can access to help them write 
independently, such as word walls, anchor charts, vocabulary lists, 
posters, dictionaries, or other print in the classroom.
rhyme 
A correspondence in the ending sounds of words (for example, land and 
sand).
rime 
The part of a syllable after the onset that contains the vowel and all that 
follows it (for example, -at is the rime in cat; -and is the rime in land).
root word
A basic word with no prefix or suffix added to it.
scribe 
When the teacher or another adult writes down what a student is saying.

English 2nd Language Curriculum 5.7
sentence starter
A writing prompt that provides the beginning of a sentence. Students 
must use their own ideas to complete the sentence. For example, “On the 
weekend, ____________.” 
sentence stems
Writing tools that help students write in complete sentences. They help 
students learn about the structure of sentences and provide cues for 
students to complete sentences. 
sentence structures 
Structures can include simple and compound sentences. Students will learn 
to use sentence structures in context and for purposeful communication.
sentence type
Statements, questions, and commands are the main sentence types. 
A statement is used to provide information or to state something. A 
question is used to inquire about something or request information. A 
command directs or gives orders.
shared reading
An instructional tool that involves the teacher and students reading a text 
together.
shared writing
An instructional tool that involves the teacher and students composing a 
piece of writing together while the teacher acts as a scribe.
simile
a figure of speech that compares two different things using the words 
"like" or "as". It is used to make description more vivid or to draw a direct 
comparison. For example, "her smile was a bright as the Sun" or "he ran 
like the wind".

5.8 Glossary
simple and clear spoken language
The speaker uses familiar vocabulary and expressions, speaks in a 
straightforward manner at a comfortable pace, and can rephrase, repeat, 
or speak more slowly if needed. 
simple future tense 
Used to describe an action that has not happened yet (for example, “I will 
jump.”).
simple past tense
Used to describe an action that has happened in the past (for example, 
“I jumped.”).
simple questions
Literal questions that are repeatedly modelled by the teacher and use 
familiar vocabulary and language structures. These questions can be 
answered through yes-or-no verbal and non-verbal responses or may 
require one-word or full-sentence responses (for example, “What colour is 
this?”, "Red.").
simple sentence
A simple sentence includes a subject, a verb, and a complete thought.
simple stories
Predictable stories with minimal text and highly supportive visuals.
simple words
Short-vowel, one- to two-syllable words that do not have double consonants 
or consonant clusters.
sounding out
An important spelling strategy that focuses on the phonetic values of 
vowels and consonant blends in the context of English spelling conventions. 

English 2nd Language Curriculum 5.9
spelling pattern 
A group of letters that represents a sound. Spelling patterns include 
groups of letters (for example, -at and -ot) as well as digraphs—that is, 
two letters that represent one speech sound (for example, ch-).
suffix
A letter or group of letters, such as -ed or -s, that is added to the end of a 
word to change its meaning.
superlative adjectives
Adjectives used to compare three or more nouns. Regular superlatives 
add an -est to the base word (for example, tallest, smallest, and fastest). 
support
The need for slow and clearly articulated spoken language, frequent 
reminders, visual cues, modelling, non-verbal communication, gestures, and 
repetition, etc.
syllable 
A phonological unit with at least one vowel (for example, cat has one 
syllable; mother has two syllables).
synonym 
A word that has the same or a similar meaning as another word. For 
example, big and large are synonyms. 
text features
The physical or design characteristics of a text that clarify and/or give 
support to the meaning in the text (for example, the title, headings, and 
illustrations).
text form
A type of text that is characterized by particular elements. Songs, posters, 
and recipes are examples of different text forms. Selecting a text form is 
connected to the purpose for writing. More than one text form may be used 
for a given purpose.

5.10 Glossary
text connections
A comprehension strategy in which the reader makes connections to 
the text. A text-to-self connection is when a student makes a connection 
between lived experiences and a text (for example, noting a similarity or 
difference between a character and oneself). A text-to-text connection 
is when a student makes a connection between texts they have read or 
viewed (for example, noting similarities in the genre, text structure, or 
topic). A text-to-world connection is when a student makes a connection 
between the text and real-world happenings (for example, noting 
similarities or differences between a current event in the community 
and something that happens or is described in the text).
visuals 
Illustrations, photographs, maps, and graphs.
vocal expressions 
Volume, stress, pitch, intonation, and phrasing. Experimenting with vocal 
expressions helps students build oral fluency. 
word wall
A collection of important words that is displayed in the classroom. 
Students can refer to the word wall when writing independently.
writing path
Where to start writing and the path to follow when forming a letter.
writing tools
A variety of tools that students can use to communicate messages in 
print, such as pencils, crayons, markers, and paint.
