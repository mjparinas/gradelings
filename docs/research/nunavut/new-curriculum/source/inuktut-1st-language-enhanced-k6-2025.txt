PREFACE
The language a child is exposed to first is the language they will acquire 
first. An Inuk child is more likely to develop linguistic competence and 
performance, initiated since birth, in a predominantly Inuktut-enriched 
environment. The form of Inuktut—in other words, the dialect the child is 
exposed to—is what they will use to think human thoughts, revel in human 
ideas, and so much more.
A child’s first language actually forms the foundation for learning another 
language, which is a crucial skill and way of being in Nunavut that our 
children need in order to thrive and prosper; we should never forget this as 
parents, teachers, institutions, and caregivers. This is a fact borne out by 
study after study, and it is a philosophy that informs the long-standing policy 
of the Government of Nunavut’s Department of Education to educate Inuit 
children in the Inuktut stream up to Grade 3.
The Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum (IL1), the Inuktut 
Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced (IL1e) and its sister 
document, the Inuktut 2nd Language Curriculum (IL2), are designed to expand 
on and extend this vision by insisting on describing Inuktut Language Arts 
in terms of their formal linguistic principles, such as those that govern 
grammatical, syntactical, phonological, and lexical considerations in 
relation to phrase-formation rules. This necessarily goes beyond teaching 
orthography (syllabics and roman) to a grammar-based, narrative-directed 
Inuit language instruction on a formalized, graduated scale intended to gauge 
degrees of the increasingly emergent creative and spontaneous novel usage of 
language on the part of the child.

This document is designed to use the ever-expanding library of levelled 
readers that emphasize vocabulary- and phrase-decoding and building rules 
of thumb for the students, and it relies on the lesson planning, rollout ideas, 
and suggestions in the IL1e Educator Handbook.
And, oh, we must never forget that language arts, especially Inuktut 
Language Arts, should be fun and interesting to draw on and keep the 
attention of these young, developing, growing minds—ideally for life.
Jaypeetee Arnakak 
Iqaluit, Nunavut

An Inuit view of language learning can be compared to a child learning how 
to compose, sing, and perform a drum dance. At first, the child may not even 
be able to hold up the drum. However, the child will progress from imitating 
songs modelled by the teacher to creating and performing a song and 
drum dance independently, expressing himself or herself through voice and 
movement. 
Inunnguiniq
Inunnguiniq is a process within Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ) that intends to 
make a child a capable human being. Collectively, it unites families, schools, 
and communities to work together and care for, raise, and teach our students. 
Inunnguiniq is grounded in the strengths of relationships and effective 
communication. Learning Inuktut in an Inunnguiniq way is founded on and 
supported by IQ Principles and Inuit ways of knowing. Inuktut is a natureembedded language and inherently expresses relationships between people 
and the environment. 
Inunnguiniq develops interconnectedness and requires language and 
respectful interaction. There is an emphasis on oral fluency and visualization 
for both verbal and memory development. The reliance on strong language 
and communication skills provides a foundation for developing the profound 
conceptual functioning required for what Inuit describe as “deep thinking” 
and the ability to become effective in seeking solutions. Inunnguiniq ensures 
a solid foundation for the future of every student and future generations. 

Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced 3.7
Instruction of Inuktut Language Arts requires collaboration with Inuit Elders 
in order to infuse core IQ values and beliefs into teaching and learning 
methods. Language learning is a process and requires the active and ongoing 
involvement of Elders and other community members to ensure that IQ lives 
in all our schools.
Our ultimate responsibility as educators and partners in education is to 
instill in our students the value of Inuit culture and language. By studying 
Inuktut Language Arts, we will teach the importance of understanding, 
compassion, harmony, and unity among Nunavummiut and the world. 
This living curriculum contributes to the process of reconciliation. Through 
teaching and learning, we serve as bridges to heal past hurts and injustices. 
Teach with Inunnguiniq as our foundation, and our children will develop into 
capable human beings. Ajunngitugut!

3.10 Introduction
About the Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language 
Curriculum, Enhanced (IL1e)
The IL1e was researched, written, and reviewed by a working group of 
educational and Inuktut-language experts from across Nunavut. The 
working group carefully created a curriculum that is accessible, balanced, 
and reflective of the unique nature of Inuktut as a language and the culture 
of Nunavut. The IL1e outlines grade-level Learning Outcomes for Inuktut 
first-language students in communities where there may be some language 
erosion.
The IL1e is designed to be used with students whose first language is Inuktut 
and who have achieved a level of fluency in Inuktut. The IL1e has been 
developed for communities or cohorts of students with strong Inuktut firstlanguage skills and teaches students to communicate in Inuktut first while 
gradually introducing English as a second language. Schools may initially 
implement the IL1 to support students in strengthening their language 
development. If students are exceeding the language expectations of the IL1, 
schools have the option to shift to the IL1e to further support the language 
needs of their students.

Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced 3.15
Learning Strands and Concepts 
The IL1e has been organized into the following Learning Strands: Oral 
Communication, Reading, and Writing. The grade-specific Learning Outcomes 
within each Learning Strand have been organized according to their 
Learning Concept so there is a clear progression of student learning from 
Kindergarten to Grade 6. 
Oral Communication 
Oral communication includes speaking and listening skills, which are 
essential for thinking, learning, and developing social skills. Listening 
and speaking enable students to explore ideas and concepts, as well as 
understand and organize their experiences and knowledge. Students learn 
to communicate thoughts, feelings, experiences, information, and opinions, 
and they learn to understand themselves and others. Oral language carries 
a community’s stories, values, beliefs, and traditions. 
The Learning Concepts in the Oral Communication Learning Strand of the 
IL1e are: 
OC.1 Listening to understand
Students show their understanding of Inuktut through the use of verbal 
and non-verbal responses. The progression is connected to the quantity 
and complexity of the vocabulary students will learn.
From Kindergarten to Grade 3, students begin to follow simple directions 
and listen for different purposes. From Grades 4 to 6, students follow 
multi-step directions and develop strategies to listen more effectively in 
different situations.
OC.2 Speaking to communicate
Students use expressive language processes in order to be understood 
by others. The Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept focus on 
the knowledge and skills students need to organize their thoughts and 
make appropriate language choices in order to communicate effectively 
for specific purposes and audiences.

3.16 Introduction
OC.3 Interacting with others 
Students engage in listening and speaking when interacting with 
others. Students first learn how to ask and respond to simple questions 
and then learn to use questions as a way of clarifying information and 
perspectives. Students also begin to interact with others by sharing 
their thoughts and feelings, and then they build on those skills in order 
to deliver presentations and speak to different audiences.
OC.4 Traditional stories and oral history
Sharing and discussing traditional stories and oral history is an 
important part of cultural heritage. The Learning Outcomes in this 
Learning Concept are the same in Grades 5 and 6. The progression is 
connected to the length and complexity of the traditional stories the 
students listen to and retell.
Reading
Reading involves making meaning from text. Students begin to develop 
lifelong reading habits and attitudes toward reading in the early years of 
school. Supporting students’ reading skills in a nurturing and engaging 
learning environment encourages students to develop an interest in and 
love of reading.
Students move through a series of developmental stages as they learn 
to read. From one stage to the next, students become more independent 
as readers by developing and applying skills and strategies for decoding, 
comprehension, and fluency.
The Learning Concepts in the Reading Learning Strand are:
R.1 Comprehension 
Comprehension refers to processes students need to interact with and 
make meaning from texts. The Learning Outcomes are similar from 
Grades 1 to 3 and Grades 4 to 6. The progression is connected to the 
increasingly sophisticated strategies students use to understand texts, 
as well as the difficulty of the texts themselves. See the IL1e Educator 
Resource Handbook for more information about grade-level texts. 

Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced 3.17
To support their reading, students first learn about comprehension 
strategies and then learn to select appropriate strategies as they read. 
Comprehension strategies include:
• Visualizing: Making a picture in your mind of what you read.
• Rereading: Going back over part of a text that has already been 
read and using the meaning of the text to read an unfamiliar word. 
• Reading on: Continuing to read after encountering a difficult or 
unknown word, then rereading the text and using the meaning of 
what was read to help understand the unknown word.
• Making connections: Making connections to the text to clarify 
and deepen understanding. There are three main types of 
connections: text-to-self, text-to-text, and text-to-world. See the 
glossary for more information about the types of text connections. 
• Determining importance: Deciding what is important 
information in a text and what is not. This will help with 
summarizing/paraphrasing (see below).
• Inferring: Interpreting what is meant in a text by combining 
information about what is read with personal experiences or 
knowledge (for example, reading for what the writer means but 
does not say directly).
• Predicting: Using prior knowledge, knowledge of other texts, or 
knowledge of what has happened in the text so far to guess what 
will happen next.
• Scanning: Looking quickly through the text to find specific 
information. This is useful for certain reading purposes, such as 
reading a text to answer a specific question.
• Self-questioning: Asking questions to oneself before, during, and 
after reading to check comprehension.
• Summarizing/paraphrasing: Reducing the amount of 
information in a text to the most important points.
For more information about comprehension strategies, see the IL1e 
Educator Handbook.

3.18 Introduction
R.2 Fluency 
Students develop the ability to read continuous text with accuracy, good 
momentum and phrasing, and appropriate pausing, intonation, and 
stress. Fluency supports comprehension and is an essential element of 
both reading out loud and reading silently. The Learning Outcomes in 
this Learning Concept are similar from Kindergarten to Grade 6. The 
progression is connected to how fluency applies to grade-level texts.

R.3 Text forms and purposes 
Students learn about different text forms. Traditional stories, posters, 
poetry, and information books are examples of text forms. Students 
also learn about the different purposes of reading, such as to be 
entertained, locate specific information, or expand vocabulary. 
Learning about the different purposes of reading and the text forms 
that serve those purposes is an important part of becoming a skilled 
reader. 
Writing
Writing is a way to communicate and learn. Learning to write involves 
certain key concepts, which include:
• Writing is a way to communicate ideas and information.
• Writing is a process.
• We write for different purposes and audiences, using different forms.

Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced 3.19
Just as students need focused and direct attention on learning to read, they 
also need daily instruction about how to write. Students move through 
a series of developmental stages as they learn to write. From one stage to 
the next, students learn more about the purposes for writing, writing as a 
process, and basic writing skills in Inuktut. As students learn, they begin to 
apply this knowledge in their own writing.
The Learning Concepts in the Writing Learning Strand are:
W.1 Purpose, form, and audience 
Students learn that writing is done for a purpose. Some common 
purposes for writing include to narrate, entertain, describe, inform, 
inquire, persuade, and analyze. Students are also exposed to and 
practise writing in a variety of text forms. Examples of text forms 
include stories, letters, reports, and poetry. Students also begin to 
consider for whom a piece of writing is intended and learn how the 
audience influences the writing (for example, when choosing vocabulary 
or text form).
W.2 Writing process
Students develop the ability to create and produce a finished text. They 
learn to plan for writing and organize their thoughts and ideas. They 
engage in writing to communicate thoughts, feelings, opinions, and 
ideas, and they learn to revise and edit their writing before sharing it 
with others. Students also learn about consulting reference tools and 
knowledge holders (including parents and family, Elders, skilled artisans, 
storytellers, and other school staff) as an important part of the writing 
process.
W.3 Conventions
Students develop an understanding of how to use the elements and 
mechanics of writing (such as spelling, grammar, punctuation, and 
word choice) to make their writing clear and comprehensible to others. 

3.20 Introduction
Grammar and Vocabulary
The IL1e Learning Outcomes include grade-appropriate vocabulary and 
grammatical elements necessary for students to listen, speak, read, and write 
effectively. The IL1e Educator Handbook provides grammar and vocabulary 
lists developed by Inuktut language experts, linguists, and educators to 
support the use of the IL1e. These lists reflect the vocabulary and syntactic 
elements that students will encounter when using books in the Inuktut 
guided reading program and other Inuktut Titiqqiriniq resources. These lists 
are intimately connected to the learning of developmentally appropriate 
language structures.
Vocabulary lists and syntactic elements
The Inuktut vocabulary lists include roots (bare verbs and nouns) that are 
completed by pronominal endings that function as subjects/objects of the 
predicate (for example, I am...; am I...?; and let me...; and other pronouns) 
along with other argument clauses of the predicate as necessary. These 
surrounding grammatical elements complete the meaning of the predicate. 
Tenses and other modifiers between the root and subject pronoun may 
be inserted and varied depending on the focus of a lesson. Incorporating 
vocabulary with syntactic elements will ensure that students are connecting 
language use to real-life purposes.
Inuktut Titiqqiriniq is a comprehensive Inuktut literacy program that 
was created in Nunavut. Inuktut Titiqqiriniq was developed by Nunavut 
educators, linguists, and language consultants, with constant testing 
and input by Nunavut classroom teachers. Inuktut Titiqqiriniq provides 
instructional tools and resources to help students develop strong 
Inuktut Language Arts skills. For more information about the Inuktut 
Titiqqiriniq program, speak to your school’s literacy coach.

Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced 3.21
Words and Phrases vocabulary lists
The Words and Phrases vocabulary lists reflect the word choices made for the 
Inuktut guided reading program in Inuktut Titiqqiriniq. Dialectal decisions 
are based on what terms the working group of Inuktut language experts from 
across Nunavut considered to be the most accessible for students learning to 
read and write. 
Social Emotional Literacy Terminology vocabulary lists
The Social Emotional Literacy Terminology vocabulary lists provide 
words and phrases for each grade connected to the development of social 
emotional expression. When selecting the words and phrases for each grade, 
consideration was given to which words and phrases would be most useful 
and accessible for students at each grade level. 
Traditional Terminology vocabulary lists
The Traditional Terminology vocabulary lists provide traditional Inuktut 
words and phrases for each grade. When selecting the words and phrases for 
each grade, attention was given to possible cross-curricular connections (for 
example, Social Studies and Science). 
The lists provided in the IL1e are just a starting point, and teachers 
should not feel limited to using only the terms provided in the lists.

GRADE 1: ORAL COMMUNICATION 
Students will develop the oral communication skills necessary to speak and interact 
with others and listen and respond to texts and to others in Inuktut.
Guiding Question: How can I use verbal and non-verbal expressions to communicate with others?
Learning 
Concept 
Students will 
learn about:
Learning Outcome
By the end of Grade 1, students will be able to:
1. Listening to 
understand 
1.OC.1.1
Follow multiple-step directions to complete grade-level tasks 
and activities.
 
Notice if the student:
• Understands and follows simple directions that have one or two steps.
Additional notes:
Students may require occasional reminders or repetition of instructions. There 
are strategies to support students who have difficulty processing language and 
remembering auditory information, such as predictable classroom routines, 
preferential seating, and visual cues.
1.OC.1.2
Begin to demonstrate active listening skills with a focus on 
verbal and non-verbal communication and gestures in formal 
and informal settings. 
 
Notice if the student:
• Waits their turn to speak.
• Looks at the person who is speaking.
• Doesn’t interrupt.
• Notices non-verbal cues.
• Listens attentively to stories told by teachers or Elders.
Additional notes:
Difficulties with hearing can be misunderstood as misbehaviour.

2. Speaking to 
communicate
1.OC.2.1
Demonstrate the ability to communicate thoughts, feelings, and 
information using non-verbal communication, gestures, and 
learned vocabulary. 
 
Notice if the student:
• Joins in with whole-class and small-group discussions and in conversations 
 initiated by others.
• Speaks in a clear, coherent manner.
• Speaks confidently with friends, classmates, and teachers.
• Takes turns in conversations without interrupting others.
• Initiates conversations with classmates, teachers, and members of the school 
 community.
• Uses conjunctions (for example, ᐊᒻᒪ/amma, -ᓗ/-lu, ᐅᕝᕙᓘᓐᓃᑦ/uvvaluunniit, 
 ᑭᓯᐊᓂ/kisiani).
• Uses correct determiners (pointing terms) (for example, ᐅᕙᖓ/uvanga, 
 ᐃᕝᕕᑦ/ivvit, ᐅᓇ/una, ᐃᖕᓇ/ingna).
• Uses correct tenses (for example, -ᓚᐅᖅ-/-lauq-, -ᓛᖅ-/-laaq-, -ᖅᑲᐅ-/-qqau-, 
 -ᓂᐊᖅ-/-niaq-, -ᕋᑖᖅ-/-rataaq-, -ᔪᒫᖅ-/-jumaaq-, -ᓯᒪ-/-sima-), with 
 occasional reminders.
• Uses simple possessives in the singular, dual, and plural. 
Additional notes: 
Non-verbal communication and gestures may include raising one’s eyebrows, 
nodding, scrunching one’s nose, or shaking one’s head. 
A short sentence includes a verb phrase with a verb root and a verb ending (for 
example, ᐊᓂᔪᖅ/anijuq [he or she exits]) and a simple noun phrase that includes a 
noun root and a preposition or article (noun ending; for example, ᐃᒡᓗᒥ/iglumi [in 
the house] or [at the house]). 
Conjunctions are word chunks (morphemes) used to connect clauses or sentences 
(for example, -ᒐᒪ/ᑲᒪ/ᓇᒪ/ᕋᒪ/-gama/kama/nama/rama).
Tense is a word element added to a phrase to indicate when the action happened 
(for example, -ᓚᐅᖅ-/-lauq-, -ᓛᖅ-/-laaq-, -ᖅᑲᐅ-/-qqau-, -ᓂᐊᖅ-/-niaq-, 
-ᕋᑖᖅ-/-rataaq-, -ᔪᒫᖅ-/-jumaaq-, -ᓯᒪ-/-sima-).
Possessive is the word element used to show the relationship of belonging 
between one thing and another (for example, ᐊᓈᓇᒐ/anaanaga [1st person], 
ᐊᓈᓇᐃᑦ/anaanait [2nd person], ᐊᓈᓇᖓ/anaananga [3rd person]).

1.OC.4.1

2. Fluency 1.R.2.1

thought.

continues to the next line down at the left side again.

4.26
1.R.2.2
Demonstrate understanding of basic sentence structures 
identifying the beginning and end of the sentence.
 
Notice if the student:
• Begins to recognize that a simple sentence has at least one noun and at least one verb.
• Recognizes familiar root words and endings. 
• Pauses at the end of a sentence.
• Reads syllabics/letters written in books, charts, and labels back to the teacher 
 without assistance.
Additional notes:
A simple sentence includes at least one noun and one verb.
A root is a basic, unmodified unit of meaning that comes at the beginning of the 
word. ᐃᓄᒃ/Inuk (person) is an example of a root. 
An example of a familiar sentence beginning is ᐅᓇ/una (for example, ᐅᓇ ᑐᒃᑐ/
una tuktu). An example of a familiar sentence ending is -ᔪᖓ/-junga (for example, 
ᑕᑯᔪᖓ/takujunga).

• Uses strategies such as:
 ° Sounding out

Additional notes:
Decoding strategies are a variety of techniques students use to decode words, 
including sounding out, using visuals (for context clues), breaking longer words into

Visuals may include illustrations and photographs.

1.W.2.4
Begin to identify ways to improve writing by proofreading

Notice if the student:
• Reads their own writing out loud to see if it makes sense (revising).

[to a person]).
A sentence is a verb phrase and a noun phrase that together convey a complete 
thought.
Editing involves the student improving the meaning of individual sentences. 
Adding, removing, and changing word chunks (morphemes) or words are examples 
of editing.

GLOSSARY 
The following definitions and examples are intended to help teachers use 
this document. The examples provided are suggestions and are not meant to 
be exhaustive. 
adjective
A word element added to describe a noun root (for example, 
-ᕐᔪᐊᖅ-/-rjuaq-).
adverb
A word element added to describe a verb root (for example, 
-ᓕᖅ-/-liq-). 
audience 
The intended readers, listeners, or viewers of a particular text. 
basic punctuation
The punctuation used to end sentences. Periods, question marks, and 
exclamation marks are examples of basic punctuation.
character
An imaginary person who inhabits a literary work. Literary characters 
may be major or minor, static (unchanging) or dynamic (capable of 
change).
clause
A group of words that consists of a subject and a verb and provides 
information about what the subject is doing.
complete sentence 
A sentence that expresses a complete idea and makes sense on its 
own. Also called a well-formed sentence.

5.2 Glossary
complex punctuation 
The punctuation used in compound and complex sentences. Commas, 
colons, and semicolons are examples of complex punctuation.
complex sentence 
A sentence consisting of a main clause and one or more subordinate 
clauses.
comprehension strategies 
The strategies that students use before, during, and after listening, 
reading, and viewing to make meaning from text. Examples include:
• Making connections from prior knowledge and experience to 
familiar texts
• Visualizing to clarify or deepen understanding of a text
• Finding important ideas 
• Predicting
• Asking questions
• Summarizing information
• Inferring 
• Analyzing and synthesizing
• Skimming text for information or details 
• Scanning text to determine the purpose or type of 
• Adjusting reading speed according to the level of difficulty of the 
text or the kind of reading (for example, a simple story or more 
complicated factual text)
concepts of print
The awareness of how print works for the purposes for reading. Print 
concepts include directionality (Inuktut text is read from left to right, 
top to bottom), the difference between syllabics/letters and words 
(syllabics/letters and finals represent sounds; words are made up of 
roots and morphemes [chunks and affixes]; there are spaces between 
words), the use of punctuation, and common characteristics of books 
(for example, title, author, front and back covers).

Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced 5.3
conjunctions
Word chunks (morphemes) used to connect clauses or sentences (for 
example, -ᒐᒪ/-ᑲᒪ/-ᓇᒪ/-ᕋᒪ/-gama/-kama/-nama/-rama).
decoding 
Using knowledge of the relationships between sounds and 
syllabics/letters to read words.
decoding strategies 
A variety of techniques students use to decode words. Decoding 
strategies include:
• Sounding out
• Using visuals (for context clues)
• Breaking longer words into parts
• Skimming
• Scanning
draft
A first attempt at writing thoughts and ideas down in an organized 
way. Some writing tasks involve writing more than one draft, before 
creating a final draft. 
editing
Improving the meaning of individual sentences. Adding, removing, 
and changing word chunks (morphemes) or words are examples of 
editing.
environmental print 
Print that can be seen in our everyday surroundings, such as signs, 
logos, labels, and packaging.
exact language
The use of less descriptive, more precise language (for example, 
instead of saying “not deep,” using the word “shallow”).
familiar texts
Texts with words and phrases students have been exposed to over 
multiple readings.

5.4 Glossary
fluency
The ability to read continuous text with accuracy, good momentum 
and phrasing, and appropriate pausing, intonation, and stress. 
genre
A category of written text that is defined by a specific style, form, or 
content (for example, narrative). 
graphic organizer
A visual framework (such as a Venn diagram, word web, or flow 
chart) that helps students organize, analyze, synthesize, and assess 
information and ideas. 
knowledge holders
Members of the school and larger community, including parents and 
family, Elders, skilled artisans, storytellers, and other school staff. 
morphemes
The smallest pieces of language that have meaning. Also called word 
chunks.
Non-verbal communication and gestures
Ways of communicating without the use of verbal language. Gestures 
may include raising one’s eyebrows, nodding, scrunching one’s nose, 
or shaking one’s head.
pace
With respect to oral communication, the speed at which a person 
communicates.
paragraph
A group of sentences that contain related information. When a text 
has more than one paragraph, the paragraphs are visually distinct 
from one another. 

Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced 5.5
phonemic awareness
The ability to recognize and manipulate speech sounds.
phrase
The verb or noun part of a sentence (for example, ᐃᓄᖕᒧᑦ/inungmut
[to a person]).
planning
Often one of the steps in the writing process, planning involves 
organizing one’s thoughts about what is going to be written. This may 
include brainstorming ideas, deciding what to write first, second, etc., 
and considering the purpose and audience. 
plot
A series of events or incidents in the action of a story, play, film, or 
novel.
possessive 
The word element used to show the relationship of belonging between 
one thing and another (for example, ᐊᓈᓇᒐ/anaanaga [1st person], 
ᐊᓈᓇᐃᑦ/anaanait [2nd person], ᐊᓈᓇᖓ/anaananga [3rd person]).
prepositional phrase
A series of words made up of a preposition and its object. Examples of 
prepositional phrases are ᓴᓂᐊᓂ/saniani, ᑎᒫᓂ/timaani, ᖄᖓᓂ/
qaangani, ᐊᑖᓂ/ataani, and ᖁᓛᓂ/qulaani.
proofreading
Fixing mistakes in spelling, punctuation, and grammar.
public speaking
Making an oral presentation to an audience. Public speaking can 
include the use of visual aids.

5.6 Glossary
public speaking strategies
Strategies that support the ability to communicate effectively to an 
audience. 
publishing
Sharing the final revised, edited, and proofread piece of writing with 
others. Sharing a piece of writing can be done by reading it out loud, 
letting someone else read the writing, or doing both. Not all writing is 
published.
punctuation
The marks used in writing to separate sentences and their elements 
to clarify meaning. Basic punctuation is used to end sentences. 
Periods, question marks, and exclamation marks are examples of 
basic punctuation. Complex punctuation is used in compound and 
complex sentences. Commas, colons, and semicolons are examples of 
complex punctuation. 
read-aloud
An instructional approach that involves the teacher reading a text 
out loud to the students. Students may need supportive prompts from 
the teacher to ask appropriate questions.
reading purpose
The objective a reader is trying to achieve by reading a particular 
piece of writing. 
reading strategies
Approaches used before, during, and after reading to decode 
unfamiliar words, determine meaning, and increase understanding of 
a text.
reference tools
Resources used to help locate information, such as word walls, posters, 
books, visual dictionaries, etc. 

Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced 5.7
return sweep
When the reader comes to the end of a line of text and continues to 
the next line down at the left side again.
revising
Making changes to the ideas and organization of the writing as a 
whole. Adding, removing, and rearranging sentences are examples of 
revising.
roman orthography
The Latin alphabet used to write Inuktut.
root
A basic, unmodified unit of meaning that comes at the beginning of 
the word. ᐃᓄᒃ/Inuk (person) is an example of a root. 
sentence
A sentence is a verb phrase and a noun phrase that together convey a 
complete thought. 
sentence structure
The complexity of a sentence (for example, main and adjunct clauses 
working together to form complete thoughts and ideas). 
sentence type
Statements, questions, and commands are the main sentence types. 
A statement is used to provide information or to state something. A 
question is used to inquire about something or request information. A 
command directs or gives orders. 
setting
The time and place of a story, play, film, or novel.

5.8 Glossary
shared reading
An instructional tool that involves the teacher and students reading a 
text together.
shared writing
An instructional tool that involves the teacher and students writing a 
text together.
short sentence
A sentence that includes a verb phrase with a verb root and a verb 
ending (for example, ᐊᓂᔪᖅ/anijuq [he or she exits]) and a simple 
noun phrase that includes a noun root and a preposition or article 
(noun ending; for example, ᐃᒡᓗᒥ/iglumi).
simple texts
Texts that have very supportive visuals.
simple sentence
A sentence that includes at least one noun and one verb.
simple stories
Stories that are predictable, have minimal text, have highly 
supportive visuals, and are familiar as well as traditional.
summarize
To retell experiences, events, and stories using only the most 
important facts and ideas. 
supporting sentences
The sentences that explain, expand, illustrate, or prove the main 
point. 

Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced 5.9
syllable
A distinct unit of pronunciation that forms the basis of the syllabic 
system (for example, ᐊᓱ/asu has two syllables; ᐱᓱᒃᑐᖅ/pisuktuq
has three). A syllable can be open or closed. An open syllable has 
no final (CV; for example, the second syllable of ᐊᓱ/asu). A closed 
syllable has a final (CVC; for example, the second and third syllables 
of ᐱᓱᒃᑐᖅ/pisuktuq).
tense
A word element added to a phrase to indicate when the action 
happened (for example, -ᓚᐅᖅ-/-lauq-, -ᓛᖅ-/-laaq-, -ᖅᑲᐅ-/-qqau-, 
-ᓂᐊᖅ-/-niaq-, -ᕋᑖᖅ-/-rataaq-, -ᔪᒫᖅ-/-jumaaq-, -ᓯᒪ-/-sima-).
text features
Features include all the components of a text that are not the main 
body. They are used to clarify and/or give support to the meaning in 
the text.
text form
A kind of text that is characterized by particular elements. 
Traditional stories, labels, and recipes are examples of different text 
forms.
text-to-self connections 
A text-to-self connection is when a student makes a connection 
between lived experiences and a text (for example, noting a similarity 
or difference between a character and oneself). 
text-to-text connections 
A text-to-text connection is when a student makes a connection 
between texts they have read (for example, noting similarities in the 
genre, text structure, or theme). 

5.10 Glossary
text-to-world connections 
A text-to-world connection is when a student makes a connection 
between the text and real-world happenings (for example, noting 
similarities or differences between a current event in the community 
and something that happens or is described in the text). 
theme
A key topic, idea, or message explored in a text. 
topic sentence
A sentence that expresses the main idea of a paragraph.
voice
Also often referred to as “tone,” “mood,” or “style.” Developing a voice 
in writing involves experimenting with language and text structure to 
create writing that is individual, expressive, and engaging.
well-formed phrase
A phrase that includes the use of verb and noun roots with the proper 
grammatical elements to create new words, such as ᖃᕆᓴᖅ + ᐅ + ᔭᖅ/
qarisaq + u + jaq to mean personal computer.
well-formed sentence
A sentence that expresses a complete idea and makes sense on its 
own. Also called a complete sentence.
word
A word is a noun or a noun or verb root that has been modified by 
an additional affix or ending to form a new word. ᐃᓄᓕᕆᔨ/inuliriji
(social worker) is an example of a word.
word chunks
The smallest pieces of language that have meaning. Also called 
morphemes.

Inuktut Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, Enhanced 5.11
word wall
A collection of important word chunks (morphemes) and words 
displayed in the classroom. Students can refer to the word wall when 
writing independently. 
writing path
Shows where to start writing and the path to follow to form a 
syllabic/letter. 
writing process
Steps followed in creating and sharing a piece of writing. These steps 
often include planning, writing, revising, editing, and publishing. 
writing purpose
The objective a writer is trying to achieve with a particular piece of 
writing.
