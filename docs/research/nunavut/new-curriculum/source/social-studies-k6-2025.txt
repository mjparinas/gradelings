ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS
The Nunavut Department of Education would like to extend its sincere 
gratitude to the large number of people who have worked hard to develop 
this made-in-Nunavut K-6 Curriculum. The process has been going on 
for many years and has included Elders, educators, department staff, 
community members, and subject matter specialists, as well as those 
involved in editing and design. 
The Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Principles are not only embedded in the 
curriculum but also truly facilitated its development. Nunavut Elders 
contributed greatly to the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework 
for Nunavut Curriculum (2007), which formed the foundation for this 
curriculum. Extensive consultations and meetings with educators, Elders, 
and other stakeholders, prior to the writing of actual learning outcomes, 
helped create the framework and the content for the curriculum. In the 
holistic tradition of Inuit culture, there was close collaboration among 
the writers of the different subject areas to ensure that the educational 
experience of Nunavut children would be fully integrated. 
The valuable efforts, knowledge, skills, and experience of all contributors 
have helped to fulfill one of Nunavut’s fundamental goals as a territory: to 
provide our students with a unique Nunavut-made curriculum – one in 
which our learners can see themselves and their communities and which 
will influence them long into the future.

NALUNAIQSIJJUT
PREFACE
Social studies education enables students to engage with and learn from the 
past through various types of evidence, including oral narratives. It supports 
students as they explore the many factors that shape society and daily life, 
cultivate respect for diverse perspectives, and use their expanded scope of 
understanding to build inclusive communities.
The kindergarten to Grade 6 Social Studies Curriculum is designed to help 
Nunavut students develop the knowledge and skills they need to become 
inquisitive, solution-seeking, and active members of their community. To 
empower students towards becoming strong culturally grounded leaders, 
this curriculum is immersed in Inuit culture and values. It fosters respect 
for Elders’ wisdom, promotes environmental stewardship, cultivates a deeper 
understanding of Inuit history and way of life, and supports students as they 
build a healthy future for themselves, their families, and their community. 
No words can adequately convey the importance of the relationship to the 
land for Inuit. Students learn to view the land as a provider and understand 
its role in sustaining life and shaping cultural identity. They examine the 
environmental impacts of human activity, reflect on sustainability practices, 
investigate the impacts of colonization, and develop mapping and spatial skills. 
Through the kindergarten to Grade 6 Social Studies Curriculum, students 
are encouraged to explore their identities, deepen their understanding of the 
world around them, and learn to live well together. They will cultivate an 
understanding of the importance of taking care of the land using traditional 
knowledge and contemporary technology.
2.1 Preface

Social Studies Curriculum 2.2
Involving Elders and local experts in the educational process creates a bridge 
between generations and fosters a sense of shared responsibility. Students 
in Nunavut are given the opportunity to participate in community-based 
projects and apply their learning to real-world situations. They will explore 
how traditional Inuit practices are affected by factors such as climate change, 
modern economic pressures, and evolving social norms. Topics such as the 
impacts of colonization and the creation of Nunavut, will help students 
develop a nuanced perspective about their place in history and their role in 
shaping the future.
By empowering students to think critically about their place in both local 
and global contexts, the curriculum aims to support students in becoming 
informed, engaged citizens who can contribute meaningfully to their 
communities and beyond.

As stated in the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for Nunavut 
Curriculum, “schooling in Nunavut should provide support to students in all 
areas of their development so that they can achieve personal goals, become 
well-equipped to contribute and serve their families and communities, 
demonstrate leadership and healthy attitudes, and be able to actively 
participate and contribute as Nunavut takes on new roles in the global 
community.”¹ To achieve this vision for education in Nunavut, it is imperative 
that the curriculum in all subject areas promotes holistic critical thinking 
and encourages continuous learning that can be transferred across contexts. 
It must also promote inclusion and provide opportunities to differentiate 
learning experiences to meet the needs of all students. The updated Nunavut 
Curriculum Framework will provide a common structure for the curriculum 
in each subject area and support this holistic, cross-curricular approach to 
teaching and learning.

The Curriculum Foundations—Nunavusiutit, Iqqaqqaukkaringniq, 
Uqausiliriniq, and Aulajaaqtut—are at the centre of the curriculum in each 
subject area. According to the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework 
for Nunavut Curriculum, the foundations “are generic in nature, transcend 
subject-specific content areas, and result in the application and transfer of 
specific learning to new contexts and situations. This approach to learning is 
designed to focus on the development of complex intellectual (metacognitive) 
skills and lead students to transformational ways of thinking and processing. 
This type of approach helps students understand the connections between 
various learnings and the strategies that lead to successful application of 
learning in new contexts.”² Within the Curriculum Foundations are the 
subject areas. These are areas of learning organized as discrete disciplines 
and taught in systemic ways over time.

The aims and principles of the Curriculum Foundations inform the Major 
Understandings in each subject area. Major Understandings are broad 
statements that reflect the essential principles and core ideas within a 
subject area that can be transferred across different areas of learning.³ These 
Major Understandings anchor the curriculum in the purpose for learning 
in each subject area and the values of Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ). They 
act as a lens through which teachers can make decisions about planning, 
instruction, and assessment.
The Major Understandings are explored through the Learning Strands, 
which represent the key concepts and processes within each subject area 
and the relationships among them. The Learning Strands remain consistent 
across grades and will encourage a richer exploration of how these concepts 
and processes work together over time. Within each Learning Strand, there 
are Learning Concepts and grade-specific Learning Outcomes. The 
Learning Concepts or Contexts outline the constructs, processes, and patterns 
within each Learning Strand. These concepts or contexts provide a structure 
through which students can organize and connect the Learning Outcomes. 
Learning Outcomes define the key knowledge, skills, and understandings 
students will develop at each grade level. These become the building blocks 
that support deeper conceptual understanding.
In addition, each grade level has Guiding Questions. The Guiding Questions
will help link the Learning Concepts or Contexts and Learning Outcomes 
back to the Curriculum Foundations. By building connections among the 
knowledge, skills, and understandings they have acquired, students will 
be able to explore the principles of the Curriculum Foundations in ageappropriate ways.
Teachers can use the Guiding Questions:
• In daily classroom activities and discussions
• As part of assessment tasks
• To plan units or themes
• To inspire inquiry-based projects

3.8 Introduction
What is the Social Studies Curriculum? 
Jaypetee Arnakak, a Nunavut visionary who first proposed Inuit 
Qaujimajatuqangit as government policy, has referred to a concept called 
the Great Conversation. One way of understanding this is to reflect on how 
since humans have been on Earth, they have been engaged in a conversation 
seeking wisdom and truth. As the conversation became global, it was most 
often confined to universities in large cities, and many, including most of the 
Indigenous People around the world, were entirely left out. This was certainly 
a considerable detriment to the Great Conversation and to those who had to 
sit silently and watch mistakes being made.
In Nunavut, as we move ahead with a new Social Studies curriculum, many 
feel that its goal must be to help prepare students become competent, 
confident, and enthusiastic participants in the Great Conversation. To realize 
this goal, it is essential that students understand Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit 
and the words and stories of their Elders. They must also develop a 
foundation that enables them to understand, respect, and engage with the 
other members of the Great Conversation. In Nunavut, Social Studies is a 
critical area of learning that involves the decisions people make to live 
well together on this planet.
Rationale
The Nunavut Social Studies Curriculum: Kindergarten to Grade 6 will provide 
a framework for engaging students in inquiry-based experiences that will 
help them own their learning, make meaning of the world they live in, and 
learn to live well together. 
Goals 
The goals of the Nunavut Social Studies Curriculum: Kindergarten to Grade 6 
are for students to develop an understanding of identity, their own and 
others’; to engage with the world; and to become capable persons. Colonization 
has played a significant role in Nunavut’s past and has created many 
obstacles for Inuit that continue to this day. The Nunavut Social Studies 
Curriculum: Kindergarten to Grade 6 must serve to address this reality and to 
provide students with tools for empowerment and reconciliation.

ORGANIZATION OF THE SOCIAL STUDIES 
CURRICULUM
Curriculum Foundation
The Social Studies Curriculum is part of the Nunavusiutit Curriculum 
Foundation.
The principles of the Nunavusiutit Curriculum Foundation focus on and 
draw from heritage, culture, history, geography, environmental science, 
civics, economics, current events, and worldviews. Through this Curriculum 
Foundation, students learn about the people and the land of Nunavut, the 
experiences of Nunavummiut in the past and present, and Nunavut’s critical 
links to national, circumpolar, and global issues.
Major Understandings
The principles of Nunavusiutit inform the three Major Understandings 
within the Social Studies Curriculum. These Major Understandings reflect 
the essential learning within the subject area and can act as a lens through 
which teachers can make decisions about planning, instruction, and 
assessment. The Major Understandings for the Social Studies Curriculum are: 
1. Inuuqatigiitsiarniq involves valuing diversity and recognizing one 
another’s rights, needs, and responsibilities.
2. Our understanding of the past comes from stories told in many ways and 
for different purposes. 
3. Knowing the land and how it sustains us provides a sense of identity and 
responsibility. 

3.10 Introduction
Learning Strands 
The Social Studies Curriculum has been organized by grade and then further 
broken down into three Learning Strands: Community, Stories, and The Land. 
Community: This Learning Strand explores relationships among people. It 
encompasses people being together, from a Kindergarten class to the entire 
world. In this Learning Strand, students will engage with diversity, rights, 
responsibilities, needs, migrations, governance, and all aspects of people 
trying to live well together.
Stories: This Learning Strand explores relationships between people and 
the past. Everything we know about the past comes to us in the form of a 
story. Even a footprint in the snow tells a story. We assess the significance 
of a story based on its impact. Finding the footprint of a lost hunter may 
become significant if it helps searchers find them. Stories can help us 
learn from the past so we can do better in the future. Understanding 
the purpose of a story and being able to confirm its validity are also very 
important aspects of this strand. As we have more access to stories through 
technology, interpreting stories is becoming more and more essential to 
living well together.
The Land: This Learning Strand explores relationships between people 
and the environment. The land provides everything we need to live, and for 
that reason, it must be respected and nurtured (Avatimik Kamattiarniq). 
The concept of the land means the terrestrial, aquatic, and atmospheric 
realms and all that reside there. Even the stars, Sun, Moon, and everything 
in space are connected to the land. In this strand, students will learn land 
literacy, or how to read the land. The rivers, the weather, the animals, and 
the plants all have vital information to share. Having a close relationship 
with the land can help us care for it and also be competent at allowing it to 
satisfy our needs. Students will also learn about diverse approaches to land 
use and how to assess these.

Social Studies Curriculum 3.11
Learning Concepts and Learning Outcomes 
Students will explore the Learning Strands of Community, Stories, and The 
Land through different Learning Concepts, which are unique to the subject 
area. In the Social Studies Curriculum, it is valuable to place learning within 
a context, both to make the learning meaningful and to ensure that certain 
aspects of knowledge are addressed. The Learning Concepts offer a useful 
learning habitat conducive to developing the Major Understandings. Social 
Studies can be a broad area of learning, and without context, it would be 
difficult for teachers to provide focus for students to see themselves in the 
learning and maintain consistency in classrooms across Nunavut. The 
Learning Concepts have been identified for each grade using the expanding 
horizons approach so that students’ learning experiences expand from the 
home to the world.
The Learning Concepts for each grade are:
• Kindergarten: Who Am I? 
In Kindergarten, students will focus on beginning to develop and 
understand who they are, or their identity. They will explore what 
makes us who we are, including family, culture, community, and 
our individual characteristics.
• Grade 1: My Community
In Grade 1, students will look specifically at their local municipality, 
but they will also develop an understanding that there are different 
types of communities and that people can belong to more than one. 
• Grade 2: Communities in Canada
In Grade 2, students will learn through the study of other 
communities in Canada. They will explore what gives other 
communities in Canada their identity and how they are 
interrelated. 
• Grade 3: Inuit and Other Indigenous Peoples
In Grade 3, students will explore Inuit and other Indigenous Peoples 
to understand both their diversity and some of their common 
characteristics.

3.12 Introduction
• Grade 4: Transition in the North
In Grade 4, students will begin to learn about the nature and 
impact of change in the North. Colonization is a central focus for 
this grade.
• Grade 5: Canadian Issues and Governance 
Grade 5 is all about Canada. Students will begin to investigate the 
processes that formed the country and the changing relationships 
among the people living here. 
• Grade 6: Global Issues and Governance
In Grade 6, students will explore global affairs and the ever-changing 
relationships among the countries and peoples on this planet. 
The Learning Outcomes in the Nunavut Social Studies Curriculum: 
Kindergarten to Grade 6 are arranged in an order of increasing complexity 
within each grade. This arrangement also demonstrates patterns across the 
Learning Strands and across grades. This is a suggested order that teachers 
may choose to follow, but as they gain experience with this curriculum, 
teachers should adjust and adapt to best meet the needs of their students.

Social Studies Curriculum 3.13
Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ) in Social Studies
Social Studies is a particularly rich subject area for teaching IQ because of its 
broad and multidisciplinary nature. Although IQ has been organized into eight 
principles, its holistic nature must always be kept in mind. The principles 
would not be necessary in a traditional Inuit pedagogy; however, they are 
necessary in the structure of the current curriculum. The Social Studies 
Curriculum has been organized into three strands: Community, Stories, and 
The Land. Each of these strands connects clearly with specific IQ Principles.
• Community: Inuuqatigiitsiarniq (people living well together). This 
principle is the foundation of the Major Understanding for this 
strand. Inuuqatigiitsiarniq involves valuing diversity and recognizing 
one another’s rights, needs, and responsibilities. 
• Stories: Pilimmaksarniq (the development of skills through 
observation, mentoring, practise, and effort). Stories have always 
been the means of transferring knowledge and wisdom to others, 
especially between generations. 
• The Land: Avatimik Kamattiarniq (taking care of the environment). 
The purpose of this strand is based entirely upon this IQ principle. 
The land will take care of you if you take care of it. 
The other five principles are integrated throughout the curriculum 
and have important roles during different parts of the learning process. 
Piliriqatigiinniq, for example, plays a strong role in the collaborative inquiry 
outcomes. The Community strand also has very strong connections to 
Aajiiqatigiingniq (consensus building and decision making, through the 
governance field of learning), Pijitsirniq (serving, through the responsibilities 
and leadership aspects), and Tunnganarniq (being welcoming and open, 
as a means toward building a healthy community). Qanuqtuurunnarniq 
(innovative solution seeking) can be applied everywhere. 

3.14 Introduction
Nunavut Social Studies competencies
The Nunavut Social Studies Curriculum: Kindergarten to Grade 6 emphasizes a 
set of six important competencies that are essential for Social Studies learning 
in Nunavut:
1. Work together with peers in Authentic Inquiry
2. Active community engagement
3. Historical Thinking Concepts
4. Holistic observation and analysis
5. Land-based learning
6. Cartographic interpretation and expression

Social Studies Curriculum 3.15
1. Work together with peers in Authentic Inquiry
Each Learning Strand begins with a Learning Outcome focused on 
students asking questions about the area of study to be explored. Not 
only is this the first step of inquiry, but it can also help the teacher take 
inventory of the extent of students’ prior knowledge. Encouraging students 
to return to these questions at the conclusion of a learning experience, a 
project, or a theme or unit is an important metacognitive experience. Each 
Learning Strand concludes with a Learning Outcome in which students 
are required to “work together with peers in Authentic Inquiry.” 
This is a competency that may be new to educators and students, but it 
is vital to extend and expand learning. Building the IQ Principles directly 
into the Learning Outcomes is one way to ensure that they are part of 
a student's learning experience. Strengthening students’ ability to work 
together reinforces the Inuit law of working together for the common 
good.4
 For more information on planning for this type of instruction 
and how it may look in the classroom, see the Social Studies Educator 
Handbook. 
The second aspect of this competency is Authentic Inquiry. Authentic 
Inquiry is an approach to learning that begins with the learner's interest 
and experience and is rooted in a place, a person or people, or an item. It 
moves from there through a process of facilitated knowledge construction 
to a particular negotiated outcome, including the contribution to one’s 
community and to the field of learning, and meets agreed assessment 
criteria.5
 Authentic Inquiry distinguishes itself pedagogically in its 
shift from a delivery of content determined by the teacher to reach a 
predetermined goal to one where the process to arrive at the learning is 
codeveloped over time.

3.16 Introduction
2. Active community engagement
To make learning in Social Studies as meaningful as possible, students 
need to be engaged in activities and experiences that involve and benefit 
their community. Even in these early grades, students can connect 
their learning to their community and find ways to participate in their 
community. This connects to the IQ Principles of Piliriqatigiingniq, 
Pijitsirniq, and Inuuqatigiitsiarniq. Community engagement also helps 
create important advocates among individuals and organizations who 
can help support students. Community engagement involves students 
participating in local activities and events, and community members 
coming into the classroom. It is common for students to ask, “Why are 
we learning this?” Strong community engagement can help answer this 
question and provide important motivation and vision for students in 
their academic pursuits.
3. Historical Thinking Concepts
Dr. Peter Seixas of the University of British Columbia began what became 
the Historical Thinking Project, of which Nunavut and all the other 
provinces and territories became participants. Through this project, six 
Historical Thinking Concepts were identified as key outcomes for students 
to effectively engage with the past.6
 The Social Studies Curriculum is 
rooted in the Historical Thinking Concepts. The purpose of this is to 
help students connect with the stories they are learning about. In past 
curricula, students would often read about an event, and its meaning 
would be provided by the writer or the historian. Using the Historical 
Thinking Concepts allows the student to become the historian. This is 
vital for Nunavut and for Indigenous scholars because so much of history 
has been written by non-Inuit and non-Indigenous People.

Social Studies Curriculum 3.17
• Historical Significance
How do we decide what and whose stories to tell?
Why should we care, today, about certain events, trends, and 
issues in the past? 
For example: Why is the signing of the Nunavut Land Claims 
Agreement important to Canadian history?
• Primary Source Evidence
How do we know what we know?
How do we find, select, contextualize, interpret, and evaluate 
sources?
Oral narrative is an important aspect of evidence, especially in 
the context of Inuit and other Indigenous Peoples. 
For example: What can an Elder’s recollections about moving 
into a community from the land tell us about the relationship 
between the Government of Canada and Inuit at that time?
• Continuity and Change
What has changed and what has remained the same over time? 
Does change always mean progress?
For example: What has changed and what has remained the 
same about being a student in the North between 1950 and 
today?
• Cause and Consequence
How and why have certain conditions and actions led to others? 
What are the causes that are hidden from view? 
For example: What were the causes and consequences of the 
tuberculosis epidemic in the North?
• Historical Perspective
What must we consider about the nature of life in the past if 
we are to truly understand it today? 
We must look at the past as a foreign country, with its different 
social, cultural, intellectual, and even emotional contexts that 
shaped people’s lives and actions. 
For example: How could young Inuit men and women accept 
arranged marriages back before the 1960s?

4. Holistic observation and analysis
Within The Land Learning Strand, there is a Learning Outcome 
that focuses on making and sharing observations about the land, 
including weather, sky, animals, plants, water, and ice. The required 
observations become increasingly sophisticated through the grades. This 
is a traditional component of Inuit pedagogy in which children were 
instructed to go out in the morning upon waking and then come into 
the tent, or iglu, to report what they had observed. This type of outcome 
reflects Inuuqatigiit: The Curriculum from the Inuit Perspective.
Observation has always been a key competency for Inuit, and it is one 
that some Elders feel is not as strong among young people as it once was. 
It is seldom found as a competency in curricula from other jurisdictions; 
however, its importance should not be overlooked in any society. This is 
a competency that can also be used in other aspects of Social Studies 
and across subject areas. Students can make careful social observations 
in their community. Careful observations can be helpful in such areas as 
search and rescue, fire, and other emergencies.
The holistic aspect of observation is also very important in Inuit society. 
In terms of environmental observation, one must use multiple senses 
and take in all aspects of weather, as well as flora, fauna, snow, ice, 
water, and human activity. This type of thinking will also yield deeper 
understanding of historical events, in that there are always multiple 
factors and perspectives to consider. As with a healthy community and 
environment, diversity is also essential to good observation.

Social Studies Curriculum 3.19
5. Land-based learning
Many Inuit view the land as a teacher. It is imperative that at least 
some part of students’ education take place on the land. It is also 
understandable that providing land-based education is not always simple 
or even possible. The quality of land-based learning in Nunavut schools 
can be highly dependent on particular schools, communities, and even 
individual staff members. Quality land programs need to be safe, and 
they also need to have rich learning expectations. The Land Learning 
Strand in this curriculum offers some Learning Outcomes that would 
be best addressed through a land-based program, if possible. If a school 
does not have land-based learning opportunities, teachers can still help 
students connect the land learning they experience with family or other 
programs to their learning in Social Studies. However, it is important to 
be sensitive to those students who do not receive land experiences from 
their family or others for many possible reasons. 
6. Cartographic interpretation and expression
Cartography is the study of maps. Reading and drawing maps is an 
important form of literacy. It is not something that we are born capable 
of doing, so it must be taught. In the early grades, it is very important 
that the expectations for learning in this area are developmentally 
appropriate. The three most important concepts in cartography are scale, 
orientation, and symbols. It will take time for students to grasp these, 
and mistakes will be quite common. The increasing use of Geographic 
Information Systems (GIS), a type of digital technology, is creating many 
new opportunities for students to both research and document the 
world. There are many types of employment opportunities for individuals 
who are comfortable with GIS, especially in Nunavut, and it is imperative 
that we help prepare our students for such opportunities. Cartography 
also plays a very strong role in Inuit culture in its relationship with the 
land. Land use maps developed in the Inuit Land-Use and Occupancy 
Project in the 1970s provided the foundation for the Nunavut Land 
Claims Agreement. Inuit drew maps showing traplines and hunting 
areas that covered almost every part of Nunavut. Students using this 
curriculum will be encouraged to use maps to help demonstrate ways of 
using and protecting the land, as is their responsibility within Avatimik 
Kamattiarniq.

An agreement signed by Nunavut Inuit representatives, the Northwest
