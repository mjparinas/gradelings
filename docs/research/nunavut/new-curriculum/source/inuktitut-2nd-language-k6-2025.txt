PREFACE
Many people assume that learning more than one language is confusing and, 
in fact, detrimental to the intellectual development of a child. Those who 
can speak two or even more languages would completely disagree with such 
an assessment, no matter what languages they speak.
This is because the language instinct that we are all born with does not 
behave, is not structured, like a computer memory. Our brains and our 
ability to think about and manipulate ideas (for amusement, to solve 
problems, etc.) just do not have the same limitations as our technologies do. 
Humans, to be sure, cannot make instantaneous calculations as computers 
can, but we can achieve and do so much more. We are human.
Babies are not taught language (not their first nor even their second 
language); they pick it up by mere exposure to the language because they 
have the neuroplasticity to absorb language and its universal patterns 
without any conscious volition. In fact, all languages contain nuances and 
structural complexities that most speakers never become aware of. But it 
certainly never detracts from their linguistic performance, and definitely not 
from their intelligence.
“Language is key to culture, and to individual and collective social wellbeing. Most consider erosion of Inuktitut, the traditional language, as 
the most serious threat to Inuit culture as a whole. Language ‘encodes’ 
our entire culture, enshrines our values and worldview, and, above all, 
bestows our identity.” 
Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Workshop (NSDC, 2000)
2.1 Preface

Inuktitut 2nd Language Curriculum 2.2
The human brain is designed like a sieve that has the capacity to capture 
language universals. All human languages have:
• Nouns, verbs, adverbs, adjectives, prepositions, conjunctions, and participles
• Phonological, syntactical, and grammatical rules
Wariness on the part of parents and even teachers stems mostly from 
the fact that no two languages ever organize the elemental and featural 
universals in the exact same way, and this gives the impression that perhaps 
some languages are somehow better than others—the “better” language 
tending to be the one that we are most familiar with.
As a bilingual person most of my life, I cannot imagine how my life would 
have turned out were I a unilingual Inuk, or if my first and only language 
were English. One language feeds off the other, one informs the other, and 
each generates insights and intellectual and psychological discernments that 
would not be possible otherwise.
It is in this spirit of possibilities for Nunavut children of all cultures and 
languages that we have lovingly, wholeheartedly developed this curriculum as 
a team of teachers, linguists, and other language specialists brought together 
by our passion for our respective crafts and professions. 
Jaypeetee Arnakak
Iqaluit, Nunavut
August 2019
“A commonly expressed fear about childhood bilingualism is that 
it will confuse the child. Some are also concerned that learning in 
Inuktitut or Inuinnaqtun will not meet the needs of the outside 
world. However, studies show that a child’s wider experiences in two 
languages and cultures will give the child many advantages, such as 
mental flexibility, enhanced concept formation, and a more diversified 
set of mental abilities. Studies also show children strong in their first 
language will acquire a second language easier and faster.”
Ilitaunnikuliriniq: Foundation for Dynamic Assessment as Learning in Nunavut Schools 
(Nunavut Department of Education, 2008)

KINDERGARTEN TO GRADE 6
As stated in the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for Nunavut 
Curriculum, “schooling in Nunavut should provide support to students in all 
areas of their development so that they can achieve personal goals, become 
well-equipped to contribute and serve their families and communities, 
demonstrate leadership and healthy attitudes, and be able to actively 
participate and contribute as Nunavut takes on new roles in the global 
community.”¹ To achieve this vision for education in Nunavut, it is imperative 
that the curriculum, in all subject areas, promotes holistic, critical thinking 
and encourages continuous learning that can be transferred across contexts. 
It must also promote inclusion and provide opportunities to differentiate 
learning experiences to meet the needs of all students. The updated Nunavut 
Curriculum Framework will provide a common structure for the curriculum 
in each subject area and support this holistic, cross-curricular approach to 
teaching and learning.

The Curriculum Foundations—Nunavusiutit, Iqqaqqaukkaringniq, 
Uqausiliriniq, and Aulajaaqtut—are at the centre of the curriculum in each 
subject area. According to the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework 
for Nunavut Curriculum, the foundations “are generic in nature, transcend 
subject-specific content areas, and result in the application and transfer of 
specific learning to new contexts and situations. This approach to learning is 
designed to focus on the development of complex intellectual (metacognitive) 
skills and lead students to transformational ways of thinking and processing. 
This type of approach helps students understand the connections between 
various learnings and the strategies that lead to successful application of 
learning in new contexts.”² Within the Curriculum Foundations are the subject 
areas. These are areas of learning organized as discrete learning disciplines 
and taught in systemic ways over time.

The aims and principles of the Curriculum Foundations inform the Major 
Understandings in each subject area. Major Understandings are broad 
statements that reflect the essential principles and core ideas within a 
subject area that can be transferred across different areas of learning.³ These 
Major Understandings anchor the curriculum in the purpose for learning 
in each subject area and the values of Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ). They 
act as a lens through which teachers can make decisions about planning, 
instruction, and assessment. 
The Major Understandings are explored through the Learning Strands, 
which represent the key concepts and processes within each subject area 
and the relationships among them. The Learning Strands remain consistent 
across grades and will encourage a richer exploration of how these concepts 
and processes work together over time. Within each Learning Strand, 
there are Learning Concepts and grade-specific Learning Outcomes. The 
Learning Concepts outline the constructs, processes, and patterns within 
each Learning Strand. These concepts provide a structure through which 
students can organize and connect the Learning Outcomes. Learning 
Outcomes define the key knowledge, skills, and understandings students will 
develop at each grade level. These become the building blocks that support 
deeper conceptual understanding.
In addition, each grade level has Guiding Questions. The Guiding Questions 
will help link the Learning Concepts and the Learning Outcomes back to 
the Curriculum Foundations. By building connections among the knowledge, 
skills, and understandings they have acquired, students will be able to explore 
the principles of the Curriculum Foundations in age-appropriate ways.
Teachers can use the Guiding Questions:
• In daily classroom activities and discussions
• As part of assessment tasks
• To plan units or themes
• To inspire inquiry-based projects

INTRODUCTION TO THE INUKTITUT 2ND 
LANGUAGE CURRICULUM (IL2)
An Inuit view of language learning can be compared to a child learning how 
to compose, sing, and perform a drum dance. At first, the child may not even 
be able to hold up the drum. However, the child will progress from imitating 
songs modelled by the teacher to creating and performing a song and 
drum dance independently, expressing himself or herself through voice and 
movement. 
Inunnguiniq
Inunnguiniq is a process within Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ) that intends to 
make a child a capable human being. Collectively, it unites families, schools, 
and communities to work together and care for, raise, and teach our students. 
Inunnguiniq is grounded in the strengths of relationships and effective 
communication. Learning Inuktut in an Inunnguiniq way is founded on and 
supported by IQ Principles and Inuit ways of knowing. Inuktut is a natureembedded language and inherently expresses the relationships between 
people and the environment. 
Inunnguiniq develops interconnectedness and requires language and 
respectful interaction. There is an emphasis on oral fluency and visualization 
for both verbal and memory development. The reliance on strong language 
and communication skills provides a foundation for the development of 
the profound conceptual functioning required for what Inuit describe as 

Inuktitut 2nd Language Curriculum 3.7
“deep thinking” and the ability to become effective in seeking solutions. 
Inunnguiniq ensures a solid foundation for the future of every student and 
future generations. 
The study of Inuktut as an additional language enables each student to 
understand and appreciate Inuktut and to use it confidently and competently 
in a variety of situations for communication, personal satisfaction, and 
learning. Research has shown that emphasizing oral communication 
and providing a language-rich environment that stresses purpose and 
understanding from the start is a crucial factor in language learning. The 
Inuktitut 2nd Language Curriculum (IL2) therefore encourages instructors to 
teach first for understanding and then for production.
Instruction in Inuktut as a second language requires collaboration with Inuit 
Elders in order to infuse core IQ values and beliefs into teaching and learning 
methods. Language learning is a process and requires the active and ongoing 
involvement of Elders and other community members to ensure that IQ lives 
in all our schools.

3.8 Introduction
The iglu is a symbol of survival and 
has also become a symbol for K–12 
education in Nunavut. The one-blockat-a-time metaphor embodies the 
stages of development children pass 
through as they progress through 
public schooling. On graduation, 
every child will have constructed 
an iglu strong enough to withstand 
their own weight and will be able to 
stand on the iglu to see what other 
opportunities can be built in the 
community and beyond. 
The children of Nunavut must be equipped to survive in this everchanging world. The age-old tools an Inuit child was given outfitted 
that child to survive the challenges of the land as well as life within a 
community of people. The caribou antler in the child’s hand represents 
the important connection to the environment. The environment 
has always supplied the natural resources required for success in the 
world. The antler is also a reminder of the value of practise and the 
importance of continual improvement in order to become successful. 
Our ultimate responsibility as educators and partners in education is to 
instill in our students the value of Inuit culture and language. By studying 
and using Inuktut, we will teach the importance of understanding, 
compassion, harmony, and unity among Nunavummiut and the world. 
This living curriculum contributes to the process of reconciliation. Through 
teaching and learning, we serve as bridges to heal past hurts and injustices. 
Teach with Inunnguiniq as our foundation, and our children will develop into 
capable human beings. Ajunngitugut!

(ILA1-F) was completed. This is a foundational research document that

The research and development used to write the ILA1-F has guided the

• IL1: Inuktut Language Arts, 1st Language Curriculum

3.10 Introduction
About the IL2
The IL2 was researched, written, and reviewed by a working group of 
educational and Inuktut-language experts from across Nunavut. The working 
group gave careful consideration to creating a curriculum that is accessible, 
balanced, and reflective of the unique nature of Inuktut as a language and 
the culture of Nunavut. The IL2 outlines grade-level Learning Outcomes for 
Inuktut second-language students. 
The IL2 is designed to be used with students whose first language is a 
language other than Inuktut. This includes students in communities 
undergoing language revitalization. The IL2 provides students with one period 
of Inuktut learning each day, allowing their Inuktut-language skills to grow 
each year. Students entering Kindergarten will have limited or no knowledge 
of Inuktut.

3.14 Introduction
Organization of the IL2 Curriculum Foundation
The IL2 is part of the Uqausiliriniq Curriculum Foundation. The principles 
of the Uqausiliriniq Curriculum Foundation focus on relationships in 
communication and language development, creative and artistic expression, 
and reflective and critical thinking. 
Major Understandings
The principles of Uqausiliriniq inform the three Major Understandings 
within the IL2 that continue from Kindergarten to Grade 6. These Major 
Understandings reflect the essential learning within the subject area 
and can act as a lens through which teachers can make decisions about 
planning, instruction, and assessment. The Major Understandings for the 
IL2 are:
1. Learning and valuing Inuktut is essential for the preservation of Inuit 
identity, culture, values, and worldview. 
2. Communicating effectively helps us to build relationships and participate 
more meaningfully in our communities. 
3. Making meaning in Inuktut encourages us to think critically and 
creatively and helps us come to common understandings. 

Inuktitut 2nd Language Curriculum 3.15
Learning Strands and Learning Concepts 
The IL2 has been organized into four Learning Strands: Oral Communication, 
Reading, Writing, and Language and Culture Appreciation. The grade-specific 
Learning Outcomes within each Learning Strand have been organized 
according to their Learning Concept so there is a clear progression of student 
learning from Kindergarten to Grade 6.
Oral Communication 
Oral communication includes speaking and listening skills. From 
Kindergarten to Grade 2, emphasis is placed on the development of oral 
communication skills. Students begin to develop familiarity with the 
sounds of the language and to learn and use the basic vocabulary needed 
to communicate simple ideas and information. From Grade 3 onward, 
students begin to use their oral communication skills to read and write 
meaningfully in Inuktut. 
The Learning Concepts in the Oral Communication Learning Strand of the 
IL2 are:
OC.1 Listening to understand
Students show their understanding of Inuktut through the use of verbal 
and non-verbal responses. The Learning Outcomes for this Learning 
Concept are the same from grades 4 to 6. The progression is connected 
to the quantity and complexity of the vocabulary students will learn.
From Kindergarten to Grade 3, students begin to develop a familiarity 
with the sounds of Inuktut and recognize familiar, grade-appropriate 
vocabulary through the use of classroom routines, games, and activities, 
and direct instruction about vocabulary associated with the themes. 
From grades 4 to 6, students become more familiar with the sounds of 
Inuktut and demonstrate understanding of familiar, grade-appropriate 
vocabulary through the use of increasingly complex classroom routines, 
games, and activities and direct instruction about vocabulary associated 
with the themes. 

3.16 Introduction
OC.2 Speaking to communicate
Students use expressive language processes in order to be understood by 
others. The progression is connected to the quantity and complexity of 
the vocabulary students will learn.
The Learning Outcomes in this strand focus on the knowledge and 
skills students need to organize their thoughts and make appropriate 
language choices in order to communicate effectively for specific 
purposes and audiences.
OC.3 Interacting with others
Students engage in listening and speaking in Inuktut when interacting 
with others. Students first learn how to respond to greetings and 
simple questions and then begin to participate in conversations. From 
Kindergarten to Grade 3, students focus on learning how to respond 
to greetings and simple questions by repeating and using nonverbal communication and gestures, and familiar, grade-appropriate 
vocabulary. From grades 4 to 6, students begin to participate in short 
conversations using their knowledge of familiar, grade-appropriate 
vocabulary, grammar, phrases, and sentences.
Reading
Reading involves making meaning from text. Just as in their first language, 
students move through a series of developmental stages as they learn to 
read in Inuktut as a second language. Most of the reading skills students 
have learned in their first language can be applied to reading in Inuktut. 
Students need to develop basic vocabulary and a strong understanding 
of Inuktut Roman orthography phonics before they are ready to begin 
reading Inuktut text.

Inuktitut 2nd Language Curriculum 3.17
The Learning Concepts in the Reading Learning Strand of the IL2 are:
R.1 Comprehension 
Students develop the processes they need to interact with and make 
meaning for Inuktut texts read out loud and independently. The progression is connected to the increasingly sophisticated strategies students use to understand texts, as well as the difficulty of the texts 
themselves. From Kindergarten to Grade 3, students rely heavily on 
visuals to support their understanding of Inuktut texts read out loud. 
From grades 4 to 6, students increasingly use their knowledge of vocabulary and grammar to understand Inuktut texts read out loud and 
simple texts read independently. Students may also begin to use reading 
comprehension strategies such as visualizing, making connections, and 
predicting. 
R.2 Fluency 
Students develop the ability to read continuous text with accuracy, good 
momentum and phrasing, and appropriate pausing, intonation, and 
stress. Fluency supports comprehension and is an essential element of 
both reading out loud and reading silently. The Learning Outcomes in 
this Learning Concept are similar from Kindergarten to Grade 6. The 
progression is connected to how fluency applies to grade-level texts.
R.3 Text forms and purposes 
Students learn how the elements of text forms (or types of texts) 
determine the ways readers interact with them. From Kindergarten to 
Grade 3, students are introduced to different literary and factual text forms 
in Inuktut. Literary text forms include narratives (such as traditional 
stories and picture books). Factual text forms include information books, 
procedural texts (such as lists, routine instructions, recipes, science 
experiments, and classroom rules), and explanations. In grades 4 to 6, 
students begin to think about how text form is connected to the different 
purposes for reading, such as reading to be entertained, to locate specific 
information, or to expand vocabulary. Learning about the different 
purposes for reading and the text forms that serve those purposes is an 
important part of becoming a skilled reader in any language.

3.18 Introduction
Writing
Writing is a way to communicate and learn. When learning to write in 
Inuktut, students can apply their skills and what they have learned about 
writing as a process in their first language. Just as with reading in Inuktut, 
students need to develop basic vocabulary and a strong understanding 
of Inuktut Roman orthography phonics before they are ready to begin 
writing in Inuktut independently. As students develop vocabulary skills 
and a basic understanding of Inuktut grammar and spelling rules, they 
will begin to be able to communicate their original thoughts and ideas in 
Inuktut in writing. 
The Learning Concepts in the Writing Learning Strand of the IL2 are:
W.1 Purpose, form, and audience 
Students learn to write in Inuktut for different purposes. The Learning 
Outcomes in this Learning Concept do not change from grades 4 to 6, 
but the purposes, texts, writing, vocabulary, and grammar increase in 
complexity.
W.2 Writing process
Students develop the ability to create and produce a finished text. To 
ensure that the IL2 prioritizes oral language development, only grades 3 
to 6 have Learning Outcomes for this Learning Concept.
Language and Culture Appreciation
Learning Inuktut will lead to a greater understanding and appreciation 
of Inuit worldviews. Students will become familiar with the writing and 
sound systems of Inuktut and develop an appreciation of language and 
culture. Their learning will help them link language to social and cultural 
contexts and empower them to develop cultural competence by exploring 
the Inuit worldview associated with language and culture. It will develop 
their motivation and ability to acknowledge their own identity, selfknowledge, attitude, and social skills, and to understand the way people 
speak, think, and behave. This learning will help students develop an 
exploratory and reflective approach to culture and language and realize 
that culture and language are inseparable. In addition, students will 
appreciate and enjoy the visual arts, songs, and dances.

Inuktitut 2nd Language Curriculum 3.19
The Language and Culture Appreciation Learning Strand focuses on the 
use of four different types of resources: traditional stories, songs, chants, 
and games. These resources promote the use of vocabulary, correct 
pronunciation, and proper grammar while immersing students in the 
culture of the language in a fun and memorable way.
The Learning Concept in the Language and Culture Appreciation Learning 
Strand of the IL2 is:
LCA.1 Appreciating traditional stories, songs, chants, and games
Students learn about traditional stories, songs, chants, and games and 
gain an appreciation of the connection between language and culture. 
The Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept are the same for 
Kindergarten to Grade 6. The progression is connected to the length, 
complexity, and subject matter of the traditional stories, songs, chants, 
and games. Many of the traditional stories, songs, chants, and games 
can be used in more than one grade level.

GLOSSARY 
The following definitions and examples are intended to help teachers use 
this document. The examples provided are suggestions and are not meant to 
be exhaustive. 
audience 
The readers for whom a piece of writing is intended.
comprehension strategies
The strategies students use before, during, and after reading to make 
meaning from text.
• Making connections to familiar texts from prior knowledge and 
experience to familiar texts
• Visualizing to clarify or deepen understanding of a text
• Finding important ideas 
• Asking questions
• Summarizing information
• Inferring 
• Analyzing and synthesizing
• Skimming text for information or detail 
• Scanning text to determine the purpose or type of material 
• Adjusting reading speed according to the level of difficulty of the 
text or the kind of reading (for example, a simple story or more 
complicated factual text)
decoding 
Using knowledge of the relationships between sounds and letters to read 
words.

5.2 Glossary
draft
A first attempt at writing thoughts and ideas down in an organized way. 
Some writing tasks involve writing more than one draft, before creating a 
final draft.
echo reading 
An activity where the teacher reads a sentence slowly and the students 
repeat, or echo, it back.
editing 
Improving the meaning of individual sentences. Adding, removing, and 
changing word chunks (morphemes) or words are examples of editing.
familiar texts 
Texts with words and phrases students have been exposed to over 
multiple readings.
familiar vocabulary 
Language that students have regularly encountered in their everyday 
school learning (for example, daily routines).
fluency
The ability to read continuous text with accuracy, good momentum and 
phrasing, and appropriate pausing, intonation, and stress.
grade-appropriate vocabulary 
Basic words, phrases, and expressions related to the themes and 
daily routines. See the IL2 Educator Handbook for grade-appropriate 
vocabulary lists. 
informational texts 
Texts written for the purpose of informing a reader about a topic. 

Inuktitut 2nd Language Curriculum 5.3
knowledge holders 
Members of the school and larger community, including parents and 
family, Elders, skilled artisans, storytellers, and other school staff.
limited support 
Support that includes occasional additional visual cues, reminders, nonverbal communication, and gestures. 
morphemes
The smallest pieces of language that have meaning. Also called word 
chunks.
non-verbal communication and gestures 
Actions such as raising one’s eyebrows, nodding, scrunching one’s nose, or 
shaking one’s head.
phrase
The verb or noun part of a sentence (for example, ᐃᓄᖕᒧᑦ/inungmut [to 
a person]).
planning 
One of the steps in the writing process. It involves students organizing 
their thoughts about what they are going to write. This may include 
brainstorming ideas, deciding what to write first, second, and so on, and 
considering the purpose and audience.
publishing
Sharing the final revised, edited, and proofread piece of writing with 
others. Sharing a piece of writing can be done by reading the writing out 
loud, by letting someone else read the writing, or by doing both.
reading purpose
The objective a reader is trying to achieve by reading a particular piece of 
writing.

5.4 Glossary
reference tools 
Resources used to help locate information, such as word walls, posters, 
books, visual dictionaries, etc. 
revising 
Making changes to the ideas and organization of the writing as a whole. 
Adding, removing, and rearranging sentences are examples of revising.
Roman orthography
The Latin alphabet used to write Inuktut.
sentence
A sentence is a verb phrase and a noun phrase that together convey a 
complete thought. 
shared reading 
An instructional tool that involves the teacher and students reading a 
text together. 
short sentence 
A verb phrase with a verb root and a verb ending (for example, 
ᐊᓂᔪᖅ/anijuq [he or she exits]) and a simple noun phrase that 
includes a noun root and a preposition or article (for example, ᐃᒡᓗᒥ/
iglumi). 
simple stories 
Predictable stories with minimal text, highly supportive visuals, and 
familiar traditional stories.
simple texts
Texts that have very supportive visuals.
simple words 
Short-vowel, two- to three-syllable words that do not have double 
consonants or consonant clusters.

Inuktitut 2nd Language Curriculum 5.5
text form 
A kind of text that is characterized by particular elements. Traditional 
stories, labels, and recipes are examples of different text forms.
word chunks
The smallest pieces of language that have meaning. Also called 
morphemes.
word wall 
A collection of important word chunks (morphemes) and words that is 
displayed in the classroom. Students can refer to the word wall when 
writing independently. 
writing purpose 
The objective a writer is trying to achieve with a particular piece of 
writing.
