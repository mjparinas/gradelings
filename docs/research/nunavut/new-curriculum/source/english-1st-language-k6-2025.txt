NALUNAIQSIJJUT
PREFACE
The English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum is a cornerstone of 
Nunavut’s educational framework. It is rooted in Nunavut's cultural heritage 
and designed to shape informed and empowered citizens who are prepared 
for the challenges of the modern world. Guided by foundational documents 
such as the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for Nunavut 
Curriculum and Inuuqatigiit: The Curriculum from the Inuit Perspective, 
the English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum adopts a holistic and 
inclusive approach to teaching and serves as a bridge between traditional 
Inuit wisdom and contemporary education. 
Language development is vital to students’ intellectual, social, and emotional 
well-being. The English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum provides 
students with the essential tools and skills they need to share ideas, 
demonstrate their learning, and explore the English language as a source of 
enjoyment and self-discovery. 
In EL1 classes, students learn to express themselves, appreciate the richness 
of language and storytelling, and participate meaningfully in traditional 
Inuit and modern dialogues and texts. They develop critical and creative 
thinking skills, explore and strengthen their Inuit identity, and learn to value 
and respect diverse perspectives. 
Inuit groups traditionally expressed themselves through oral narratives. 
While these narratives varied from group to group, Inuit are unified by 
storytelling. English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum is informed by 
Inuit experiences and promotes Inuit ways of knowing and doing. 
Today it is more important than ever to be grounded in Inuit ways of 
knowing and learning and to be able to communicate and work with people 
from other cultures. English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum
encourages Nunavut students to develop global perspectives in an 
increasingly connected world while upholding Inuit identity and culture. 
2.1 Preface

English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum 2.2
The English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum integrates communitybased storytelling and collaborative learning to foster a deeper understanding 
of language and its impact on identity, culture, and community. Students 
gain a greater sense of themselves and the world by reading and reflecting 
on various texts, listening to stories from the local and global community, 
and building relationships and understanding with peers and community 
members. To reflect the significance of storytelling in Inuit culture, oral and 
written traditions are equally emphasized.
Language skills are necessary to understand others, discover new interests, 
and share ideas and learning. The English Language Arts - 1st Language 
Curriculum strives to make literacy accessible for all students by focusing 
on inquiry-based learning and authentic engagement in everyday contexts. 
Students learn to understand their experiences, advocate for themselves and 
others, and engage meaningfully with the world around them.
As they progress through the curriculum, students learn to articulate their 
feelings and ideas and support them with reasoning and research. They 
develop important skills such as clear communication, thoughtful analysis, 
and creative expression, which improve their learning across all subjects and 
prepare them for higher education and success in future careers.
By reflecting Inuit heritage and Elders’ wisdom, the English Language Arts - 
1
st Language Curriculum supports long-term cultural preservation, enhances 
community engagement, and advances ongoing reconciliation efforts. 
Partnerships with Elders, community members, and families contribute to a 
dynamic and evolving literacy education that promotes a safe and supportive 
environment. With the English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum, 
students can discover the transformative power of words and develop the 
confidence they need to shape their future while honouring their past.

As stated in the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for Nunavut 
Curriculum, “schooling in Nunavut should provide support to students in all 
areas of their development so that they can achieve personal goals, become 
well-equipped to contribute and serve their families and communities, 
demonstrate leadership and healthy attitudes, and be able to actively 
participate and contribute as Nunavut takes on new roles in the global 
community.”¹ To achieve this vision for education in Nunavut, it is imperative 
that the curriculum in all subject areas promotes holistic, critical thinking 
and encourages continuous learning that can be transferred across contexts. 
It must also promote inclusion and provide opportunities to differentiate 
learning experiences to meet the needs of all students. The updated Nunavut 
Curriculum Framework will provide a common structure for the curriculum 
in each subject area and support this holistic, cross-curricular approach to 
teaching and learning.

The Curriculum Foundations—Nunavusiutit, Iqqaqqaukkaringniq, 
Uqausiliriniq, and Aulajaaqtut—are at the centre of the curriculum in each 
subject area. According to the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework, 
the foundations “are generic in nature, transcend subject-specific content 
areas, and result in the application and transfer of specific learning to new 
contexts and situations. This approach to learning is designed to focus on the 
development of complex intellectual (metacognitive) skills and lead students 
to transformational ways of thinking and processing. This type of approach 
helps students understand the connections between various learnings and 
the strategies that lead to successful application of learning in new contexts.”² 
Within the Curriculum Foundations are the subject areas. These are areas 
of learning organized as discrete learning disciplines and taught in systemic 
ways over time.

English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum 3.3
The aims and principles of the Curriculum Foundations inform the Major 
Understandings in each subject area. Major Understandings are broad statements 
that reflect the essential principles and core ideas within a subject area that can 
be transferred across different areas of learning.³ These Major Understandings 
anchor the curriculum in the purpose for learning in each subject area and 
the values of Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ). They act as a lens through which 
teachers can make decisions about planning, instruction, and assessment. 
The Major Understandings are explored through the Learning Strands, which 
represent the key concepts and processes within each subject area and the 
relationships among them. The Learning Strands remain consistent across grades 
and will encourage a richer exploration of how these concepts and processes 
work together over time. Within each Learning Strand, there are Learning 
Concepts and grade-specific Learning Outcomes. The Learning Concepts outline 
the constructs, processes, and patterns within each Learning Strand. These 
concepts provide a structure through which students can organize and connect 
the Learning Outcomes. Learning Outcomes define the key knowledge, skills, 
and understandings students will develop at each grade level. These become 
the building blocks that support deeper conceptual understanding.
In addition, each grade level has Guiding Questions. The Guiding Questions 
will help link the Learning Concepts and the Learning Outcomes back to 
the Curriculum Foundations. By building connections among the knowledge, 
skills, and understandings they have acquired, students will be able to explore 
the principles of the Curriculum Foundations in age-appropriate ways.
Teachers can use the Guiding Questions:
• In daily classroom activities and discussions
• As part of assessment tasks
• To plan units or themes
• To inspire inquiry-based projects

INTRODUCTION TO THE ENGLISH LANGUAGE 
ARTS - 1ST LANGUAGE CURRICULUM (EL1)
Language and literacy are tools for making meaning of the world around us. 
According to Ilitaqsiniq (previously the Nunavut Literacy Council), “literacy 
is a skill that enables people to interpret and effectively respond to the world 
around them... It includes the ability to learn, communicate, read and write, 
pass on knowledge and participate actively in society.”4
 The ability to think, 
learn, and participate in languages and literacies opens pathways to building 
relationships and developing a shared understanding with others. The 
English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum (EL1) provides a framework 
for flexible language and literacy learning in order for students to be active 
participants in local and global communities, through multiple ways of 
knowing and multiple modes of making and communicating meaning.
Rationale
The Nunavut Kindergarten to Grade 6 EL1 is designed to help students 
develop the language and literacy skills, processes, and understandings 
needed to become effective communicators, develop and express their own 
ideas, and think deeply about the ideas and perspectives of others. Through 
this curriculum, students engage in ongoing language and literacy learning 
that encourages them to make connections and expand their abilities in new 
contexts and experiences. The EL1 is designed to be used with students whose 
first language is English and who have acquired a level of fluency in English. 
—
Notes
4. Nunavut Literacy Council, Unipkausivut: Building Language and Literacy Skills through 
Oral History (Government of Nunavut: 2004), 35.

English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum 3.7
Goals
The goal of the EL1 is to provide Nunavut students with accessible, flexible, 
and supportive language and literacy learning experiences in English. 
Students will develop their ability to communicate effectively with others and 
build essential literacy skills. As students progress through the grades, they 
will develop the skills, processes, and understandings necessary for making 
meaning across multiple learning contexts. 
The EL1 aims for students to:
• Use language to effectively communicate, collaborate, and build 
consensus with others
• Understand and respect the perspectives, ideas, and opinions of others 
• Engage with a variety of texts in purposeful ways through listening, 
speaking, reading, writing, viewing, and representing 
• Think critically and creatively about a variety of texts to construct 
and communicate personal meaning
• Transfer language and literacy skills to a variety of contexts and 
situations in adaptive and innovative ways
• Advocate for themselves and their communities in meaningful ways 
• Reflect on their learning process to create and achieve learning goals 
• Celebrate and appreciate the impact, influence, and artistry of Inuit 
and other Indigenous texts and worldviews

3.10 Introduction
Curriculum Foundation
The EL1 is part of the Uqausiliriniq Curriculum Foundation. The Uqausiliriniq 
Curriculum Foundation focuses on relationships in communication and 
language development, creative and artistic expression, and reflective and 
critical thinking. The principles of the Uqausiliriniq Curriculum Foundation 
include building relationships, respecting others, thinking creatively and 
seeking to understand, and improving on the present and building strength 
for the future.
Major Understandings
The principles of Uqausiliriniq inform the three Major Understandings 
within the EL1 that continue from Kindergarten to Grade 6. These Major 
Understandings reflect the essential learning within the subject area and can 
act as a lens through which teachers can make decisions about planning, 
instruction, and assessment. The Major Understandings for the EL1 are: 
1. Effective communication helps us to build relationships and come to 
common understandings based on mutual respect and empathy. 
2. Strong language skills provide the foundation for creative and critical 
thinking, innovation, self-advocacy, and the ability to seek effective 
solutions in all aspects of our lives. 
3. Creating texts allows us to share ideas, experiences, and ways of knowing 
with the wider community. 

English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum 3.11
Learning Strands and Learning Concepts 
The EL1 has been organized by grade and then further broken down into 
three Learning Strands: Oral Communication, Reading, and Writing. The 
grade-specific Learning Outcomes within each Learning Strand have been 
organized according to their Learning Concept so that there is a clear 
progression of student learning from Kindergarten to Grade 6.
Oral Communication 
Oral communication includes listening and speaking skills, which are 
essential for thinking, learning, and developing social skills. Listening 
and speaking enable students to explore ideas and concepts, as well 
as understand and organize their experiences and knowledge. Oral 
communication skills continue to be crucial for sharing oral history, 
traditional knowledge, and ways of knowing in communities across 
Nunavut. Students learn how to communicate thoughts, feelings, 
experiences, information, and opinions as well as learn to understand 
themselves and others. Students will explore oral stories that are important 
to their personal, family, cultural, and community identity as well as reflect 
on the perspectives and worldviews around them. 
The EL1 emphasizes English as the language of instruction. However, it is 
important to create an inclusive environment where all languages and 
dialects are respected and valued. Communicating openly to everyone 
demonstrates respect and sensitivity, the IQ principle of Tunnganarniq 
(fostering good spirits by being open, welcoming, and inclusive), which 
promotes socialization in an inclusive way. 

3.12 Introduction
The Learning Concepts in the Oral Communication Learning Strand of the 
EL1 are:
OC.1 Listening to understand
The Learning Outcomes in this concept focus on active listening and 
observational skills, or purposeful listening, to engage physically and 
mentally with speakers and oral texts. Students learn to follow multistep directions to complete tasks and solve problems as well as develop 
strategies to listen effectively in a variety of social and academic 
situations. 
OC.2 Speaking to communicate
Students use expressive language processes in order to be understood by 
others. The Learning Outcomes in this concept focus on the knowledge 
and skills students need to organize their thoughts and make 
appropriate language choices to communicate effectively for specific 
purposes and audiences.
OC.3 Interacting with others 
Students engage in listening and speaking when interacting with 
others. The Learning Outcomes in this concept focus on promoting 
the IQ Principles of Tunnganarniq, inclusive and open ways of 
communication, and Inuuqatigiitsiarniq, which encompasses the 
understanding, cooperation, and mutual caring that build relationships 
between people. Students learn to interact with others by sharing their 
thoughts, feelings, and opinions. As they progress through the grades, 
students build on their communication skills in order to collaborate 
with others, build consensus, and seek an understanding of perspectives 
and opinions that are different from their own. 

English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum 3.13
Reading
Reading involves making meaning from texts. This strand will support 
students as they develop the knowledge, skills, and understandings 
necessary to read and make meaning from texts. Students will acquire 
lifelong habits and attitudes toward reading. Students will demonstrate the 
IQ principle of Pilimmaksarniq, which involves the development of skills 
through observation, practise, and effort, as they develop their reading skills 
through continued practise. This strand encourages the use of various texts 
with themes and topics related to Nunavut and the IQ Principles and that 
represent multiple perspectives. 
The Learning Outcomes in this strand not only focus on print but also 
emphasize ideas, knowledge, experiences, and perspectives represented 
through digital, visual, audio, and physical texts. Reading are active 
processes that require students to make meaning, evaluate sources, and 
develop personal connections to texts. 
The Learning Concepts in the Reading Learning Strand are:
R.1 Comprehension 
Comprehension refers to processes students need to interact with and 
make meaning of texts. The Learning Outcomes in this concept focus 
on helping students develop comprehension strategies and respond 
creatively and critically to texts. As students progress through the 
grades, they learn to identify points of view within texts and ways that 
the author may influence the reader. Students will explore how they 
relate to characters and events in stories and consider why authors 
make specific choices when creating texts. Students will develop and 
apply research skills to use and evaluate sources, locate information, 
and answer self-generated questions. 
To support their reading, students first develop and learn to apply comprehension strategies and then learn to select appropriate strategies as 
they read. Please refer to the EL1 Educator Handbook for more information about reading comprehension strategies.

3.14 Introduction
R.2 Fluency
Fluency involves the ability to read smoothly, accurately, and with 
appropriate expression. It supports comprehension and is an essential 
element of both reading out loud and silently. The Learning Outcomes 
in this concept focus on developing the knowledge, skills, and 
understandings to match print to oral language, so that students’ 
reading processes become more automatic, efficient, and focused on 
meaning-making. 
R.3 Text forms and purposes
Stories, information books, movies, posters, websites, maps, and 
demonstrations of traditional knowledge are examples of text forms. 
In this concept, students will come to an understanding that text 
forms are connected to the different purposes for reading, such as to be 
entertained, to learn about something new, or to understand a different 
perspective. Learning to make informed decisions about how to select 
and use texts for various purposes helps students become capable and 
independent readers and viewers. 
Writing
Writing helps us to communicate ideas and information for different 
purposes and audiences using different forms. This strand focuses 
on students developing the knowledge, skills, and understandings 
to effectively communicate their thoughts, ideas, and opinions with 
others. This strand will build on the concepts and processes within oral 
communication and reading. Writing should be connected to listening 
and speaking experiences that reinforce and extend students’ knowledge 
of themes, topics, language structures, and vocabulary. In this strand, 
students will also learn to write or create their own texts for various 
purposes and audiences. Students will develop an awareness of the 
mechanics of writing and learn to use the conventions that are essential 
to deliver an effective piece of writing. 

English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum 3.15
The Learning Concepts in the Writing Learning Strand are:
W.1 Purpose, form, and audience
The Learning Outcomes in this concept focus on developing the knowledge, skills, and understandings students need to create meaningful 
texts for a variety of purposes and audiences. Students will use critical 
and creative thinking to make informed decisions about how to create and organize their texts. As students develop their skills over time, 
they will explore creative use of language to develop their own voice in 
writing. 
W.2 Writing process
An important part of learning how to create texts is understanding 
that writing is a processe. Students will step into the role of writer and 
text creator and engage in the steps of the writing process in order 
to produce finished texts. Texts may include written or performed 
narratives, reports, poems, storyboards, demonstrations, and 
multimedia forms. Through the Learning Outcomes in this concept, 
students will plan, draft, revise, edit, and publish their own texts with 
increasing independence through each grade. 
Please refer to the EL1 Educator Handbook for more information 
about each step in the writing process.
W.3 Conventions 
Students will learn to use the elements and mechanics of writing 
(such as spelling, grammar, punctuation, and word choice) to write 
and represent their ideas for others clearly and coherently. As students 
progress through the grades, they will be able to write at increasing 
levels of complexity.

ENGLISH LANGUAGE ARTS CONSIDERATIONS
Supporting students’ success in language and literacy learning requires teachers 
to carefully plan interactive opportunities that promote purposeful and 
meaningful learning experiences. Teachers can help students make connections 
to languages and literacies that are important to their daily lives as well 
as draw from their prior knowledge and experiences to expand and enrich 
students’ learning. 
This section provides essential information to inform planning and 
instruction in the EL1 classroom. Please refer to the EL1 Educator Handbook 
for further support and resources. 
Creating an inclusive learning environment 
Inuglugijaittuq: Foundation for Inclusive Education in Nunavut Schools notes 
that the way to start building an inclusive classroom is to nurture each 
student’s sense of belonging in the daily life of the classroom. Teachers in 
the EL1 classroom can foster this sense of belonging by presenting frequent 
opportunities for students to:
• Communicate their needs and expectations of the class, the theme, 
the lesson, etc.
• Use self-expression to share with others about themselves, their 
interests, and their personal experiences
• Be acknowledged for who they are as individuals and what they need

3.20 Introduction
The EL1 is designed to help students develop their abilities to solve problems, 
make group decisions, respect differences, and contribute to collective goals. 
Teachers can provide opportunities for various groupings so that students 
can begin to share leadership roles to complete tasks and group projects. 
This helps to build a classroom community of collaborative learning over 
time. Ensuring that IQ Principles are at the core of all learning experiences 
will lead to transformative ways of interacting, respecting, supporting, and 
valuing each other.6
When an inclusive learning environment exists, students can explore new 
ways of thinking as they learn from others and take risks in the learning 
process. Establishing and maintaining an inclusive classroom is a key part of 
motivating and engaging students in language and literacy learning.
Critical literacy 
Critical literacy extends students’ learning beyond reading and writing skills. 
It builds agency within students as they become active participants, creators, 
and critical consumers of the world around them. It is crucial that students 
can assess and analyze the texts around them as they are increasingly 
exposed to media and information in their everyday lives. Students learn 
that they have the power to decide what texts and messages they engage 
with and how and when they engage with them. Applying a critical literacy 
lens to the EL1 classroom supports students as thoughtful decision-makers 
and flexible thinkers in languages and literacies, and it encourages them to 
find, join, and create spaces with others who have shared interests.7

In the EL1 classroom, a critical literacy lens can be applied to interactions 
and engagement with all text forms in grade-appropriate ways across 
Kindergarten to Grade 6. In Critical Literacy: Enhancing Students' 
Comprehension of Text, authors McLaughlin and DeVoogd identify the 
following principles of critical literacy:
• Challenging common assumptions and values
• Exploring multiple perspectives and recognizing those that are absent 
or silenced
• Examining relationships, particularly those involving differences in power
• Reflecting on and using literacy practises to take action for social justice8
In the EL1 classroom, students can apply a critical literacy lens by examining 
and questioning information from various sources, exploring multiple 
perspectives, advocating for perspectives that are not represented, recognizing 
bias and stereotyping, and analyzing the message(s) of a text and how the 
text creator can influence or persuade the audience. Students will contribute 
to their communities through languages and literacies that reflect their 
identities and enable them to advocate for themselves, their communities, 
and the environment.
While teachers apply a critical literacy lens to their lessons, it is important 
that they provide a safe space for their students and acknowledge that 
some issues and discussions can be sensitive for students. Ataqqigusungniq 
(sensitivity and respect) is imperative for discussions within critical literacy 
as students explore issues surrounding bias, stereotyping, and power 
imbalances.

3.22 Introduction
An important consideration for non-Inuit teachers is that they examine their 
own personal worldviews that may differ from Inuit ways of learning and 
teaching. Non-Inuit teachers must honour the role they play in reconciliation 
on a daily basis in their communities and ensure that their classrooms 
are spaces that amplify Inuit voices, perspectives, and ways of knowing. 
Consulting with their school team and local knowledge holders helps to 
best inform culturally responsive teaching practises. It is also important 
to celebrate the strengths and traditions of the community. Community 
knowledge holders should be invited into the classroom whenever possible to 
share their knowledge and perspectives. 
Refer to the EL1 Educator Handbook for suggestions for and examples of how 
to apply a critical literacy lens to learning experiences across Kindergarten to 
Grade 6. 
Using authentic texts 
Authentic texts are texts that are not specifically created for language 
learning purposes. In the EL1 classroom, students should have frequent 
opportunities to interact with a range of authentic texts that are from 
local and global communities. Texts should be relevant, current, credible, 
and representative of multiple perspectives and worldviews, including Inuit 
and Indigenous ways of knowing. Students should be able to access texts for 
different purposes and be provided with appropriate support when accessing 
and constructing meaning from increasingly complex texts. Engaging with 
authentic texts and learning how to use them is an introduction to critical 
thinking. Some examples of authentic texts include:
• Visual texts (for example, illustrations, prints, wall hangings, 
photographs, and wordless books)
• Printed texts (for example, local newspapers, menus, flyers, maps, 
books, signs, and labels)
• Digital texts (for example, websites, TV shows, movies, radio, and social 
media)
• Physical texts (for example, the land, structures, carvings, and 
demonstrations of traditional knowledge)

PERSPECTIVE 
According to the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for 
Nunavut Curriculum, the four Curriculum Foundations—Nunavusiutit, 
Iqqaqqaukkaringniq, Uqausiliriniq, and Aulajaaqtut—were designed to promote 
a holistic approach to education that encourages students to transfer learning 
across subject areas and develop deeper, transformative ways of thinking and 
learning. Planning learning experiences around thematic units or integrated 
projects can provide students with opportunities to apply their learning in 
different contexts. 
Language Arts provides rich opportunities for students to transfer their 
language and literacy skills across all other subject areas. This supports 
flexible thinking that can help students develop understandings that are at 
the core of a particular topic, idea, subject area, or context. Within the EL1, 
there are many ways for students to build on prior knowledge and connect to 
their learning in other subject areas. 
Students will engage with a variety of texts and respond to them through 
creative expression, including dramatic and visual arts. This will create 
opportunities for students to make connections to Arts Education. As 
students progress through the grades, they will engage in aspects of the 
inquiry process and use research skills to make presentations that span 
familiar topics and themes. This will create opportunities for direct 
connections to be made to Social Studies. These are just a few examples of 
the ways in which the EL1 is designed to create space for students to not only 
connect what they are learning to their lives outside the classroom, but also 
broaden and deepen their skills and understandings by transferring them 
across subject areas and new contexts.
Refer to the EL1 Educator Handbook for suggestions for and examples of how 
to meaningfully integrate this curriculum with learning experiences in 
other subject areas.

USE OF INFORMATION AND

used to engage and enrich student learning in the EL1 classroom. ICT tools

develop literacies, and interact with each other.

experiences with others. Pictures and recordings of community events and 
traditional knowledge can be highlighted by narrative dialogue, written text, 
audio recordings, and animation. Digital storytelling is an example of how 
ICT can provide opportunities for students to share stories through creative 
self-representation while promoting language and literacy skills.9
 Projects and 
tasks that draw on students’ creativity and innovation can showcase student 
learning and reveal the function, adaptability, and vibrancy of personal, 
community, and cultural identity.

integrate ICT into the EL1 classroom.

GLOSSARY 
The following definitions and examples are intended to help teachers use 
this document. The examples provided are suggestions and are not meant to 
be exhaustive.
adjective
A word that describes or modifies a noun (for example, smooth, bumpy, 
fast, short, sweet). 
adverb
A word that describes or modifies a verb (for example, loudly, quickly, 
slowly).
audience
The intended readers, listeners, or viewers of a particular text. 
basic punctuation
The punctuation used to end sentences (for example, periods, 
exclamation marks, and question marks). 
character
A real or imaginary person that inhabits a literary work. Literary 
characters may be major or minor and static (unchanging) or dynamic 
(capable of change). 
complete sentence
A sentence that expresses a complete idea that makes sense on its own. Also 
called a well-formed sentence. 

5.2 Glossary
complex punctuation 
Punctuation that is used in compound and complex sentences. Commas, 
colons, and semicolons are examples of complex punctuation.
complex sentence
A sentence consisting of a main clause and one or more subordinate 
clauses (for example, “The students were tired because they had been 
playing for a long time.”). 
compound sentence
A sentence includes at least two independent clauses and is typically 
joined by a conjunction. 
compound word
A word that is made up of two smaller words (for example, blue-berry, 
news-paper, tooth-brush).
comprehension strategies
A variety of techniques that students use before, during, and after 
listening, reading, and viewing to construct meaning from texts. 
Examples include:
• Making connections from prior knowledge and experience to 
familiar texts
• Visualizing to clarify or deepen understanding of a text
• Finding important ideas
• Asking questions
• Summarizing information 
• Inferring 
• Analyzing and synthesizing
• Predicting
For more information about comprehension strategies, please refer to 
the EL1 Educator Handbook.

English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum 5.3
concepts of print
Concepts related to the way language is conveyed in print. Print concepts 
include directionality (reading from left to right and top to bottom); 
the difference between letters and words; spacing between words and 
sentences; and characteristics of books (for example, title, author, and 
front and back covers). 
conjunctions
A part of speech that connects words, phrases, or sentences (for example, 
like, but, so, because).
CVC words
Words that follow the consonant-vowel-consonant pattern (for example, 
cat, bat, sat). 
decoding
Using knowledge of the relationship between sounds and letters to read 
words. 
decoding strategies 
A variety of techniques students use to read words. Decoding strategies 
include:
• Sounding out
• Using visuals for context clues
• Breaking longer words into parts
• Skimming
For more information about decoding strategies, please refer to the EL1 
Educator Handbook.
digraph
A combination of two letters that represent a single sound (for example, sh, 
ch, ay, ow, ee). 

5.4 Glossary
diphthong
A combination of two vowel sounds that begins as one sound and glide 
into another sound (for example, oi, oy, ay, oa). Some digraphs are also 
diphthongs. 
dramatic play
Dramatic play encourages rich oral language experiences. Teachers can 
introduce new vocabulary and intentionally link the dramatic play to texts 
and learning themes. 
draft
A first attempt at writing thoughts and ideas down in an organized way. 
Some tasks involve creating more than one draft, before creating a final 
draft. 
editing 
Improving the meaning of the text. Adding, removing, and changing words 
or details in images are examples of editing. 
environmental print
Print that can be seen in our everyday surroundings, such as signs, logos, 
labels, and packaging. 
familiar context
A setting or situation for communication that is understood by the 
student through repeated experience. 
genre
A category of text that is defined by a specific style, form, or content. 
graphic organizer
A visual framework (such as a Venn diagram, word web, or flow chart) that 
helps students organize, analyze, synthesize, and assess information and 
ideas. 

English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum 5.5
high-frequency words
Words that appear most frequently in printed text. With practise, these 
words are read automatically.
homophone
Words that sound the same but have different spellings and different 
meanings. For example, be/bee, blue/blew, hair/hare. 
homograph
Words that are spelled the same and sounds the same when spoken but 
have different meanings based on the context. For example, the word fly 
in the sentence "birds can fly" and "a fly is buzzing around" have different 
meanings.
knowledge holders
Members of the school and larger community, including parents and 
family, Elders, skilled artisans, storytellers, and other school staff.
language devices
Different ways that language can be used in texts, such as rhyme, 
personification, descriptions, imagery, etc. 
legible writing
Writing that is clear and easy to read by the teacher and others. 
mentor texts
Pieces of writing or texts that model good writing, organization, and 
delivery, which students can use to inform how they create their own 
texts. 
non-verbal communication and gestures
Ways of communicating without the use of verbal language. This can 
include raising one’s eyebrows, nodding, scrunching one’s nose, shaking 
one’s head, and shrugging one’s shoulders. 

5.6 Glossary
onset and rime
The onset is the initial consonant or consonant cluster of a word (for 
example, in the word ship, sh- is the onset). The rime is the vowel or 
vowel and consonants that follow the onset of a word (for example, in the 
word ship, -ip is the rime). 
pace
With respect to oral communication, the speed at which a person 
communicates. Pace also refers to the rate at which a reader can read 
print out loud and silently.
paragraph
A group of sentences that contain related information. When a text has 
more than one paragraph, the paragraphs are visually distinct from one 
another.
personal dictionaries
A support tool that is student made. Students can keep track of unfamiliar 
words that they wish to use in their writing. 
phonemic awareness
The ability to recognize and manipulate individual sounds in spoken 
words. Before students learn to read printed text, they must understand 
that words are made up of speech sounds or phonemes. Phonemes are 
the smallest parts of sound in a spoken word that make a difference in a 
word’s meaning. For example, changing the first phoneme in sat from s
to c changes the word from sat to cat.
For more information about phonemic awareness, please refer to the EL1 
Educator Handbook.

English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum 5.7
phonics 
The systematic and structured teaching of relationships between written 
letters and spoken sounds and how to use these correspondences to 
decode and spell words. Phonics instruction connects with phonemic 
awareness skills and concepts of print as students learn the relationship 
between written letters of the alphabet and the individual sounds of 
spoken language in English. 
phonological awareness
The ability to identify and manipulate larger parts of spoken language, 
such as syllables, onset and rime, rhyming words, and individual sounds 
(phonemes). Phonological awareness supports young learners in building 
strong reading and writing skills in later years. 
For more information about phonological awareness, please refer to the 
EL1 Educator Handbook. 
planning
Often one of the steps in the writing process, planning involves 
organizing one’s thoughts about what is going to be created. This may 
include brainstorming ideas; deciding what to write first, second, and so 
on; and considering the purpose and audience. 
plot
A series of events or incidents in the action of a story, play, film, or novel. 
proofreading
Fixing mistakes in spelling, punctuation, and grammar. 
publishing
Sharing the final revised, edited, and proofread text with others. Ways 
of sharing a text can include reading it out loud, putting it on display, 
presenting it to an audience, or letting others read or view it. 
read-aloud
An instructional approach that involves the teacher reading a text out loud 
to students. 

5.8 Glossary
reading goals
Goals that can be set by the student and teacher around improving and 
strengthening the student’s reading skills. Goals can be based on phonics, 
fluency, comprehension, vocabulary development, or using a range of 
reading strategies. 
reading purpose
The objective a reader is trying to achieve by reading a particular piece of 
writing. 
reading strategies 
Approaches used before, during, and after reading to decode unfamiliar 
words, determine meaning, and increase understanding of a text. 
return sweep 
When the reader comes to the end of a line of text and continues to the 
next line down at the left side again. 
revising
Making changes to the ideas and organization of the text as a whole. 
Adding, removing, and rearranging sentences or images are examples of 
revising. 
sentence stem
An instructional tool that helps students write complete sentences. 
Sentence stems help students learn about the structure of sentences and 
provide cues for them to complete sentences. For example _____ surprised 
me because... I think that... I like to... I agree with ____ because...
sentence structure
The complexity of a sentence and the way it is organized. Common 
sentence structures include simple, compound, and complex sentences. 
sentence type
Statements, questions, and commands are the main sentence types. A 
statement is used to provide information. A question is used to inquire 
about something or request information. A command directs or gives 
orders. 

English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum 5.9
setting
The time and place of a story, play, film, or novel. 
simple sentence
A sentence that includes at least one noun and one verb. 
spelling patterns
A group of letters that represent a sound. Spelling patterns include 
groups of letters (for example, -at and -ot) as well as digraphs—that is, 
two letters that represent one speech sound (for example, ch-). 
supporting sentences
The sentences that follow the topic sentence in a paragraph that explain, 
expand, illustrate, or prove the main point.
text connections 
A comprehension strategy in which the reader makes connections to the 
text. A text-to-self connection is when a student makes a connection 
between lived experiences and a text (for example, noting a similarity or 
difference between a character and oneself). A text-to-text connection 
is when a student makes a connection between texts they have read or 
viewed (for example, noting similarities in the genre, text structure, or 
topic). A text-to-world connection is when a student makes a connection 
between the text and real-world happenings (for example, noting 
similarities or differences between a current event in the community and 
something that happens or is described in the text). 
text creator
Someone who creates their own texts through writing, drawing, speaking, 
movement, and other forms of representation. 
text features
The physical or design characteristics of a text that clarify and/or 
give support to the meaning in the text (for example, title, headings, 
subheadings, bold and italic fonts, and illustrations). 

5.10 Glossary
text form
A kind of text that is characterized by particular elements. Traditional 
stories, labels, and recipes are examples of different text forms.
text structure
The patterns that are used to organize ideas and information in written 
text. It is important for students to learn to recognize these patterns 
because they will help students with comprehension. 
three cueing systems
Cues that effective readers use in combination to read unfamiliar words 
and sentences and construct meaning from texts. Meaning cues help 
readers predict the meaning of words or sentences using context and 
prior knowledge. Students can ask themselves, Does it make sense?
Structural cues help readers make sense of text using knowledge of 
language patterns and how words are put together in phrases and 
sentences. Students can ask themselves, Does it sound right? Visual cues 
help readers decode unknown words using knowledge of letter-sound 
relationships and spelling patterns. Students can ask themselves, Does it 
look right?
topic sentence
A sentence used at the beginning of a paragraph that is used to express the 
main idea of the paragraph. 
verb tense
Verbs (action words) come in three tenses: past, present, and future. The 
past is used to describe things that have already happened, the present 
describes things that are happening currently or that are continuous, and 
the future describes things that have not happened yet or that will happen.
voice
It is also often referred to as “tone,” “mood,” or “style.” Developing a voice in 
writing and representing involves experimenting with language and text 
structure to create texts that are individual, expressive, and engaging. 

English Language Arts - 1st Language Curriculum 5.11
well-formed sentences
A sentence that expresses a complete idea and makes sense on its own. 
Also called a complete sentence. 
word wall
A list of words prominently displayed in the classroom that teachers use 
to help students become familiar with commonly used words. 
writing path
The path to follow when forming letters in writing. 
writing process
Steps followed in creating and sharing a piece of writing. These steps 
often include planning, drafting, revising, editing, proofreading, and 
publishing. 
writing purpose
The objective an author or text creator is trying to achieve with a 
particular text. Encouraging students to talk about the texts they are 
making helps communicate the idea that writing and other forms of 
representing have a purpose.
