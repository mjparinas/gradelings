PREFACE
The kindergarten to Grade 6 Physical Education, Health, and Wellness 
Curriculum empowers Nunavut students to become active, healthy, and 
resilient members of their communities. Rooted in Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit 
Principles, the curriculum ensures students receive an education that is not 
only academically challenging but also culturally rich and relevant.
Learning in physical health and wellness classes is student-centered, 
experiential, and hands-on. Students participate in new physical challenges 
with peers, develop valuable strategies for regulating their social/emotional 
responses, and recognize the importance of effort and teamwork in 
achievement. By overcoming challenges and learning new skills, students 
build the confidence and resilience they need to handle challenges in all 
areas of their lives.
As they learn and play traditional Inuit games and activities, students 
gain an appreciation for how their ancestors’ physical strength and fitness 
were closely tied to their survival and knowledge of the land. They learn 
that being physically active is not just about exercise but a way of life that 
honours their heritage.
Students learn about traditional Inuit approaches to wellness, such as 
physical health, nutrition, and harmonious relationships with family, 
community, and the environment. They learn about the importance of 
harvesting and preparing traditional foods when they are available and 
understand that healthy living for Inuit means being fit through daily 
activities and eating well when food is abundant.
2.1 Preface

Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum 2.2
The Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum helps students 
develop valuable strategies for regulating social and emotional wellbeing. It draws on Inuit wisdom to teach students how to cope with life's 
challenges, including dealing with loss and grief. Students learn healthy 
ways to support one another and reflect the strong community bonds that 
are central to Inuit culture. They start to identify the traits of a healthy 
relationship so they can make informed decisions about their personal 
connections and understand that they have a right to respectful and 
fulfilling relationships.
The curriculum invites Elders and local experts to play an integral role in 
students’ education. It seeks to create a bridge between generations and 
strengthens the bonds between school teams, parents, Elders, and local 
health authorities to create a supportive network for students' overall 
health. The hope is that healthy habits, responsible decision-making, and 
wellness practices learned at school are reinforced and applied in students' 
daily lives within their families and communities.
The Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum supports students 
in becoming informed, engaged citizens who contribute meaningfully to 
their communities and the wider world. It helps them develop a lasting 
commitment to their physical, mental, and emotional well-being and 
prepares them to navigate life’s challenges with resilience and purpose.

What Is Physical Education, Health, and Wellness?
For many decades, the connection between physical, mental, and SocialEmotional health and well-being was not represented or explored in school 
curricula. Physical Education curricula often focused on physical fitness and 
the development of skills needed to be successful in sports or competitive 
games. Health curricula focused on issues such as hygiene, diet, and sexual 
education; however, mental and Social-Emotional health were often not 
valued or prioritized. Education systems across Canada and around the world 
are beginning to recognize that physical, mental, and Social-Emotional 
health and well-being are interconnected and interdependent and that the 
development of knowledge, skills, and understandings in these areas should 
be approached and nurtured in a holistic way. 
Research from the Qaujigiartiit Health Research Centre states, “The Inuit 
worldview sees the individual as part of an interconnected space, bringing 
together people, land, and spirit.”4
 In order to maintain positive health and 
well-being, students must actively participate in the process of “becoming 
aware of and practising behaviours that contribute to a sense of social, 
spiritual, physical, intellectual, and emotional balance.”5

Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum 3.7
According to Nunaliit Aulajut: Nunavut’s Sport, Physical Activity, and Recreation 
Framework, “in Nunavut, traditional games, cultural activities, sport, 
physical activity, and recreation are part of an active, healthy lifestyle. These 
activities improve physical and mental health, and they strengthen family 
and community bonds. They also encourage the development of skills and 
positive attitudes that help people work together, address challenges, and build 
strong communities.”6
 Providing students with opportunities to engage in 
different sports and activities that are enjoyable and connected to culture and 
community will help them build an appreciation for the role physical activity 
plays in physical as well as mental, emotional, and social health.
Rationale
The physical, mental, social, and emotional health and wellness of students 
are essential for their lifelong learning and development. The Nunavut 
Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum: Kindergarten to 
Grade 6 is designed to help students develop the knowledge, skills, and 
understandings they need to live a healthy and active life. Students will 
discover the physical, mental, social, and emotional benefits of daily physical 
activity and be empowered to make choices that support their own health 
and wellness. The curriculum will address issues and challenges students 
in Nunavut face and nurture the development of their self-concept and 
resilience. Students will develop strategies to recognize and regulate emotions 
and learn to build caring, healthy relationships. Through this curriculum, 
students will cultivate their sense of self and develop an appreciation for 
where they come from and how the land contributes to their health and 
wellness. They will also discover how maintaining their own health and 
wellness can help them support the health and wellness of their family, their 
community, and beyond.

3.12 Introduction
Curriculum Foundation
The Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum is part of the 
Aulajaaqtut Curriculum Foundation. The Aulajaaqtut Curriculum Foundation 
focuses on wellness, safety, and one’s place in society. The principles of 
the Aulajaaqtut Curriculum Foundation include harmony, responsibility, 
discipline, and wisdom.
Major Understandings
The principles of Aulajaaqtut inform the four Major Understandings within 
the Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum that continue from 
Kindergarten to Grade 6. These Major Understandings reflect the essential 
learning within the subject area and can act as a lens through which 
teachers can make decisions about planning, instruction, and assessment. 
The Major Understandings for the Physical Education, Health, and Wellness 
Curriculum are: 
1. Participating in physical activity contributes to positive health and 
wellness.
2. Understanding how our bodies grow and change helps us make healthy 
choices.
3. Understanding who we are helps us live in harmony and balance with 
ourselves, others, and the land.
4. Learning to maintain our own health and wellness enables us to 
contribute to the health and wellness of others. 

Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum 3.13
Learning Strands and Learning Concepts 
The Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum has been organized 
by grade and then further broken down into three Learning Strands: Physical 
Literacy, Healthy Living, and Social-Emotional Wellness. The Learning 
Outcomes within each Learning Strand have been organized according 
to their Learning Concept so that there is a clear progression of student 
learning from Kindergarten to Grade 6. Some Learning Outcomes continue 
across grades to provide students with additional time and support to develop 
essential skills, practises, and understandings related to physical and mental 
health and wellness.
Physical Literacy
The Physical Literacy Learning Strand focuses on developing the 
fundamental movement skills, body awareness, and motivation students 
need to live an active life. This Learning Strand will support students as 
they acquire the competencies and techniques needed to successfully 
engage in a wide variety of physical activities in ways that are enjoyable and 
long-lasting.
PL.1 Physical competence
Students will develop the ability to move efficiently and effectively in a 
variety of contexts. The Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept 
focus on acquiring locomotor, body, and object control skills and 
practising and refining them over time. Students will have opportunities 
to apply their skills in different contexts and environments and to 
develop the correct techniques that will help them succeed at a variety 
of physical activities.
PL.2 Body awareness
Students will develop an understanding of how their bodies can move 
and how movement affects their physical health. They will also explore 
how different environments can impact the way they move. Through 
the Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept, students will learn 
how their bodies respond to physical activity and ways to monitor and 
enhance their participation in different types of activities. 

3.14 Introduction
PL.3 Motivation and participation
Students will develop an understanding of the physical, social, and 
emotional factors that can support lifelong active living. Through the 
Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept, students will discover 
what helps them participate in physical activity and what may cause 
challenges or barriers for themselves and others. They will also develop 
strategies to promote fair play and inclusion for all.
Healthy Living
The Healthy Living Learning Strand explores the knowledge, skills, and 
understandings students need to support and begin to advocate for their 
mental and physical health and safety. Within this Learning Strand, 
students will examine the factors that contribute to healthy living as 
well as the factors that can create barriers. They will also engage in the 
principles of Pijitsirniq, including: supporting their own health and wellness 
and the health and wellness of others; becoming aware of personal, local, 
and global interdependence; and contributing to the common good.
HL.1 Growth and development
Students will develop an understanding of how the body grows and 
changes over time. They will explore basic human needs and examine 
how different factors and stages of development can affect how their 
body grows and changes.
HL.2 Health literacy
Students will acquire the ability to access, understand, and critique 
health information they need to maintain physical and mental 
health and safety. The Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept 
focus on helping students develop an awareness of the resources and 
supports that are available to them, including traditional medicine 
practises. They will learn to discern between reliable and unreliable 
sources and explore ways they can share important health and safety 
information with others.

Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum 3.15
HL.3 Making healthy choices
Students will learn to integrate the knowledge and skills needed to 
make decisions and develop plans to maintain their own mental and 
physical health and safety. They will learn how to set personal healthy 
living goals and develop strategies and techniques to help them work 
toward those goals.
Social-Emotional Wellness
The Social-Emotional Wellness Learning Strand explores how learning 
to regulate and manage emotions, developing a strong sense of self, and 
learning to interact with others in healthy ways benefit students’ overall 
health and wellness. Students will be encouraged to discover and embrace 
who they are, where they come from, and how they can contribute 
to the world around them. They will also engage in the principles 
of Inuuqatigiitsiarniq, including respecting others, building positive 
relationships, and caring for others.
SE.1 Emotional literacy
Students will develop the ability to understand and express their emotions in healthy ways. They will learn to recognize and manage stress 
and build resiliency. They will also learn to recognize and respond to 
the emotions of others, build empathy, and contribute to the emotional wellness of others.
SE.2 Self-concept
Students will work to build a positive self-concept—a strong set of 
beliefs and perceptions about who they are. Through the Learning 
Outcomes in this Learning Concept, students will explore different 
facets of their identity and begin to recognize the harmful impacts 
of bullying, harassment, and discrimination. They will learn 
how resiliency and a sense of belonging can help them overcome 
challenges and maintain their overall health and wellness.

3.16 Introduction
SE.3 Positive relationships
Students will develop an understanding of how caring behaviours look 
and feel and the ability to connect with others in safe and healthy 
ways. The Learning Outcomes in this Learning Concept will explore 
the characteristics of healthy and unhealthy relationships and help 
students identify and take action when they are in uncomfortable or 
unsafe situations. They will learn to recognize their role in creating 
strong relationships and how building meaningful connections can 
support healthy living.

EDUCATION, HEALTH, AND WELLNESS CURRICULUM
Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ), or “that which Inuit have always known to 
be true,” is the foundation for Social-Emotional, spiritual, mental, and 
physical well-being. When learning is embedded in culture, it supports a 
sense of identity, a feeling of belonging, and a connection to those around 
us.7
 Grounding the Nunavut Physical Education, Health, and Wellness 
Curriculum: Kindergarten to Grade 6 in IQ will provide students with 
opportunities to see how cultural health supports overall health and 
well-being. While students and teachers should be engaging with the eight 
IQ Principles holistically, the Physical Education, Health, and Wellness 
Curriculum provides opportunities to focus specifically on:
• Pijitsirniq (serving or providing for family or community): Maintaining 
our own health and well-being gives us the ability, information, and 
motivation to support the health and well-being of our families and 
communities as well. Learning about public health measures, safety 
practises, and strategies for healthy and active living can benefit not 
only ourselves but also those around us.
• Inuuqatigiitsiarniq (showing respect and a caring attitude for others): 
Recognizing the importance of having respect for themselves and 
others and learning how to demonstrate caring behaviours will help 
students build healthy relationships.

CREATING A SAFE LEARNING ENVIRONMENT
The Nunavut Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum: 
Kindergarten to Grade 6 will explore many concepts and topics that 
are essential to the health and wellness of all students. However, before 
engaging students in these conversations, interactions, and experiences, 
educators must ensure that the learning environment is physically and 
emotionally safe and inclusive for all students. To engage students in 
Nunavut in meaningful and transformative learning within the Physical 
Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum, instructional and assessment 
practises must be trauma-informed. Educators need to be responsive to the 
psychological, biological, social, and spiritual needs of students and must 
understand the ways that trauma can influence health and wellness. The 
root of effective teaching and learning within the Physical Education, Health, 
and Wellness Curriculum must be “genuine, authentic and compassionate 
relationships” between educators, students, and families.8
 
To create a safe learning environment, it is important to recognize that 
growth and development, whether physical, social, or emotional, do not 
happen at the same time and pace for all children. Each child has their 
own unique set of strengths and abilities that should be acknowledged and 
valued. It is important that students are encouraged to demonstrate physical 
skills to the best of their abilities and that all students can participate 
equitably in games, sports, and activities. A safe learning environment 
must also reflect all students who participate in it. Students need to feel

Students should be able to express their full identity freely and see themselves

The Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for Nunavut Curriculum

comfortable using the Inuktut names of the body parts, this contribution 
should be acknowledged and respected.

TEACHING FROM A CROSS-CURRICULAR 
PERSPECTIVE
According to the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for 
Nunavut Curriculum, the four Curriculum Foundations—Nunavusiutit, 
Iqqaqqaukkaringniq, Uqausiliriniq, and Aulajaaqtut—were designed to 
promote a holistic approach to education that encourages students to 
transfer learning across subject areas and develop deeper, transformative 
ways of thinking and learning. Planning learning experiences around 
thematic units or integrated projects can provide students with 
opportunities to apply their learning in different contexts. 
Within the Nunavut Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum: 
Kindergarten to Grade 6, there are many ways for students to build on 
prior knowledge and connect their learning in other subject areas. In this 
curriculum, students will explore the human body, how it functions, and 
how it grows and changes over time. They will have opportunities to build 
on what they have learned or are learning in Science. This curriculum also 
integrates Inuit culture, practises, and traditions; explores relationships to 
the land; and examines the impacts of colonization. This will create space 
for students to connect to learning taking place in Social Studies. As students 
learn to build relationships and resolve conflicts in healthy ways through 
this curriculum, they will be able to apply communication skills developed 
within Language Arts. These are only a few examples of ways in which the 
Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum is designed to create 
space for students not only to connect what they are learning to their lives 
outside of the classroom but also to broaden and deepen their skills and 
understandings by transferring them into new settings and contexts. The 
Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Educator Handbook will provide 
more examples and suggestions for how to integrate this curriculum 
meaningfully with learning experiences in other subject areas.

USE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATION 
TECHNOLOGY
The use of information and communication technology is an important 
part of health literacy, and it provides essential tools to help students 
advocate for and maintain their own physical and mental health. Using 
these tools to support the Physical Education, Health, and Wellness 
Curriculum, where available, can help enhance student learning and 
allow them to use the tools in a supervised environment. Students can 
use technology to find health-related information, connect with services, 
or build their social networks. All these applications of technology can 
have a positive impact on students’ health and wellness. However, it is also 
important that students learn to use technology safely and do not put 
themselves and others at risk. They need to learn to differentiate between 
reliable and unreliable sources of information and to create personal 
boundaries to manage their use of technology outside of school. These 
skills are a focus of the Health Literacy and Social-Emotional Wellness 
Learning Strands, and more information and resources related to the use of 
information and communication technology can be found in the Physical 
Education, Health, and Wellness Educator Handbook.

GLOSSARY 
The following definitions and examples are intended to help teachers use 
this document. The examples provided are suggestions and are not meant to 
be exhaustive.
abusive or exploitative behaviours or situations
When a person is being hurt, mistreated, or taken advantage of by 
another person.
accelerate
To increase speed or move faster.
adolescence
The period following the onset of puberty, during which a young person 
develops from a child into an adult.
belonging
The human emotional and psychological need to be connected to and 
accepted by others.
body control skills
Movements involving body position, posture, and coordination, such as 
bending, balancing, stretching, and twisting.
boundaries
The limits a person sets to communicate to themselves or others what is 
safe and comfortable for them. 
bullying
Repeatedly seeking to harm, intimidate, or coerce someone perceived as 
vulnerable or with less power.

5.2 Glossary
chronic illness
A health condition that is persistent and long term. Examples include 
diabetes, asthma, and depression.
communicable diseases
Illnesses that can be transmitted or passed from person to person, such 
as coronavirus, influenza, or tuberculosis.
consent
Giving or getting permission for something to happen.
decelerate
To decrease speed or move slower.
dysregulation
A disruption in the normal functioning of the body, including the 
nervous system.
emotional dysregulation
The inability to manage emotions in healthy ways. Using strategies to 
calm the mind and body can help return to a regulated state.
emotional safety
Feeling comfortable to express one’s thoughts and feelings without fear of 
being rejected.
fine motor skills
Movements that use the small muscles in the hands, wrists, and fingers. 
These include the movements required for eating, writing, drawing, 
getting dressed, and playing with toys. 
gross motor skills
Movements that require the muscles in the arms, legs, and torso. These 
skills are required for daily movements such as walking, running, 
wheeling, jumping, and throwing.

Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum 5.3
harassment
Creating an unpleasant or hostile situation through uninvited and 
unwelcome verbal or physical conduct or conduct online.
harm-reduction strategies
Strategies that focus on helping people make healthy and informed 
choices about substance use.
healthy
Being physically and mentally well. This includes, but is not limited to, 
the absence of illness or ailment.
hygiene practises
Habits or routines for keeping clean to promote good health and prevent 
illness, such as washing hands, sneezing or coughing into one’s elbow, 
and bathing or showering regularly.
identity
The characteristics or qualities that distinguish a person or group. 
Identity can be shaped by factors like race or ethnicity, culture, gender 
and gender expression, sexuality, religion, and social roles. 
locomotor skills
Types of movements that incorporate travelling across a surface, such as 
running, climbing, and sliding.
media
Means of mass communication, including but not limited to books, 
magazines, and newspapers; television; video games; social media 
platforms; and digital news platforms.
medicinal substances
Substances used to treat and prevent illnesses or diseases or to restore or 
support health and wellness (for example, vaccines, antibiotics, insulin, 
and prescription medications).

5.4 Glossary
needs
Things a person requires to survive, such as food, water, shelter, and sleep.
non-communicable diseases
Health conditions that cannot be transmitted or passed from person to 
person, such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, or diabetes.
non-medicinal substances
Substances that could be harmful to the mind and body. These can 
include poisons, psychoactive substances, and the non-medical use of 
prescription drugs.
nutrients
Substances or ingredients that promote growth, provide energy, and 
maintain life.
object control skills
Movement skills involving the control of objects, primarily with the 
hands and feet. They may also involve implements such as racquets or 
bats.
peer pressure
Influence from members of one’s peer group.
personal space
The physical area immediately surrounding someone. Moving into 
someone’s personal space without their consent may cause that person 
to feel uncomfortable or unsafe. Understanding personal space helps 
students interact safely and respectfully with others.
physical safety
Being free of the risk of harm or injury to the body.
pivoting
The action of stepping with one foot while keeping the second foot at its 
point of contact on the floor.

Physical Education, Health, and Wellness Curriculum 5.5
puberty
A period of rapid growth and development and physical, emotional, 
and social change that typically begins between 10 and 12 years of age. 
However, the timing of this stage of development can vary widely among 
children. 
reliable health information
Health information that comes from trustworthy institutions, 
organizations, or health care professionals and is based in facts and 
evidence (for example, information from doctors, traditional medicine 
practitioners, and Government of Nunavut Department of Health 
websites).
resilience
The capacity to manage and recover efficiently from challenges or 
difficulties.
self-advocacy
The ability to speak up for or assert one’s needs or what is in one’s best 
interest.
sexual identity
How a person expresses themselves sexually or who they are sexually or 
romantically attracted to.
sexuality
A person’s capacity for sexual thoughts, feelings, and behaviours. 
shifting
The action of changing place, position, or direction.
side-stepping
The action of stepping to the side to avoid a person or object.

5.6 Glossary
stereotype
A generalized or oversimplified belief or idea about a particular type of 
person or group of people.
stress
Physical, mental, or emotional strain or tension. Stress is how the body 
responds to challenges or demands. An example of good stress may be 
what a person feels before competing in a game or performing in front 
of an audience. This type of stress can be exciting or motivating. An 
example of bad stress can occur as a result of a conflict or if a person is 
feeling overwhelmed or unsafe.
stressor
A stimulus that causes stress.
unreliable health information
Health information that may contain statements that are out of date 
or factually incorrect, may include information that has been altered or 
changed, or may be based in a particular bias or opinion (for example, 
posts from individual social media accounts and websites without a clear 
author or source).
wants
Things a person may desire or wish for but does not need to survive.
