holistic tradition of Inuit culture, there was close collaboration among 
the writers of the different subject areas to ensure that the educational 
experience of Nunavut children would be fully integrated. 
The valuable efforts, knowledge, skills, and experience of all contributors 
have helped to fulfill one of Nunavut’s fundamental goals as a territory: to 
provide our students with a unique Nunavut-made curriculum – one in 
which our learners can see themselves and their communities and which 
will influence them long into the future.

Art is part of who we are as Inuit. It reflects our heritage and supports our

skills, and self-expression.

write back then. Everything was oral. I used to do little drawings on gum 
wrappers. That's when I started being an artist. As children keep learning 
art, they improve. They will begin to develop their own signature, a style 
like no one else’s.

INTRODUCTION TO THE NUNAVUT CURRICULUM,
KINDERGARTEN TO GRADE 6
As stated in the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for Nunavut 
Curriculum, “schooling in Nunavut should provide support to students in all 
areas of their development so that they can achieve personal goals, become 
well-equipped to contribute and serve their families and communities, 
demonstrate leadership and healthy attitudes, and be able to actively 
participate and contribute as Nunavut takes on new roles in the global 
community.”¹ To achieve this vision for education in Nunavut, it is imperative 
that the curriculum in all subject areas promotes holistic critical thinking 
and encourages continuous learning that can be transferred across contexts. 
It must also promote inclusion and provide opportunities to differentiate 
learning experiences to meet the needs of all students. The updated Nunavut 
Curriculum Framework will provide a common structure for the curriculum 
in each subject area and support this holistic, cross-curricular approach to 
teaching and learning.

subject area. According to the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework

subject-specific content areas, and result in the application and transfer of 
specific learning to new contexts and situations. This approach to learning is 
designed to focus on the development of complex intellectual (metacognitive) 
skills and lead students to transformational ways of thinking and processing. 
This type of approach helps students understand the connections between 
various learnings and the strategies that lead to successful application of 
learning in new contexts.”² Within the Curriculum Foundations are the 
subject areas. These are areas of learning organized as discrete disciplines 
and taught in systemic ways over time.

The aims and principles of the Curriculum Foundations inform the Major 
Understandings in each subject area. Major Understandings are broad 
statements that reflect the essential principles and core ideas within a 
subject area that can be transferred across different areas of learning.³ These 
Major Understandings anchor the curriculum in the purpose for learning 
in each subject area and the values of Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ). They 
act as a lens through which teachers can make decisions about planning, 
instruction, and assessment.
The Major Understandings are explored through the Learning Strands, 
which represent the key concepts and processes within each subject area 
and the relationships among them. The Learning Strands remain consistent 
across grades and will encourage a richer exploration of how these concepts 
and processes work together over time. Within each Learning Strand, there 
are Learning Concepts and grade-specific Learning Outcomes. The 
Learning Concepts outline the constructs, processes, and patterns within 
each Learning Strand. These concepts provide a structure through which 
students can organize and connect the Learning Outcomes. Learning 
Outcomes define the key knowledge, skills, and understandings students will 
develop at each grade level. These become the building blocks that support 
deeper conceptual understanding.
In addition, each grade level has Guiding Questions. The Guiding Questions 
will help link the Learning Concepts and Learning Outcomes back 
to the Curriculum Foundations. By building connections among the knowledge, 
skills, and understandings they have acquired, students will be able to explore 
the principles of the Curriculum Foundations in age-appropriate ways.
Teachers can use the Guiding Questions: 
• In daily classroom activities and discussions
• As part of assessment tasks
• To plan units or themes
• To inspire inquiry-based projects

What is the Arts Education Curriculum?
Art, like language, can connect community, history, culture, and identity. 
Inuit artists have been drawing inspiration from their culture, their history, 
and their environment to create art for millennia.4
 Art in Nunavut integrates 
traditional and modern practises into artistic works that are unique to the 
people, the geography, and the culture of the territory. 
Historically, the Fine Arts curriculum has been divided into distinct 
disciplines and limited to the areas of visual arts, music, dance, and 
drama. These disciplines often rely on Eurocentric representations and 
interpretations of art and do not always reflect the perspectives and creative 
practises of Indigenous communities. The new Nunavut Kindergarten–Grade 
6 Arts Education Curriculum takes a holistic approach to teaching and 
learning through the arts and focuses on the processes, experiences, and 
benefits of creating art and developing a creative practise. It also embraces 
the creative practises and traditions of Inuit and emphasizes the exploration 
of the creative works of local artists, Elders, and other community members. 
Not all learning can be sustained by language alone; the arts can be used as 
a medium of inquiry to open more pathways for exploration, communication, 
and expression. When students use creative practises to make connections 
between the arts and other subject areas, they gain a deeper understanding 
of both. Interdisciplinary projects that use art as a foundation allow students 
to explore new subject material and demonstrate their learning.

Arts Education Curriculum 3.7
Rationale
The Nunavut Kindergarten–Grade 6 Arts Education Curriculum is designed 
to give students opportunities to develop their own creative processes while 
also exploring the views, perspectives, and experiences of others. Creating and 
responding to art can foster students’ curiosity, critical and creative thinking, 
and problem-solving skills. Art also helps them learn empathy, develop 
their identity, and work toward a sense of well-being. This curriculum aims 
to provide a well-rounded Arts Education experience that will encourage 
students to make cross-curricular connections and see the role art can play 
in their lives outside the classroom.
Goals 
From Kindergarten to Grade 6, students will:
• Develop skills and tools to express themselves creatively.
• Communicate their thoughts, feelings, and perspectives through art.
• Acknowledge and appreciate the creative processes and artistic 
works of others.
• Respond to works of art using critical thinking and problem-solving 
skills.
• Investigate Inuit culture and history through traditional artistic 
processes and practises.
• Explore how art can contribute to positive health and well-being.

3.10 Introduction
Curriculum Foundation
The Arts Education Curriculum is part of the Uqausiliriniq Curriculum 
Foundation. The Uqausiliriniq Curriculum Foundation focuses on 
relationships in communication and language development, creative and 
artistic expression, and reflective and critical thinking. The principles of 
the Uqausiliriniq Curriculum Foundation include building relationships, 
respecting others, thinking creatively and seeking to understand, and 
improving on the present while building strength for the future.
Major Understandings
The principles of Uqausiliriniq inform the four Major Understandings within 
the Arts Education Curriculum that continue from Kindergarten to Grade 6. 
These Major Understandings reflect the essential learning within the subject 
area and can act as a lens through which teachers can make decisions about 
planning, instruction, and assessment. The Major Understandings for the 
Arts Education Curriculum are: 
1. Creating and responding to art allows us to build a deeper understanding 
and empathy for ourselves and others in our community.
2. Engaging with art allows us to connect to places, histories, cultures, and 
ideas across time.
3. Playful and holistic exploration of artistic practises allows us to nurture 
meaningful creative expression.
4. Reflecting on creative works, both our own and others’, fosters creative 
thinking, problem-solving, and knowledge for the future.

Arts Education Curriculum 3.11
Learning Strands 
The Arts Education Curriculum has been organized by grade and then 
further broken down into three Learning Strands: Experimenting and 
Creating, Connecting and Reflecting, and Interpreting and Responding. 
Experimenting and Creating 
In this strand, students will explore a variety of artistic processes and 
techniques to generate and develop creative works.
Connecting and Reflecting
In this strand, students will cultivate their own creative practise and 
investigate the role art can play in building their identity and maintaining 
their well-being.
Interpreting and Responding
In this strand, students will examine and evaluate the artistic works 
of others to understand how they communicate meaning across place 
and time.

3.12 Introduction
Learning Concepts and Learning Outcomes 
Students will explore the Learning Strands through Learning Concepts, 
which are unique to the subject area. In Arts Education, it is valuable to place 
learning within a context to provide a focus for students to see themselves 
in the learning, to help them connect what they are learning to the outside 
world, and to maintain consistency in classrooms across Nunavut. The 
Learning Concepts offer a useful learning habitat conducive to developing the 
Major Understandings and nurturing Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ) through 
the arts.
The Learning Concepts for each grade are: 
Kindergarten: Art and Play
In Kindergarten, students will explore themselves and the world around 
them through play. Play encourages creativity by inviting new possibilities, 
ideas, and relationships and helps to develop each child’s unique perspective 
and individual style of creative expression.
Grade 1: Art and My Community
In Grade 1, students will explore the role communities play in the creation of 
art. Students will experiment with and apply the theme of community as 
they consider how artists reflect on place and belonging within their work.
Grade 2: Art and the Land
In Grade 2, students will focus on the relationship between art and the 
land. They will explore how the land teaches, connects, and inspires them 
as artists through inquiry, experimentation, and play. 
Grade 3: Indigenous Art
In Grade 3, students will focus on Indigenous art and explore the practises 
and materials used by Indigenous artists around the world to represent 
place, time, culture, histories, and ideas. 

Arts Education Curriculum 3.13
Grade 4: Art and Media
In Grade 4, students will expand their understanding of how art is produced, 
presented, and performed by exploring different forms of media and 
technology that artists use to create and share their work.
Grade 5: Art and Identity
In Grade 5, students will reflect on who they are as individuals and explore 
their identity through creative practises to develop a better understanding 
of themselves, their community, and the world around them.
Grade 6: Art in Action
In Grade 6, students will examine how art can inspire change and prompt 
meaningful conversations about questions or issues that are important to 
the artist, their community, and the world around them.
The Learning Outcomes in the Arts Education Curriculum are specific 
to each grade level; however, some Learning Outcomes continue across 
grades to provide students with additional time and support to develop 
essential skills, practises, and understandings related to Arts Education.

ARTS-INTEGRATED LEARNING AND THE 
CREATIVE PROCESS
Arts-integrated learning is an instructional approach that involves using the 
arts as an avenue through which students apply and connect content from 
other subject areas. In a non-integrated classroom, students switch between 
subjects without developing any connections between them and only learn 
the content, skills, and understandings of each discipline. Connecting the 
arts to other subjects in a meaningful, integrative way emphasizes the 
Major Understandings of each subject and fosters learning, particularly for 
comprehension and application. The arts are not in service of the content; 
instead, the content and the arts are connected through the creative process.
Engagement in the creative process is at the core of arts integration. The 
creative process is reflected in the three Learning Strands: Experimenting 
and Creating, Connecting and Reflecting, and Interpreting and Responding. 
By not placing emphasis on the final product but celebrating exploration, 
practise, and reflection, students gain critical thinking skills that apply to all 
areas of learning. The creative process teaches students to use their inherent 
curiosity to ask questions, engage with content, and seek answers through 
exploration and experimentation.

TEACHING FROM A CROSS-CURRICULAR 
PERSPECTIVE
According to the Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit Education Framework for 
Nunavut Curriculum, the four Curriculum Foundations—Nunavusiutit, 
Iqqaqqaukkaringniq, Uqausiliriniq, and Aulajaaqtut—were designed to 
promote a holistic approach to education that encourages students to 
transfer learning across subject areas and develop deeper, transformative 
ways of thinking and learning. Planning learning experiences around 
thematic units or integrated projects can provide students with 
opportunities to apply their learning in different contexts. 
Within the Kindergarten–Grade 6 Arts Education Curriculum, there are 
many ways for students to build on prior knowledge and connect what 
they are learning to their lives outside the classroom. The Arts Education

USE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATION 
TECHNOLOGY
The use of information and communication technology is an important 
part of Arts Education. Using these tools to support the Arts Education 
Curriculum, where available, can help enhance student learning and 
allow them to use the tools in a supervised environment. Students can use 
technology to develop and produce their own creative works and find and 
explore the creative works of others.
A fundamental aspect of Arts Education is imitating and reproducing art. 
Fair dealing is a part of copyright law that allows schools to use copyrighted 
material for educational purposes. Students should be able to distinguish 
between situations in which it is appropriate and situations in which it is 
inappropriate to use someone else’s work. While claiming another’s work as 
your own is plagiarism, the goal should not be to stop students from copying 
but rather to initiate a discussion about originality and giving credit to an 
artist when their work has been used.
All these applications of technology can have a positive impact on students’ 
creative practise and learning within Arts Education. However, it is also 
important that students learn to use technology safely and do not put 
themselves and others at risk. They need to learn to differentiate between 
reliable and unreliable sources of information and to create personal 
boundaries to manage their use of technology outside of school.
More information and resources related to the use of information and 
communication technology can be found in the Arts Education Educator 
Handbook.

GLOSSARY 
The following definitions and examples are intended to help teachers use 
this document. The examples provided are suggestions and are not meant to 
be exhaustive.
creative practises
Activities or pursuits that develop artistic skill through curiosity, 
exploration, and imagination.
creative works
Tangible products of creative activities or practises.
critique
A specific, constructive, and informed judgment that focuses on the 
creation, not the creator.
elements
The definable components of a creative work.
personal criteria
The individual standards students use to determine which creative works 
they will share or not share with others.
processes and techniques
The different ways creative works can be expressed, made, or produced.
tools and materials
The various instruments, supplies, and resources used by individuals to 
create, express, and explore artistic concepts and ideas.
