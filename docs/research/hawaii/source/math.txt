Front Matter
 Hawaiʻi Department of Education’s Commitment to Mathematical Excellence

 A PreK-20 mathematics education that cultivates a joyful and inclusive learning environment that
 celebrates the truly diverse people of the islands remains at the heart of the work of the Hawaiʻi
 Department of Education (Department). The Department’s revised standards are designed to
 build upon this foundation and strengthen the rigorous problem-solving and critical thinking skills
 that ensure a strong connection between mathematical concepts and real-life applications.

 Through access to transformative experiences rooted in Hawaiʻi’s unique communities, the
 Department’s goal is to empower all learners to excel and develop a lifelong appreciation for
 mathematics that will unlock their full potential for a brighter future. This enhanced approach is
 defined by a genuine mathematical rigor that requires students to:

        Apply concepts rather than memorize procedures or mimic algorithms.
        Productively struggle through tasks.
        Communicate and explain their mathematical thinking with accuracy and precision, using
        mathematical language and reasoning.
        Connect and build upon mathematical ideas.

The Department defines mathematical fluency as accuracy, efficiency, and flexibility—aligning with

      Accuracy – Solving a problem correctly.
      Efficiency – Solving a [problem] in a reasonable amount of time by selecting an appropriate

      Flexibility – Seeing multiple ways to solve a problem and adapting strategies as needed.


Reasoning: The Scalable Core
Fluency is built on the principle that reasoning is the core of mathematical understanding.
Reasoning from foundational properties builds skills that transfer to any context whereas
procedural gimmicks work for only one type of problem. Students strengthen this understanding by
connecting multiple representations—using manipulatives like base ten blocks or algebra tiles—for
example, to see how the distributive property applies across situations. Whether a child
decomposes 7 × 8 into (7 × 5) + (7 × 3) or an algebra student factors 2x2 + 7x into x(2x + 7)
, reasoning creates durable knowledge that scales across grades. Focusing on reasoning prepares
students for future success and makes math feel meaningful.

Representations, as shown below, are crucial for supporting this level of understanding. For
example, by building connections between concrete, semi-concrete, and abstract representations
simultaneously, students see how these different forms link together all at once. This integrated
approach allows them to move flexibly between ideas, ensuring they develop a deeper
understanding of mathematical concepts and stronger problem-solving skills.

A Framework for Practice and Instruction
The Standards for Mathematical Practice (SMPs) define the behaviors and habits of mind we
expect in every mathematically proficient student. They show what it looks like to actively engage in
mathematics. The Department’s standards support these key practices:

   1. Make sense of problems and persevere in solving them
   2. Reason abstractly and quantitatively
   3. Construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others
   4. Model with mathematics
   5. Use tools strategically
   6. Attend to precision
   7. Look for and make use of structure
   8. Look for and express regularity in repeated reasoning

These practices are essential threads in every learning experience, ensuring mathematics is
meaningful, connected, and applicable.

The Teacher’s Role: The Effective Mathematics Teaching Practices (EMTPs)

Teachers are central to creating a joyful, inclusive learning environment where every student
thrives. The Effective Mathematics Teaching Practices (EMTPs) provide a research-based
framework of high-impact instructional moves that promote deep learning, build mathematical
fluency, and support rigor. The Department’s revised standards are designed to be enacted through
these practices (National Council of Teachers of Mathematics, 2014):

      Establish mathematics goals to focus learning
      Implement tasks that promote reasoning and problem solving
      Use and connect mathematical representations
      Facilitate meaningful mathematical discourse


                                                                                                                         5
     Pose purposeful questions
     Build procedural fluency from conceptual understanding
     Support productive struggle in learning mathematics
     Elicit and use evidence of student thinking




By committing to these practices, educators ensure that Hawaiʻi classrooms develop not just factual
knowledge, but skilled, confident mathematical thinkers. The alignment of SMPs (what students do)
and EMTPs (what teachers do) forms the foundation of the Department’s shared effort to improve
mathematics education, ensuring a robust and engaging experience for both teachers and students
in their mathematical journey.

Using the Standards
The Hawaii Mathematics Standards use a structured coding scheme that includes the following
elements:

   1. Grade Level
      This element identifies the grade level or high school course of the standard:
            Kindergarten is represented by “K”
            Grades 1-8 are represented by their respective number
            High School courses are represented by abbreviation (e.g., “A1” for Algebra 1)

   2. Domain / ʻŌpaʻa
      Each standard is categorized within a domain and represented by a short abbreviation (e.g.,
      “NBT” for “Numbers and Operations in Base Ten”). Domains are larger groups of related
      standards. Each domain can further be broken down into clusters of related standards (e.g.,
      “Generalize place value understanding for multi-digit whole numbers”). For high school, the
      organization of the standards has transitioned from the use of domain and cluster to ʻŌpaʻa
      (unit or set) to reflect a more coherent storyline for high school by suggesting a sequencing of
      the standards.

   3. Standard
      This element specifies the standard within a particular domain / ʻōpaʻa.

While clusters are no longer included in the coding scheme (because each standard is uniquely
numbered within each domain / ʻōpaʻa), they continue to exist as a way of further grouping tightly

Clusters and standards may be accompanied by clarifications and limitations. A clarification further
explains/details expectations around a cluster or standard and can help specify what should be
assessed as part of the standard. They must be viewed as supporting the standard rather than
representing the standard in its entirety. A limitation describes the boundaries and restrictions in the
scope of a particular cluster or standard. To easily distinguish between clarifications and limitations,

High School Overview
The standards for high school have been revised to clarify and streamline concepts while elevating
the Standards for Mathematical Practice, resulting in a coherent order that carefully builds on
knowledge introduced in previous grade levels. High school mathematics standards are organized
by individual courses rather than as a single 9-12 “High School” grade band as was the case in the
Common Core State Standards. Each cluster of standards is now called “ʻŌpaʻa” which is a
grouping of related concepts.

The transition from 8th grade to 9th grade is crucial for students. Starting the high school
mathematical experience with Geometry + Probability has several benefits including making clear
real-world connections and fostering mathematical confidence. The Geometry + Probability
standards have been revised to apply and reinforce proportional and linear algebraic concepts
taught in middle school. By applying algebraic representations to geometric figures, students use
variables as representations of real world quantities— angle measures, side lengths, areas. This
provides a crucial mental anchor before they are asked to manipulate variables fluently beyond
linear contexts. Algebra 1 + Data Analytics continues to explore linear, exponential and quadratic
functions to model data and relationships.

In year three, students choose between two course options aligned to their post-secondary goals:
Quantitative Reasoning or Algebra 2. Quantitative Reasoning is a new course designed for
students who plan to pursue a major or a career which does not require calculus. Algebra 2 has
been modified to build a stronger alignment and bridge to precalculus. This is illustrated in the
figure on the next page.

Rationale for Geometry Before Algebra 1

A new sequence for high school mathematics is developed, where Geometry will be the default 9th
grade mathematics course.

     The Geometry course will not require Algebra 1 as a prerequisite.
     Students who earn an Algebra 1 credit in the 8th grade enroll in Geometry in 9th grade. In the
     10th grade, students have the option to take Quantitative Reasoning or Algebra 2.
     Students who did not take Algebra 1 in 8th grade (most students) enroll in Geometry in 9th
     grade, followed by Algebra 1 in 10th grade. In the 11th grade, students have the option to take
     Quantitative Reasoning or Algebra 2.



                                                                                                  10
The proposed shift to a Geometry-first pathway in high school offers the following benefits:

     Real-World Connections are More Obvious: The applications of geometry are often more
     immediately apparent in the real world – architecture, design, art, navigation, etc. This can
     make the subject matter feel more relevant and motivating for 9th graders, potentially sparking
     greater interest in mathematics before diving into the more abstract manipulations of algebra.
     Fosters Mathematical Confidence: In high school, introducing geometry first allows
     students to develop a positive relationship with mathematics through its visual and hands-on
     nature, setting them up for greater success in algebra.
     Supports Freshman Cohorts: This proposed pathway enables schools to create 9th grade
     cohorts that include math as part of their teaming or academy structures. An additional benefit
     to having all students in Geometry is to have a greater diversity of perspectives when learning
     logic and reasoning. By changing this order, students on an accelerated track will still be able
     to take Algebra 1 in 8th grade, while there will be a common Geometry class for all 9th
     graders.
     Alignment Leads to Smoother Transitions: Students benefit from learning Geometry in 9th
     grade because the middle school geometry standards directly support the high school
     geometry standards. Moving Geometry to 9th grade also enables students to seamlessly
     transition from Algebra 1 directly to Quantitative Reasoning or Algebra 2 in the following year;
     schools that have made this change have noted that Algebra 2 students do not need as much
     “review” of Algebra 1 at the start of the year. Collectively, this allows students to have a more
     coherent mathematical experience.
     Alignment with 9th Grade Sciences: The concepts covered in middle school mathematics
     provide sufficient background knowledge needed for important applications commonly
     covered in 9th grade science courses. It is not essential for high school studies to begin with
     Algebra 1.


A Strategic Approach: Building a Stronger Foundation for
Algebra 1 with Geometry First
The traditional Algebra 1 → Geometry sequence is well-established and valued for its focus on
building procedural algebraic skills. However, many students struggle to move beyond procedure to
true conceptual understanding, which can hinder their success in Algebra 1 and beyond. A
Geometry-first sequence addresses this challenge head-on. It is not about delaying algebra, but
about strategically front-loading the logical reasoning and contextual understanding that allow
students to engage with Algebra 1 on a much deeper and more successful level.




                                                                                                       11
1. Addressing the "Abstraction Hurdle"

  A common challenge is the abstract nature of algebraic concepts in Algebra 1, which can
  create early anxiety and a mindset that math is just about memorizing rules and contextually
  absent algorithms.

       A More Accessible On-Ramp: Geometry leverages the concrete, visual-spatial skills
       they developed in 8th grade with transformations and the Pythagorean Theorem within
       real-world contexts. This allows them to experience success and build confidence at the
       start of their high school journey.
       Reinforcing Algebraic Thinking: By applying algebra skills to geometric figures,
       students use variables to represent real world quantities —angle measures, side
       lengths, areas. This provides a crucial mental anchor before they are asked to
       manipulate variables fluently beyond linear contexts.

2. Forging the "Why": How Geometry Emphasizes Problem Solving

  Many teachers observe students who can follow the steps to solve an equation but falter
  when faced with a novel problem. This is often a gap in logical reasoning, not procedural skill.
  Geometry is the ideal environment to forge this skill.

       Building Logical Arguments: The process of building a logical proof from a set of
       given information is a perfect analogue for solving a complex algebraic equation.
       Students learn to justify every step and understand how each one follows logically from
       the last. This is the very essence of algebraic reasoning.
       From "How" to "Why": After justifying claims in Geometry, a student is far better
       equipped to understand underlying algebraic concepts (e.g., why the distributive
       property works through the area model, or why they can perform the same operation on
       both sides of an equation). They move from mimicking a process to understanding its
       logical foundation.

3. Algebra in Action: Reinforcing Skills Through Constant Application

  A key concern is whether students will have the requisite algebra skills for Geometry. The
  reality is that a well-designed Geometry course is rich with algebraic application, providing
  constant, distributed practice.

       Contextualized Skill-Building: Instead of practicing algebra in isolated problem sets,
       students are constantly using it as a tool to solve interesting geometric problems. They
       set up and solve equations to find missing angles in a transversal diagram or use the
       distance formula (a direct application of the Pythagorean Theorem) to prove properties
       of quadrilaterals.


                                                                                                  12
           Answering "When Will I Use This?": This integrated approach organically answers
           the ubiquitous question. Algebra becomes a necessary and powerful tool for discovery,
           not a set of abstract rules to be memorized. This contextual reinforcement can lead to
           better long-term retention of algebraic skills than an isolated Algebra 1 course.

By sequencing Geometry first, we give students the "why" before the "how." They enter Algebra 1
with stronger logical faculties, a better grasp of what variables can represent, and a year of
experience applying algebraic tools in meaningful contexts. It is a proactive strategy to build more
resilient, thoughtful, and ultimately more successful mathematical problem-solvers.
