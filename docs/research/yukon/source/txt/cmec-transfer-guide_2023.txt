             Secondary Education in the Provinces and Territories of Canada: A Student Transfer Guide 2023–24

                                                             Yukon

Part 1 – Summary Statement

1.1 Introduction

The aim of the Yukon Kindergarten to Grade 12 education system is to enable all students to develop their individual potential
and to acquire knowledge, skills, and abilities needed to graduate with an education that is relevant to, and representative of
their lives and community. Yukon’s education system expresses a committed approach to our Yukon First Nations’ content and
ways of knowing, doing, and being.
Yukon Education works with First Nations communities and governments to develop school programs. Formal education
agreements have been signed between the Government of Yukon and some First Nations. This has led to the development of
curriculum and programs in the traditional territories of those First Nations. For example, through our agreement with the
Tr’ondëk Hwëch’in, we have accredited three cultural camps: First Hunt, First Fish, and Spring Camp.
Yukon First Nations culture is incorporated into many courses and programs in Yukon schools. Residential schools, Our Stories
of Residential Schools in Yukon and Canada, is now part of the mandatory Social Studies 10 course. Social Studies (Grade 5):
Yukon First Nations Governance; and Yukon First Nations Citizenship.

Ancestral Technology (Grades 10 and 11): English First Peoples (Grades 10, 11 and 12); and BC First Nations Studies (Grade 12).

Furthermore, in Yukon’s education system there are experiential learning programs with a Yukon First Nations’ focus. Such as,
Community, Heritage, Arts, Outdoors and Skills (CHAOS) program at Wood Street Centre in Whitehorse (Grades 9 and 10);
Tr’ondëk Hwëch’in Traditional Camp at Robert Service School in Dawson City; and land-based experiential education with the
Vuntut Gwich’in First Nation at Chief Zzeh Gittlit School in Old Crow.
The curriculum model is made up of three elements: Content, Curricular Competencies, and Big Ideas. Three elements, the
Content (Know), Curricular Competencies (Do), and Big Ideas (Understand) all work together to support deeper learning.
Educators combine the three elements in ways they see fit to personalize learning in their classrooms. All areas of learning are
based on a “Know-Do-Understand” model to support a concept-based and competency-driven approach to learning.
Content is the essential knowledge that students must learn at each grade level. Students are expected to know the content.
Curricular Competencies are explicit statements of what is expected at each grade level in each area of learning. Curricular
Competencies are the skills, strategies, and processes that students develop over time and apply to their ongoing lifelong
learning. Curricular Competencies are unique to each area of learning. Students are expected to be able to demonstrate (do)
the Curricular Competencies.
Big Ideas consist of generalizations, principles, and the key concepts important within an area of learning. Big Ideas represent
what students will understand at the completion of their grades in each area of learning. These concepts extend beyond a
single grade and help build a broad base for a student’s future understanding.

These elements are all woven together to form the curriculum structure in the following manner:
   •    Understand the Big Ideas and concepts that can be transferred to other settings.
   •    Demonstrate (do) and apply the Curricular Competencies within each area of learning.
   •    Know the content of each of the subject areas.
Core Competencies are sets of intellectual, personal, social, and emotional proficiencies that all students need to engage in
deep, lifelong learning. Core Competencies underpin the curricular competencies in all areas of learning. They are directly
related to the educated citizen and as such are what we value for all students in the system. The Core Competencies are
                                                                                                                                  1
Communication (communicating and collaborating), Thinking (creative, critical, and reflective thinking), and Personal and
Social awareness and responsibility, positive personal and cultural identity, social awareness, and responsibility).
Literacy is defined as the ability to understand, critically analyze, and create a variety of forms of communication, including
oral, written, visual, digital, and multimedia, to accomplish one’s goals. Numeracy is defined as the ability to understand and
apply mathematical concepts, processes, and skills to solve problems in a variety of contexts. While they are commonly
associated with language learning and mathematics, literacy and numeracy are fundamental to, and applied in, all areas of
learning.
More information about BC’s curriculum, which Yukon implements, can be found at
https://curriculum.gov.bc.ca/curriculum/overview.
More information about Yukon’s education system can be found at http://lss.yukonschools.ca/

1.2 Organization of School System

The Yukon education system is divided into three levels: primary (Kindergarten to Grade 3), intermediate (Grades 4 to 9), and
graduation years (Grades 10 to 12). (https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/education/administration/kindergarten-to-grade-
12/graduation/handbook_of_procedures.pdf)
Each level of the Yukon education system emphases a range of knowledge, skills, and attitudes that students develop. These
skills are developed through competency-based curriculum and standards-based assessment demonstrated through inquiry-
based approaches and studies in English language arts, mathematics, science, social studies, fine arts, and applied design skills
and technologies from Kindergarten to Grade 12. The competency-based approach is comprised of prescribed curriculum to
ensure all students — not solely those planning to go to university — gain the thinking, communication, personal, and social
skills needed to become lifelong learners who are aware of themselves and connected to their communities.
Yukon Education sets high-level policies, monitors system performance, and distributes funding, while school authorities are
responsible for the day-to-day operation of schools and the delivery of educational programs to students.

Yukon Education serves as a government body as well as a school authority (with 20 schools in its direct authority). Two other
school authorities exist in Yukon; the Commission scolaire francophone du Yukon (CSFY) (with three schools) and the First
Nations School Board (FNSB) (with 11 schools as of SY23/24), including summer schools and online learning schools incorporated
within the entire education system.

1.3 Explanation of Terms Used (including special-education/student-services terms)

Ministry‐Authorized Courses
BC’s Course Registry (www.bced.gov.bc.ca/datacollections/course_registry_web_search/search-home.en.php) lists courses
authorized by the Ministry of Education and Child Care, including both curricula developed by the ministry and external
credentials approved by the ministry. (See External Credential Courses, below.) The Course Registry includes course titles and
codes, grade levels, and the number of credits students can earn for each course.

Locally Developed Courses
Locally developed courses are Grades 10, 11, or 12 non-credit courses developed by a local school. The courses are based on
subject matter from a particular field of knowledge and on a skill set selected and organized by a particular school or school
district. However, it is required that specific course codes be used as described in the B.C. Graduation Program Handbook of
Procedures (https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/education/administration/kindergarten-to-grade-
12/graduation/handbook_of_procedures.pdf) for reporting course enrollment and completion, and use of these codes must
be approved by the school authority. Locally developed courses count towards elective credits towards graduation. These
courses are approved by the Minister of Education and/or delegate.

External Credential Courses
These courses are organized sets of learning activities that fall outside the British Columbia school curriculum and are listed in
the Course Registry (e.g., Royal Conservatory of Music). Students receive graduation credit for successfully completing an
external course. These courses are of an educational standard deemed equivalent to or exceeding that of ministry-authorized
                                                                                                                                  2
Grade 10, 11, or 12 courses.

Independent Directed Studies (IDS) Courses
Independent Directed Studies (IDS) courses are student-initiated and conducted under teacher supervision. These courses are
based on the content of ministry-developed or Board/Authority-Authorized Grades 10 to 12 courses. An IDS course can be for
1, 2, 3, or 4 credits, where 1 credit represents, the value attached to the knowledge, skills, and attitudes most students can
acquire in approximately thirty hours of instruction. Although IDS is intended to allow students to pursue curriculum in more
detail or to focus on only some of the content of a course that has not been taken, IDS can also be used to recognize learning
in courses students do not complete.

Post‐Secondary (Dual Credit) Courses
These courses, offered from qualifying post-secondary institutions, lead to a post-secondary credential. A student presenting
a transcript from a recognized institution showing successful completion of a post-secondary course that leads to a credential
is entitled to have that course count towards secondary school graduation.

Yukon School Completion (“Evergreen”) Certificate
Yukon School Completion (“Evergreen”) Certificate is intended to celebrate success in learning that is not recognized in the
Yukon School Certificate of Graduation (Dogwood Diploma). It is used to recognize the accomplishments of students with
disabilities who have an Individual Education Plan (see Individual Education Plan, below) and have met the goals of their
education, other than graduation. The Evergreen Certificate is not a graduation credential; students who receive an Evergreen
certificate have not graduated. The Yukon School Completion Certificate is a specifically designed certificate, distinct from the
Dogwood Diploma.

In addition to receiving an Evergreen Certificate, Yukon Education provides students with a BC Ministry transcript showing all
credit and locally developed courses completed that also contains a statement indicating that the student has been issued a
Yukon School Completion Certificate. The Transcript of Grades provides prospective employers and post-secondary
institutions with relevant information about the student’s accomplishments. A French version of the Evergreen Certificate is
available upon request.

Individual Education Plan (IEP)
An Individual Education Plan (IEP) is a documented plan developed for a student with special needs that describes
individualized goals, adaptations, modifications, and the services to be provided, and it includes measures for tracking
achievement.

Career Programs
Career programs are local educational programs, focusing on a career or career sector that combine related, in-school
coursework with a work experience component. Career program participation appears on a student’s transcript. Information
concerning career programs can be found at https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/education-training/k-12/support/career-
programs.

1.4 Course Designation

Courses at the Grades 10 to 12 levels are assigned an official code that includes the course name and grade level. For example,
French 11 (FR 11) is the Grade 11 French course. Locally developed courses carry the designation “LD” at the beginning of the
title and “X” at the beginning of the code, whereas board/authority-authorized courses begin with a “Y.” External courses use
a “U” at the beginning of the code. Independent directed studies courses are coded with the prefix “IDS” followed by the
regular course code for the related ministry-developed or board/authority-authorized course. Post-secondary courses that
count towards graduation use “PSI” at the beginning of the code, and the name of the post-secondary institution where the
course was completed shows on the transcript.

1.5 Time Allotments and Course Load.

In Grades 4 to 9, minimum time allotments expressed as percentages of total instructional time are recommended for each
required area of study. They suggest the priority Yukon Education expects schools to give to each area of study. It is up to each
                                                                                                                                3
school to design a timetable appropriate for all students. Variation in the recommended times is allowed to address the
learning needs of individual students and the specific needs of communities.

Yukon Education does not specify required hours of instruction per course, but rather focuses on evidence of student learning
to demonstrate that each learning standard has been met. This can vary in time depending on the student’s needs.

The total number of annual instructional hours for Kindergarten is 853, for Grades 1–7 is 878, and for Grades 8–12 is 952.

1.6 Curriculum Organization

Yukon implements BC’s curriculum, and it is organized in to “typical” disciplines. More information is available at
http://www.curriculum.gov.bc.ca. Additional details are included in Section 2 for courses included in BC’s graduation
program.

Students in Yukon’s graduation program must complete at least 80 credits of Grades 10 to 12 coursework to satisfy minimum
graduation requirements. (See Section 1.9, below for more detail regarding graduation credit.) The length and scope of
courses are reflected in the credit value awarded to them. Courses may have a value of 1, 2, 3, or 4 credits. Historically, a 4-
credit course is between 100–120 hours of instructional time. Most courses are worth 4 credits.

1.7 Prerequisites and/or Co-requisites

There are no prerequisites for senior secondary ministry courses; however, students are usually recommended to complete
the lower-level course before enrolling in the next level. Schools, in consultation with school authorities, parents and
students, inform appropriate placement decisions.

1.8 Assessment and Evaluation Practices; and Transcripts (including issuing authority)

For Kindergarten to Grade 9, a four-point Provincial Proficiency Scale is used to communicate student learning in all areas of
learning. The four points of the scale are Emerging, Developing, Proficient, and Extending. For students in Grades 10 to 12,
letter grades indicating students’ level of performance in relation to the learning standards set out in the curriculum for each
course are accompanied by percentage marks. The successful completion of a course numbered 10, 11, or 12 requires a
minimum grade of C- or 50 per cent. Yukon follows assessment guidelines as set out in Communicating Student Learning
Applebook.

Information concerning student reporting can be found at https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/education-training/k-
12/administration/legislation-policy/public-schools/student-reporting

Permanent Student Record
The purpose of the permanent student record is to record the history of a student’s education program from Kindergarten
through Grade 12. Copies can be requested from the last Yukon or BC school in which the student was enrolled. The
permanent student record must be retained by school districts for 55 years after a student has withdrawn or graduated from
school.
Transcript of Grades
Transcripts showing a student’s results in Grades 10, 11, and 12 courses are produced centrally by Yukon Education. A
transcript is the official document that indicates successful completion of Grades 10, 11, and 12 courses, course achievement
levels, program participation, and how many graduation requirement credits have been completed. Transcripts only record
successfully completed courses. If a student has repeated a course, only the highest mark is reported on the transcript.

1.9 List of Graduation Credentials (including credentials giving access to postsecondary education), Requirements for
    Graduation, and Issuing Authorities

Successful completion of a graduation-level educational program is recognized through the awarding of a Yukon Certificate of
Graduation (the Dogwood Diploma). There are two graduation programs leading to a Dogwood Diploma: the Yukon

                                                                                                                                   4
Graduation Program (2023) and the Adult Graduation Program. Students who successfully complete provincial adult
graduation requirements are awarded the Yukon Adult Graduation Diploma (the Adult Dogwood Diploma).

School Completion Certificates, in contrast, are intended to recognize the accomplishments of students who have succeeded in
meeting goals of their educational program other than graduation and are especially intended to recognize the
accomplishments of students with disabilities who complete the goals and objectives stated in their Individual Education Plan.

The following information describes the Dogwood Diploma and the requirements for graduation for Yukon (2023) and Adult
Graduation programs.

Dogwood Diploma
The Dogwood Diploma is the Yukon/BC Certificate of Graduation for the province’s graduation programs. A student who
meets the applicable graduation requirements (Yukon Graduation Program [2023] or Adult Graduation Program) is entitled to
receive a Dogwood Diploma (or Adult Dogwood Diploma in the Adult Graduation program). A French version of the Dogwood
Diploma is issued to students who meet requirements for the Programme francophone or French Immersion program.

Yukon Graduation Program (2023)
To graduate, students must earn a minimum of 80 credits, which include Required Courses (52 credits) and Elective Credits
(minimum 28 credits).
    •   Elective Credits are additional credits earned by students to support their academic, career, or personal interests.
    •   Of the 80 credits needed for graduation, at least 16 credits must be at the Grade 12 level, including a required Grade
        12 language arts course (4 credits) and the required Career Life Connections course (4 credits). The remaining
        required 8, Grade 12-level credits may be from Required Courses or Elective Courses.
    •   Effective July 1, 2023: At least 4 credits must include Indigenous-focused coursework
        (https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/education-training/k-12/sup- port/indigenous-focused-graduation-
        requirements).
    •   Students earn elective credits for locally developed courses.

Students in English must complete three graduation assessments to graduate:
    •  The Grade 10 Numeracy Assessment
    •  The Grade 10 Literacy Assessment
    •  The Grade 12 Literacy Assessment

Students in French Immersion must complete the following to graduate:
    •  French Immersion students can earn both a Yukon Certificate of Graduation (Dogwood Diploma) and a Diplôme de fin
       d’études secondaires. To graduate with both diplomas, French Immersion students must meet the graduation
       requirements for the Dogwood Diploma along with the completion of four provincial Graduation Assessments:
             o  The Grade 10 Numeracy Assessment
             o  The Grade 10 Literacy Assessment
             o  The Grade 12 Literacy Assessment
             o  Évaluation de littératie 12 - français langue seconde immersion

Students in Core French (Francophone) must complete the following to graduate:
    •  Francophone students can earn both a Yukon Certificate of Graduation (Dogwood Diploma) and a Diplôme de fin
       d’études secondaires. To graduate with both diplomas, Francophone students must meet the graduation
       requirements for the Dogwood Diploma along with the completion of five provincial Graduation Assessments:
             o   The Grade 10 Numeracy Assessment
             o   The Grade 10 Literacy Assessment
             o   The Grade 12 Literacy Assessment
             o   Évaluation de littératie 10 - français langue première
             o   Évaluation de littératie 12 - français langue première

    •   Social Studies 10 (4 credits) and Social Studies 11 or 12 (4 credits)

Elective Credits
Students must earn at least 28 elective credits. These credits can be for the following:
    •    Additional Grades 10, 11, or 12 Ministry-Developed Courses
    •    External Credentials
    •    Board/Authority-Authorized Courses

Indigenous-focused Graduation Requirement
Students are also required to complete at least 4 credits in Indigenous-focused coursework. There is no change to the total
number of credits required to graduate, which remains at 80. Courses used to meet this requirement may also be used to meet
other graduations requirements (e.g., English First Peoples 12 would meet both this requirement and the Grade 12 Language
Arts requirement).

The B.C. Graduation Program Policy Guide (https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/education/kindergarten-to-grade-

required course requirements. The Course Registry
(www.bced.gov.bc.ca/datacollections/course_registry_web_search/search-home.en.php) can also be used to find courses
satisfying graduation requirements. Additional information concerning the graduation numeracy and literacy assessments can
be found at https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/education-training/k-12/support/curriculum-and-
assessment/assessments/graduation.

Adult Graduation Program
Learners who are 18 years of age or older can combine credits earned at both secondary and post-secondary schools towards a
Yukon Adult Graduation Diploma (Adult Dogwood Diploma). Adult learners may also pursue a regular Dogwood Diploma. To
complete the Adult Graduation Program, adult students must earn at least 20 credits in the secondary system (five, 4-credit
courses) or complete five courses in the post-secondary system. Courses and credits can be counted from Yukon school
system and/or from a college Adult Basic Education program. This is a common credential between both secondary and post-
secondary systems in Yukon/BC and is recognized as true secondary school graduation, along with the regular Grade 12
Dogwood Diploma.
    •   Accounting 11 and 12 count as a mathematics requirement on Adult Dogwood Diploma only.
    •   Students working on the Adult Dogwood cannot use Board/Authority-Authorized courses for credit.

Of the five courses required for the Adult Dogwood Diploma, at least three must be completed after the adult student has
entered the Adult Graduation Program, either through enrolment (instruction) or Prior Learning Assessment. This means that
students can receive credit toward the Adult Dogwood Diploma for no more than two qualifying courses completed while in
the Yukon (school-aged) Graduation Program. Learners cannot receive an Adult Dogwood Diploma using only courses
completed prior to enrolling in the Adult Graduation Program.

Adult learners are not required to complete the Graduation Numeracy or Literacy Assessments to graduate with the Adult
Dogwood Diploma.

1.10 Other types of Programs

Online Learning
Online learning is a classroom alternative. It used to be called distributed learning. With this style of learning, students:
    •    connect with their Yukon certified teacher from anywhere in Yukon using the internet, phone, or mail;
    •    work with their teacher to reach goals in their learning plan;
    •    take a program or course online or by web conferencing, teleconferencing, or correspondence; and
    •    get support through opportunities for in-person learning and interactions with other students.

Students sign up for online learning with an Online Learning School. Online learning continues to provide a flexible option for
students to learn from home that meets education standards.

Additional information about online learning can be found at https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/education-training/k-
12/support/classroom-alternatives/online-learning.

1.11 Assessment of Out-of-Province/Out-of-Territory and Foreign Studies.

Decisions regarding the assessment and placement of out-of-province and foreign students are made within each school
authority, in consultation with Yukon Education. Most often, school placement recommendations are made by a school
administrator in collaboration with a Yukon Education Consultant. If additional information is needed, the International
Credential Evaluation Service (ICES) can be contacted. ICES was established as a national service evaluating international
education credentials and is operated by the British Columbia Institute of Technology (BCIT).

Further information about ICES can be found at http://www.bcit.ca/ices.
