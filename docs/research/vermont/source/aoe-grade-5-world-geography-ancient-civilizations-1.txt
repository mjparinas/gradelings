   Grade 5 – World Geography and Ancient Civilizations I: Western Asia, the
               Middle East, North Africa, Sub-Saharan Africa
Overview
In grade 5, students will explore how people moved, traded, and interacted with others in early civilizations. They will learn
how geography, power, wealth, and religion shaped the growth and culture of these societies. Students will focus on
regions like Western Asia, the Middle East, North Africa, and Sub-Saharan Africa. They will also learn about important
cultural contributions and lasting achievements. By studying ancient history, students will better understand the roots of
the modern world.

Looking Back
In grade 4, students examined change and continuity within Vermont and United States history from pre-colonial times
through the Civil War. Students learned the concept of movement and its impact on people, goods, and ideas and how
natural resources have influenced economic development. Students examined the conflicts and compromises that
resulted from migration and settlement to understand the tensions and factors that led to the fight for independence, the
establishment of the United States, and civil unrest.

Looking Ahead
In grade 6, students will again explore the world and ancient civilizations by examining how movement and migration
impacted the interactions between expanding civilizations through conquest and trade, including the physical and political
geography and ancient societies of South and East Asia, Oceania, Latin America, and Europe. Students will note how
geography, power, wealth, and religion influenced culture, change, and development within civilizations as they examine
how the perspectives of political science, economics, geography, history, and archaeology apply to the study of regions
and countries.

Inquiry
Why inquiry? Using inquiry as an instructional model for the teaching of social studies allows students to authentically ask
questions, search for and analyze evidence, and provide explanations and arguments to challenges facing their world.
       The inquiry instructional model is driven by authentic questions or problems that have relevance for children.
       Students learn content, skills, and dispositions as they seek answers to questions and as they apply their learning
       to take informed action. Pioneered by Dewey (1938), inquiry is an attempt to make classroom experiences feel
       more like the authentic, curiosity-driven experiences that motivate learning outside of school. (Using Inquiry to
       Prepare Students for College, Career, and Civic Life, elementary edition, p. 22)
The Inquiry Arc is defined in Dimension 1 (questions), Dimension 3 (evidence), and Dimension 4 (communication) of the
College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards. In the AOE-developed Proficiency-
Based Learning Social Studies Hierarchy, Dimensions 1, 2, and 3, as well as evidence indicators found within Dimension
2 – History, have been combined into a singular Critical Proficiency – the Inquiry Process. Opportunities for showing
proficiency in the Inquiry Process fall within the K-2, 3-5, 6-8 grade bands of the Priority Performance Indicators (below):
Constructing Compelling and Supporting Questions, Gathering and Evaluating Sources, and Communicating and
Critiquing Solutions. The Inquiry Process Critical Proficiency, as with all of the CPs, begins its focus in the elementary
grades and spans through high school, preparing students to attain proficiency in social studies for college and career
readiness.
The Critical Proficiency (CP) remains the same K-12 and identifies the enduring skills and knowledge that all students
need to demonstrate (across contexts) to be prepared for future learning, including post-secondary career and college
readiness. Priority Performance Indicators clarify what will be taught and assessed at each grade cluster.
Critical Proficiency (K-12): Students act as social scientists as they ask questions, think critically, solve problems, and
share conclusions.

Resources
Agriculture - C3 IDM: Students will examine sources related to the development of agriculture, the emergence of ancient
writing in Mesopotamia, and the rise of social inequalities as they construct an argument in response to the compelling
question “Was the development of agriculture good for humans?”
Ancient Trade Networks - OER - History project: Local networks might have been the most common and important
types of networks in this period, but long-distance regional trade networks also connected societies across Afro-Eurasia
and the Americas. Trade along these routes was slow and dangerous, but it allowed for the exchange of people, goods,
ideas, and diseases across many regions. The networks changed the communities they touched and transformed
production and distribution in every region.
4. Analyze how regional networks of exchange came into existence and how they became larger and more
   interconnected over time.
5. Evaluate how the sharing of goods and ideas such as metals and iron-making skills transformed societies.
6. Map regional trade networks to understand how communities were interconnected through trade and exchange.

Mali Wealth - C3 IDM: This inquiry focuses on understanding how the Kingdom of Mali became a center of wealth in
West Africa. The questions, tasks, and sources in this inquiry asks students to analyze a variety of primary and secondary
sources to determine the most important factor for generating wealth in Mali: geography, trade, or religion.
Nomadic Life - C3 IDM: This inquiry leads students through an investigation of prehistoric nomadic life. By investigating
the compelling question “How do we know the past without written evidence?” students evaluate how nomads
communicated, where they lived, and how they defined their needs based on the places they lived. The formative

Ancient Agrarian Societies: Nubia and Ancient Egypt - OER - History Project: In this reading, students will learn
about the Nile River Valley. Best known for pyramids and mummies, communities in this area, such as Egypt and Nubia,

Ancient Egypt - OER- History Project: Ancient Egypt was the site of some of the world’s oldest agrarian societies. Over
5,000 years ago, Egyptian pharaohs first rose to power and built mighty empires and astounding monuments. For 3,000
years, Egyptian pharaohs ruled the lands of the Nile River Valley. The Nile River was the key to Egypt’s success. In this
article [with guiding questions], you’ll learn about how the river shaped Ancient Egypt and the people who lived there from
c. 3400 to 30 BCE.
Ancient Hebrews - C3IDM: This C3 Inquiry leads students through an investigation of ancient Hebrew history and
Judaism by using various sources that consider the historical and theological foundations, as well as issues related to
geography and place. By investigating the compelling question, students examine the history of the ancient Hebrew
people, the principles that form the foundation of Judaic thought, the connection of history and religion to Israel, and the
geographic variance in religious practices. By completing this inquiry, students begin to understand issues revolving
around the factors that help to form a people’s identity, as well as the fluidity of defining a group within any predetermined
criteria.
Building the Pyramids of Ancient Egypt - PBS learning Media: In this interactive lesson supporting literacy skills,
students learn how historians think the pyramids were built and why they were built—as doorways to the afterlife for the
pharaohs. Students develop their literacy skills as – OER – Big History Project. They explore a social studies focus on
ancient Egyptian society and how those at the top organized the rest of the population in order to build these amazing
structures. During this process, they read informational text, learn and practice vocabulary words, and explore content
through videos and interactive activities.
Egyptian Flooding - C3 IDM: This inquiry focuses on the impact of the flooding of the Nile River on ancient Egypt,
specifically the costs and benefits of the flooding. Through examination and analysis of various photographs, videos, and
article excerpts, students will consider how these sources can be used to convey the impact of flooding on multiple groups
of people in ancient Egypt.
