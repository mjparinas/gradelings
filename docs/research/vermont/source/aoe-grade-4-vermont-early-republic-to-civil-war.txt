 Grade 4 - Vermont, the Early Republic and the U.S.: Pre-Colonial Times through the Civil War
Overview
In grade 4, students will examine change and continuity in Vermont and United States history from pre-colonial times
through the Civil War. Students will understand the concept of movement and its impact on people, goods, and ideas;
learn how natural resources have influenced economic development; and examine the conflicts and compromises that
resulted from migration and settlement. This learning will allow students to understand the tensions and factors that led to
the fight for independence, the establishment of the United States, and subsequent Vermont statehood. Students will
prepare for their role as responsible and informed citizens by examining the Vermont Constitution and the United States
Constitution, including the determinants of power and its effects; exploring the development, structure, and function of the
United States government as well as the role of individual rights and responsibilities; examining the choices made by
various groups of people on the development of the United States through the Civil War; and learn how events, people,
and ideas shaped and influenced our democratic system. Grade 4 provides the framework for further study of Vermont
and United States History in Grade 7, civics in Grade 8, and United States History/American Government in high school.

Looking Back
In grade 3, students expanded on their understanding of the concept of community and widened their global lens by
exploring Africa, Asia, Europe, and Oceania. Students learned about continuities and differences among cultures,
governments, traditions, and resources. Students learned about the idea of culture spreading across borders and were
able to see the ways various cultural groups connect and interact.

Looking Ahead
In grade 5, students will examine how people moved, traded, and interacted with others in early civilizations. They
will learn how geography, power, wealth, and religion shaped the growth and culture of these societies. Students

Inquiry
Why inquiry? Using inquiry as an instructional model for the teaching of social studies allows students to authentically ask
questions, search for and analyze evidence, and provide explanations and arguments to challenges facing their world.
       The inquiry instructional model is driven by authentic questions or problems that have relevance for children.
       Students learn content, skills, and dispositions as they seek answers to questions and as they apply their learning
       to take informed action. Pioneered by Dewey (1938), inquiry is an attempt to make classroom experiences feel
       more like the authentic, curiosity-driven experiences that motivate learning outside of school. (Using Inquiry to
       Prepare Students for College, Career, and Civic Life, elementary edition, p. 22)
The Inquiry Arc is defined in Dimension 1 (questions), Dimension 3 (evidence), and Dimension 4 (communication) of the
College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards. In the AOE-developed Proficiency-
Based Learning Social Studies Hierarchy, Dimensions 1, 2, and 3, as well as evidence indicators found within Dimension
2 – History, have been combined into a singular Critical Proficiency – the Inquiry Process. Opportunities for showing
proficiency in the Inquiry Process fall within the K-2, 3-5, 6-8 grade bands of the Priority Performance Indicators (below):
Constructing Compelling and Supporting Questions, Gathering and Evaluating Sources, and Communicating and
Critiquing Solutions. The Inquiry Process Critical Proficiency, as with all of the CPs, begins its focus in the elementary
grades and spans through high school, preparing students to attain proficiency in social studies for college and career
readiness.
The Critical Proficiency (CP) remains the same K-12 and identifies the enduring skills and knowledge that all students
need to demonstrate (across contexts) to be prepared for future learning, including post-secondary career and college
readiness. Priority Performance Indicators clarify what will be taught and assessed at each grade cluster.
Critical Proficiency (K-12): Students act as social scientists as they ask questions, think critically, solve problems, and
share conclusions.

A Lesson Plan for Independence Day, the Fourth of July - Center for Civic Education: In this lesson, students will
examine some of the fundamental ideas about government that are contained in the Declaration of Independence and the
Preamble to the United States Constitution. Students should be able to explain those ideas and identify which ideas the
class holds in common.

Assessing Access - Learning for Justice: Students will apply the principles of American democracy to people with
disabilities by analyzing how individuals and groups in American society have struggled to achieve the liberties and
equality promised in the principles of American democracy.

Becoming a State - Vermont History Explorer: This three-day lesson introduces the students to the conflicts between
the Yorkers and the New Hampshire Grants by asking the students to pretend they are settlers or investors who receive
charters and patents for new towns in Vermont. The students divide the towns and investigate the natural resources on
their land. After writing their own constitution, the students learn about Vermont’s Constitution and see the official
document that made Vermont the 14th state.

BRI, JR - Bill of Rights Institute: Character, History, and Civics for Younger Learners: Ten lessons that are primary
source based, inquiry-based, content-area literacy instruction focused, modular, and time-friendly. Each lesson contains:
paper-based worksheets, a tie to their BRI Jr. Principles & Virtues Framework, performance-based assessments, Lexiled
readings, games and/or simulations, and an optional video component.

Constitution Lessons: Select by grade level. The Center for Civic Education has developed constitution-focused lesson
plans with topics such as “What Basic Ideas About Government are Included in the Preamble to the Constitution?” and
“What Should the Representative Do?”

Declaration of Independence - C3 IDM (5th grade inquiry that may need support): This inquiry asks why countries
declare their independence. As an integral early step in the process of becoming independent, a declaration of
independence functions as an argument for why people should be free. This inquiry focuses on the argument made in the
United States Declaration of Independence. With a firm understanding of the American colonists’ argument for

How Did Opposition to Laws Fuel the American Revolutionary War? - iCivics Education: What happens when
people don’t have a say in the laws? In this inquiry-based unit, students will explore how the American colonies’
resistance to laws fueled the American Revolutionary War. They will analyze different points of view about the Stamp Act,
investigate colonial reactions to the Intolerable Acts, and determine when exactly the American Revolutionary War began.

Life, Liberty, and the Pursuit of Happiness. National Archives: Students will learn about the Declaration of

From Slavery to Freedom in Colonial Times - PBS Learning Media: Learn the story of Venture Smith, an African boy
who was enslaved and brought to America in 1737. In this interactive lesson, students will explore the differences
between slavery in the North and South as they learn about Smith’s journey from slavery to freedom.
General Store Activity - Vermont History Explorer: This activity was developed for the Vermont History
Museum's History for Homeschoolers class focused on agriculture, and in connection with the General Store within
the Freedom & Unity exhibit. During this activity, students act as if they are Vermont farmers in the 1830s. They will
barter their goods for others with the same value within their community.
Manhattan Purchase - C3 IDM: On the surface, the compelling question for this inquiry, “What's the real story behind the
purchase of Manhattan?" that asks students to explore the background to the story of the sale of Manhattan to the Dutch
in 1626 for $24 of beads and trinkets. A closer look at the story reveals a range of myths and unknowns including who
was involved in the transaction, what the transaction actually meant, and what was exchanged.
Seaclass - Mystic Seaport Museum: This digital learning opportunity can support educator learning about the whaling
industry with content including, “Why Whaling? The Impact and Industry Explained” and “Women in Whaling: Hidden
Histories of the Sea.”
Teaching the Erie Canal - Erie Canalway National Heritage Corridor: Interdisciplinary lessons (math, reading, PE,
social studies, art, music, science) including mapping, “Cooking on the Erie Canal,” and making a canal boat.
The Beaver Trade and the Purchase of Manhattan - PBS Learning Media: Learn about the extensive fur trade
between the Dutch and Native Americans that sustained the early settlements of New Netherland in this video from Dutch
New York; Teaching Tips and Discussion Questions included.
The Panic of 1837 - C3 IDM: Students investigate the compelling question, “How Did Cotton Sow the Seeds of Panic?”
Students identify the market forces of demand and supply during the boom and bust of the cotton industry in the United
States during the time surrounding the Panic of 1837. They learn how economic and political forces impacted the
treatment of enslaved persons in the United States during the lead up to The Panic of 1837. The depth of this inquiry is in
the reflection on what role reliance on enslaved people as labor played in the economic growth of the United States. This
emphasizes that individual and institutional choices can lead to the exploitation of individuals and groups.

Chief Joseph of the Nez Perce Speaks for the Indians - PBS Learning Media: Students review and answer questions
about a Nez Perce chief’s various speeches he gave in the mid 1800s in order to gain insight about how the United States
government and white settlers were affecting American Indian lives.

“Does Where You Live Matter?” C3 IDM: This annotated inquiry focuses on physical geography in general and on the
relationship between early (pre-1700) Native American nations and their environments in particular through the
compelling question “Does where you live matter?” The compelling question highlights the idea that geography is not a
neutral entity. Environments can exert an influence on human existence, but they are not immune from change—human
activity can modify the physical landscape. (While this inquiry focuses on New York, there were no state boundaries at
that time, so it is still relevant for Vermont learners.)

Florida Footprints: A Feral Land (1704-1845) - PBS Learning Media: By the 18th and 19th centuries, Florida had been
known to the Europeans for hundreds of years, but this area was far from tame. Spanish, French, British, American and
Native Americans fight for control over “A Feral Land” once known as La Florida. Lesson includes video, maps, timelines
and discussion questions.

How Does a Map Show the Relationship Between People and the Environment? - iCivics Education: In this lesson,
students will think about how a map can show the relationship between people and the environment. The lesson begins
with students learning new vocabulary to support the lesson. They will analyze a natural resource map of Maryland and
list the vast resources available. Using historical maps and contemporary images, students will look for evidence of how

Louisiana Purchase Debate - PBS Learning Media: Students read information and excerpts from primary sources

Manifest Destiny and the American Indians - PBS Learning Media: Students study the change in tribal lands over

North America’s Great Waters: Explorers and Traders - PBS Learning Media: In this lesson, students will explore the
expeditions of four French explorers. After viewing three short videos about Samuel de Champlain, Louis Jolliet and
Jacques Marquette, and René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de la Salle, students will analyze a painting that shows Champlain
leading a fleet of canoes on an expedition and then read journal entries that describe Jolliet and Marquette’s expeditions.
The lesson will conclude with students creating a game that encompasses the travels, settlements, and expansion of
French territory in North America during the 1600s.

The New Nation - PBS Learning Media: Six lesson plans focused on Alexander Hamilton, Thomas Paine, and Benjamin
Franklin; the lessons provide an overview and a procedure which includes vocabulary, additional resource materials, and
necessary background for the educator.

The Vermont 250th in the Classroom - Vermont 250th Commission: This document provides varied lessons and
resources to engage students in learning about the founding of the nation and the critical role that Vermont and
Vermonters played in the War for Independence and the legacy they left on America; suggested grade levels have also
been provided for elementary, middle, and high school students.

Westward Expansion, 1790-1850: Interactive Map - PBS Learning Media: Students can explore the territorial and

What Strategies Did Native Nations Use To Protect Their Sovereignty During the American Revolutionary War? -

A Sailor’s Life for Me - USS Constitution Museum: The War of 1812 is often referred to as our country’s second war of
independence. As a young nation, the United States' economy, territory, and rights of individual citizens were again
threatened by the British. A Sailor’s Life for Me! presents life at sea during the War of 1812 for those serving aboard USS
Constitution, one of the few naval vessels in America’s young navy, and now a national symbol, through interactive
games, primary sources, and Museum resources.
American Indians in a Changing World -The Gilder Lehrman Institute for American History: Students will examine

migrated across the United States. They will use the knowledge gained through their analysis to write their own songs,
poems, and letters.

Betrayal - C3IDM: This inquiry leads students through an investigation of leaders during the American Revolution. By
investigating the compelling question ‘Is betrayal always bad?’ students evaluate the actions of King George III and
George Washington. The formative performance tasks build on knowledge and skills through the course of the inquiry and

Elementary Curriculum: Colonial America to Reconstruction - The Gilder Lehrman Institute for American History:
This curated suite of resources for elementary school educators will help expand your knowledge of important topic areas
in the elementary curriculum through a selection of six curated topics, such as Colonial America and the American
Revolution. You will find lesson plans, student activity sheets, videos for students, interactive maps and timelines,
recorded lectures, and free professional development courses.
Frederick Douglass: Orator, Editor, and Abolitionist - PBS Learning Media: Students explore what it means to speak
out for your beliefs, or to right a wrong. After watching a short video, they will reflect on Douglass’s courage and the
importance of literacy in his activism. They will read excerpts from Douglass’s autobiographies and examine an 1850
etching of Douglass being pulled away from a stage prior to giving a speech. Finally, they will reflect on Douglass’s
importance as both a historical figure and as a role model for their own lives.

“Heroes of the American Revolution”- C3 IDM (5th grade inquiry that may require support): This inquiry leads students
through an investigation of the American Revolution and the varying impacts of individuals who could be deemed heroes
based on their impact. The compelling question, “What defines a hero?” allows students to consider all the actions of an
individual and if they think that they are worthy of the title of hero.

“New France” - C3 IDM (5th grade inquiry that may require support): This inquiry focuses on the emergence, growth, and
collapse of the New France colony in North America. French explorers, missionaries, traders, and settlers established an
important presence in North America, beginning with Jacques Cartier’s explorations in 1534 and continuing through the
19th century. The development of the fur-trading industry, along with a relatively stable relationship with Native peoples in
North America, peaked around 1710.

Pre- 1860 Disunion - American Battlefield Trust: This lesson allows students to look at pre-War cultures and
economies of the Northern and Southern states and includes the actions of John Brown at Harper’s Ferry.

The Mexican-American War - PBS Learning Media: In this clip from Latino Americans, students will learn about the
Mexican-American War. Until the mid 1800s, the region that is now the western United States was part of Mexico.
Meanwhile, inspired by the notion of Manifest Destiny, United States leaders tried to buy and later went to war over this
expanse of land. The result was that not only land, but people and long-standing settlements also became part of the
United States.

students will examine an 1864 photograph of Truth and read excerpts of her most famous speech. The lesson concludes
with students choosing a new name for a current-day exemplar of perseverance.
