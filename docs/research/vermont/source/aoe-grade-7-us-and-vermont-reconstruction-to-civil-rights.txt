   Grade 7 – Vermont and the U.S. – From Reconstruction to the Civil Rights
                             Movement (Draft)
Overview
In grade 7, students will build on their knowledge of Vermont and U.S. politics, geography, economics, and peoples as
they re-visit the sectional conflicts over slavery that continued into Reconstruction and beyond and led to the long struggle
in the 19th and 20th centuries for civil rights. Grade 7 content spans decades; takes students through Reconstruction,
industrialization, and world wars, and allows students to dig into critical topics and America’s role in them, such as human
rights, the Holocaust, genocide, women’s suffrage, and atomic weaponry. By the end of grade 7, students will have a
broad understanding of the roles that politics and power have played both locally and on a world stage; be aware of their
changing cultural and physical environments; and gain keen insight as to the ways in which individuals, businesses,
governments, and societies make decisions. This broad journey provides a framework for understanding and will allow for
deeper analysis of history in high school.

Looking Back
In grade 6, students examined how movement and migration impacted the interactions between expanding civilizations
through conquest and trade, including the physical and political geography and ancient societies of Latin America, South
and East Asia, Oceania, and Europe.

Looking Ahead
In grade 8, students will utilize past knowledge and understanding to expand their focus on the core concepts and
principles underlying America’s democracy. Students will collaborate to identify a problem(s) and develop solutions that
would require government involvement and participate in student-led civics projects. Students will also learn about the
workings of both national and global economies and develop an understanding of the decision-making processes
of individuals, businesses, and governments and how they are connected.

Inquiry
Why inquiry? Using inquiry as an instructional model for the teaching of social studies allows students to authentically ask
questions, search for and analyze evidence, and provide explanations and arguments to challenges facing their world.
       The inquiry instructional model is driven by authentic questions or problems that have relevance for children.
       Students learn content, skills, and dispositions as they seek answers to questions and as they apply their learning
       to take informed action. Pioneered by Dewey (1938), inquiry is an attempt to make classroom experiences feel
       more like the authentic, curiosity-driven experiences that motivate learning outside of school. (Using Inquiry to
       Prepare Students for College, Career, and Civic Life, elementary edition, p. 22)
The Inquiry Arc is defined in Dimension 1 (questioning) of the College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social
Studies State Standards. In this Framework, Dimension 3 (evidence sourcing), and Dimension 4 (communicating), as well
as some evidence indicators found within Dimension 2 – History, have been combined with Dimension 1 into a singular
Inquiry Process, as evidenced below. This Inquiry Process begins in the elementary grades and increases in depth and
complexity as it spans through high school, preparing students to spiral their learning and skill acquisition in the social
studies for college and career readiness.
Inquiry Process (K-12): Students act as social scientists as they ask questions, think critically, solve problems, and
share conclusions.

Resources
Fair Labor Practices, Current and Historical- New Jersey: Then and Now - PBS Learning Media: Working conditions
in the Paterson silk industry and the farms of the Pinelands varied for the immigrant and seasonal laborers. Labor
movements and leaders like Elizabeth Gurley Flynn in Paterson and Elizabeth White at Whitesbog worked to improve the
conditions of workers including fair hours, conditions, and benefits. This lesson will examine the labor conditions of
historical industries in New Jersey while comparing these with modern fair labor practices and issues both in the country
and around the world.
Gilded Age and Progressive Era - Bill of Rights Institute: In these three units - with 8 lessons and 2 videos - students
will respond to the following inquiry: “How did Americans at the turn of the 20th century address the rapid technological,
demographic, and economic changes around them?” Provide students an in-depth look at this formative period in United
States history. Through primary-source-based activities and engaging narratives, students will be exposed to this
fascinating period and analyze its numerous parallels to today.
Industrial Revolution: Uncovering America - PBS Learning Media: How did the Industrial Revolution change the
United States? What makes industrialization possible? How does art reflect the varying experiences within a capitalist
economy? In suggested activities, students consider multiple viewpoints of images from the Industrial Revolution;
research and evaluate arguments for and against capitalism; and investigate their own community to create a portrait of
their local industry.
The Industrial Age in America: Robber Barons and Captains of Industry - National Endowment for the
Humanities: In this lesson students will be able to evaluate the actions taken by various captains of industry and robber
barons of the time.
The Industrial Age in America: Sweat Shops. Steel Mills, and Factories - National Endowment for the Humanities:
In this lesson, students will analyze the working conditions that led to the Haymarket Affair, the Homestead Strike, and the
Triangle Shirtwaist Factory Fire and the actions taken by the managers and workers involved in the incidents, and
evaluate the significance of the featured events to the labor movement, the industrialists involved, and the attitude of the
American people toward working conditions in the United States.

Resources
A Cheyenne Odyssey - Mission US: A Public Media Project: Mission US is an award- winning media project that
immerses young people in transformational moments in US history; teacher guide. In “A Cheyenne Odyssey,” players
become Little Fox, a Northern Cheyenne boy whose life is changed by the encroachment of white settlers, railroads, and
U.S. military expeditions. As buffalo diminish and the U.S. expands westward, players experience the Cheyenne’s
persistence through conflict and national transformation.

A Cold Reception: Anti-Immigrant Sentiment in the United States - PBS Learning Media: This media-enhanced
lesson plan will use video from Dr. Henry Louis Gates Jr.'s PBS series Faces of America to explore the hostile reception
immigrants have often received from anti-immigrant "nativists" in an America not always eager to accept them and the
change they represent.

A People on the Move- Great Migrations - PBS Learning media: In this lesson, students will contextualize the Great
Migration (1910–1940) to better understand its importance and impact on Black Americans and the country. Students will
discuss the concepts of resistance and cultural flourishing in the context of the Great Migration. Additionally, students will
participate in historical research and presentation, as well as creative expression, to dive deeper into the challenges and
triumphs of this historical moment.

City of Immigrants - Mission US: A Public Media Project: Mission US is an award- winning media project that
immerses young people in transformational moments in US history; teacher guide. In “City of Immigrants,” players
navigate New York’s Lower East Side as Lena, a young Jewish immigrant from Russia. Trying to save money to bring her
parents to America, she works long hours in a factory for little money and gets caught up in the growing labor movement.

Hunter-Dunbar Expedition - C3 IDM: This inquiry leads students through an investigation of the Hunter-Dunbar
expedition, commissioned by President Thomas Jefferson after the purchase of the Louisiana Territory from France. By
investigating the compelling question “Is the Hunter-Dunbar Expedition Worth Remembering?” students evaluate the
contributions Dr. George Hunter and William Dunbar made in the discovery and cataloguing of natural resources,
geographic features, and Native American groups in the lower portion of the Louisiana Territory (present-day Arkansas).
The formative performance tasks build on knowledge and skills through the course of the inquiry and help students draw
evidence from articles, charts, graphs, and maps while justifying their reasoning in writing.

Immigration and Displacement: Uncovering America - PBS Learning Media: In this set of artworks and suggested
activities, students will explore the motivations of immigration and causes of displacement throughout American history,
practice telling stories, investigate transformational journeys, and reflect on the American dream.

Population Profile of Our New Nation: A Comparison of the 1790 and 1800 Censuses - Statistics in Schools: In
this activity, students will refer to the 1790 and 1800 censuses to compare the total population with the population of
enslaved people, drawing conclusions from the data.
Promised Land - C3 IDM: This inquiry leads students through an investigation of immigration experiences in the United
States. By investigating the compelling question “Is America the ‘Promised Land’ for the world’s immigrants?” students
evaluate the motivating factors and deterrents for immigration to the United States both in the past and present. The
formative performance tasks help students build on knowledge and skills through the course of the inquiry and allow
students to recognize the push/pull factors for immigrants throughout our history.

They’re Coming to America: Immigrants Past and Present - PBS Learning Media: In this lesson, students explore the
history of this nation of immigrants. In the introductory activity, students identify their own countries of heritage, as well as
those of their classmates. Students then identify ethnic groups that migrated to the United States during various historic
waves of immigration. In the learning activities, students watch video segments from Faces of America to develop an
understanding of key motivations for immigration and explore online resources to examine specific immigrant experiences
from various points in American history. In the culminating activity, students utilize their historical knowledge and
examination of case studies to develop a brief narrative summarizing the experiences, aspirations, and emotions of a
hypothetical immigrant to then the past or the present.

Up from the Dust - Mission US: A Public Media Project: Mission U.S. is an award- winning media project that
immerses young people in transformational moments in U.S. history; teacher guide. In “Up from the Dust,” players take on
the roles of twins Frank and Ginny Dunn, whose family wheat farm is devastated by the Great Depression and Dust Bowl.
As they experience the hardships of the 1930s, players learn about Americans’ strategies for survival – as individuals,
communities, and a nation.

The American Revolution- PBS Learning Media: This Ken Burns series offers media clips with lessons for grades 6-8:
“Demographics of the British Colonies”, Native Americans and British Colonial America: Sovereignty, Diplomacy, and
Resistance”, and “Patriot, Loyalist Neutral: Differing Perspectives on the American Revolution”.
African Americans in the Gilded Age - Bill of Rights Institute: Constitutional amendments were ratified during and
after the Civil War to protect the natural and civil rights of African Americans. Despite these legal protections, the condition
of African Americans significantly worsened in the last few decades of the nineteenth century. In this 120-minute lesson,
Students will trace and evaluate efforts to achieve equality and protect the natural rights of blacks during the period
following the Civil War and analyze primary source documents and respond to a document-based question concerning
efforts to achieve racial equality.
An Uphill Battle: Fighting Injustice on the Homefront During WWI - National Archives Harry S. Truman Museum:
Students will rotate through four learning centers where they will read primary and secondary sources about injustices
during World War I. This will culminate in brainstorming solutions to each type of injustice.
Civil Rights Movement: The Declaration’s Influence - PBS Learning Media: In this interactive lesson, students
explore key speeches given by Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr., during the 1960s civil rights movement. They will analyze how
he employed language to connect the movement to the Declaration of Independence and to justify its goals.
Fighting for the Right to Fight - The National World War II Museum: This middle school guide provides an
introductory essay, profiles of prominent wartime African Americans, and three primary source-based lesson plans.
Korea’s Place in Teaching Social Studies - World History Digital Education. In partnership with the National Council
for Social Studies (NCSS), the World History Digital Education Foundation offers a set of free resources for social studies
teachers to incorporate lessons on the history, geography, economy, and civic society of the Republic of Korea. The
Learning Module is composed of four modules: History, Geography, Economics, and Civics; each content area provides
inquiry lessons, resources, and activities.

Reconstruction: Middle School Inquiry - American Battlefield Trust: In this inquiry lesson, students will respond to

community as well as communities across the country. Find lessons archived by time period, state, or various themes-
such as African American History, American Presidents, Civil Rights, and Conservation.

centuries. The history of suffrage offers an opportunity to examine women’s roles at critical points in the nation’s history,
and to think about the impact of women’s voting behavior on politics in our time.
