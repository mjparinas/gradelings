                       Grade 8 – Civics and Economics in Action (Draft)
Overview
In grade 8, students will utilize past knowledge and understanding to expand their focus on the core concepts and
principles underlying America’s democracy; the structure and limits of the national government as set forth in the United
States Constitution, including an examination of democratic ideals; and the role of the citizen in a democratic society.
Students will collaborate to identify a problem(s); develop solutions that would require government involvement; and
participate in student-led civics projects focused on developing civic knowledge, skills, dispositions, and media literacy.
Students will also learn about the workings of local, national, and global economies and develop an understanding of the
decision-making processes of individuals, businesses, and governments and how they are connected. Gaining an
understanding of economic resources, such as human capital, land, technology, and investments, and how they play a
role in big-picture decision making in a global environment will assist students in making informed decisions in a wide
variety of contexts.

Looking Back
In grade 7, students built on their knowledge of Vermont and United States politics, geography, economics, and history as
they re-visited the sectional conflicts over slavery that continued into Reconstruction and beyond and led to the long
struggle in the 19th and 20th centuries for civil rights.

Inquiry
Why inquiry? Using inquiry as an instructional model for the teaching of social studies allows students to authentically ask
questions, search for and analyze evidence, and provide explanations and arguments to challenges facing their world.




Contact: Martha Deiss, Curriculum & Instruction Division, at Martha.Deiss@vermont.gov
       The inquiry instructional model is driven by authentic questions or problems that have relevance for children.
       Students learn content, skills, and dispositions as they seek answers to questions and as they apply their learning
       to take informed action. Pioneered by Dewey (1938), inquiry is an attempt to make classroom experiences feel
       more like the authentic, curiosity-driven experiences that motivate learning outside of school. (Using Inquiry to
       Prepare Students for College, Career, and Civic Life, elementary edition, p. 22)
 The Inquiry Arc is defined in Dimension 1 (questioning) of the College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social
Studies State Standards. In this Framework, Dimension 3 (evidence sourcing), and Dimension 4 (communicating), as well
as some evidence indicators found within Dimension 2 – History, have been combined with Dimension 1 into a singular
Inquiry Process, as evidenced below. This Inquiry Process begins in the elementary grades and increases in depth and
complexity as it spans through high school, preparing students to spiral their learning and skill acquisition in the social
studies for college and career readiness.
Inquiry Process (K-12): Students act as social scientists as they ask questions, think critically, solve problems, and
share conclusions.

Resources
Analyzing Correlations Between Education and Income - Statistics in Schools: Students will explore maps
containing census data from 1950 through 2000. They will analyze how education levels and median household incomes
have changed over time and determine how the two might be correlated. Students will also come up with ideas for policies
that could help address issues related to income and education.
Fiscal Policy and Monetary Policy: What’s the Difference? - Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond: Activities and
lesson plans for students to understand the policy differences and the role of the Federal Reserve in influencing the
economy.
Illicit: The Dark Trade - National Geographic: Resources are unevenly distributed across the surface of the Earth, and
no one country has all the resources it needs. Therefore, each country must trade with others, resulting in a world of
global economic interdependence—an exchange between “producers” (those who produce or manufacture goods) and
“consumers” (those who purchase or use the goods). The two following activities help students understand the concept of
economic interdependence between different countries, and understand terms such as trade, import, and export. A
trading simulation will help students understand the conditions that encourage or discourage trade among countries. In

 Grade 8 - Civics and Economics in Action (Draft)         Page 13 of 18
 (Revised: November 12, 2025)
the activities, students will split into country groups and employ product-unit trading cards to represent the net worth of
five world countries. They will use these trading cards to obtain goods not available in their country and to disperse goods
that their country produces. Students will discover that some commodities are more desirable than others and that shifts
in an economic market can directly affect the price of goods.
The Federal Reserve and You - Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia: In this lesson, students view a film segment
from The Federal Reserve and You that introduces the Federal Reserve. Using an anticipation/reaction guide, students
learn what the Fed is, what it does, and what its impact on the economy is.
The Story of Monetary Policy - Federal Reserve Bank of New York: This lesson plan allows students to discuss the
ways in which monetary policy can help reduce periods of economic sickness.
Where Should I Live? Using U.S. Census Bureau Data to Make Decisions - Statistics in Schools: Students can use
this lesson as a means to learn and interpret data when they are considering their public policy. Students will use the U.S.
Census Bureau’s QuickFacts data access tool to examine information about three cities, including population, education,
and income data. Students will draw conclusions about life in those three cities to determine which city they would like to
live in as an adult.
Why We Pay Taxes - NextGen Personal Finance: This unit 3, lesson 4 lesson helps students understand fiscal policy
as they analyze why federal taxes are removed from their paycheck.
