INTRODUCTION TO THE K-12 MONTANA ENGLISH LANGUAGE ARTS AND
LITERACY CONTENT STANDARDS
This document outlines the Montana Content Standards for English Language Arts and Literacy. Adopted in 2025 and implemented in 2027, the
standards are designed to expose students to ELA content that will ensure their readiness for college, career, and community engagement. The
Montana Content Standards for English Language Arts and Literacy do not dictate curriculum or pedagogy, but rather, drive curriculum creation. The
K-12 content standards describe what students should know, understand, and be able to do in these content standards. These K-12 standards define
end-of-year expectations and a cumulative progression designed to enable students to meet college and career readiness expectations no later than
the end of high school (Administrative Rule 10.53.101).
The Montana English Language Arts and Literacy Content Standards also reflect the constitutional mandate to provide instruction that includes the
distinct and unique heritage and contemporary contributions of American Indians in a culturally responsive manner (Montana Constitution Article X
Section 1(2) and statutes §20-1-501 and §20-9-309 2(c), MCA). This allows students to recognize and respect the historical and contemporary
knowledge across the unique cultures of Montana’s 12 federally recognized tribes, ensuring that the teaching of ELA in Montana integrates cultural
understanding, respect, and relevance for all Montana students.



Purpose of this Document
This document serves as a comprehensive resource to support a K-12 perspective on ELA education in Montana and is designed to assist educators,
schools, and districts in understanding and implementing the Montana Content Standards for English Language Arts and Literacy. While it does not
provide in-depth, grade-specific guidance, those resources will be made available separately. Specifically, this resource aims to:
   •   Clarify the purpose and goals of the Montana Content Standards for English Language Arts and Literacy.
   •   Provide an overview of the standards for educators, families, students, and others invested in K-12 ELA education.
   •   Offer guidance on ELA instruction from a K-12 perspective, while directing users to grade-specific guidance documents for more detailed
       recommendations.
   •   Support schools and districts in implementing ELA practice and content standards effectively across all grade levels.
For grade-level support, please refer to the expanded grade-level specific guidance documents, which outline special considerations, examples, and
elaborations by grade.

Frequently Asked Questions - Navigating the K-12 Standards
What are the standards?
Content standards define the knowledge or skills that every student should know and be able to do at the conclusion of a particular grade level (REL
Southeast, 2020). Montana’s ELA content standards begin with kindergarten and continue through 12th grade, providing a clear progression of
learning that builds a strong foundation in literacy concepts and skills related to reading, writing, speaking, and listening.
In addition, Montana includes K–12 ELA literacy practices intended to guide the development of cognitive and “soft” skills such as critical thinking,
collaboration, and more in age-appropriate ways throughout a student’s education. Literacy practices are the broad habits, skills, and ways of thinking
that are essential for effectively and authentically engaging with texts and the real world. They are intended to promote a deeper understanding of
English language arts by encouraging students to develop and practice transferable skills necessary for to pursue diverse opportunities and success
in post-secondary education, careers, and community participation in a rapidly evolving world. The literacy practices are designed to be integrated
throughout the teaching and learning of ELA in combination with the ELA content standards.

How should the coding scheme be read and understood?
The Montana Content Standards for English Language Arts and Literacy use a structured coding system to help educators, schools, and invested
parties efficiently locate individual standards within the general framework. The coding structure is consistent across K-12, although the inclusion of
sub-standards as referenced with lowercase letters should be noted specifically for Reading - Foundational standards in grades K-3.
Elements across the K-12 Standards
      K-12 standards follow a coding scheme that includes the following elements:
           1. State indicator: MT
              Every Montana ELA content standard begins with the MT designation, signifying that the standard is unique to the state of Montana
              and reflects the standards Montana has codified within its Administrative Rules.

           2. Grade Level: Letter or Number
              This element identifies the grade level of the standard:
              • Kindergarten is indicated by “K”
              • Grades 1-8 are represented by their respective numbers (e.g., “1” for Grade 1)
              • High School (9-12) standards are designated by “9-10” or “11-12.”
                                                                                                                                                   5


           3. Strand: Abbreviated Representation
              Each standard is categorized within a strand, represented by a short abbreviation. A strand within a set of K-12 content standards for
              ELA is a broad organizational category that groups related to standards around a key ELA concept or set of concepts (REL Southeast,
              2020). Strands support the organizational structure and help illustrate the progression of literacy concepts and skills across grade
              levels. The strands within ELA include Reading, including the sub-strands of Foundational, Vocabulary, Fluency, and Comprehension;
              Writing; Research and Inquiry; and Speaking and Listening.

           4. Standard Item: Number
              This element specified the standard’s sequence within a particular strand. If a standard includes sub-items, as is the case for
              foundational reading standards in grades K-3, a letter may follow this number to indicate any subdivisions within the standard.

MONTANA’S K-12 LITERACY PRACTICES
Montana’s Literacy Practices describe varieties of expertise that ELA educators at all levels should seek to develop in all students. These practices are
based upon fundamental skills central to both ELA as well as general educational, career, and civic success.
As part of the revision process for Montana’s ELA standards, the revision task force reviewed research compiled by the Regional Education Laboratory
(REL) Northwest (see Appendix H: References). In condensing the English Language Arts and Literacy Standards, they elected to create literacy
practices. This decision followed the lead of the mathematics content standards revision team, who had adopted the Common Core’s Standards for
Mathematical Practice in 2011 and revised them in 2023. While the ELA revision task force did not have prior Montana literacy practices to adapt, the
adoption of these reflects similar literacy or humanities practices adopted by other states, as well as the Common Core Habits of Mind.



What is a Literacy Practice Standard?
In a broad sense, literacy practices are habits, behaviors, or ways of engaging in reading, writing, speaking, listening, and viewing used for
meaningful purposes within a specific context. They are what students (and adults) do with texts, and they encompass skills (i.e., various reading
strategies), social practices (collaboration or problem solving), and expectations surrounding reading and writing. The Literacy Practices differ from
the grade-level ELA content standards in that they provide guidance for how students should engage with their learning, whereas the standards
identify what students should learn by the end of a given grade level. Literacy practices broadly guide the instruction, and standards identify the skill.
Literacy practices are not intended to be used as a checklist, a grading system, or a replacement for explicit and systematic literacy instruction.
The creation of Montana’s Literacy Practices was primarily based upon the Common Core descriptions of Students Who are College and Career ready
in reading, Writing, Speaking, Listening, and Language and the Montana’s Mathematical Practices (adapted from the Common Core State Standards
for Mathematical Practice). There is no one specific definition of literacy practices within the field of English language arts instructions; rather,
researchers across disciplines have used the term to identify text-based and discipline-specific meaning making activities (Moje, 2008); multimodal
meaning making (Kress, 2010); socio-cultural ways of using text (Street, 1995; Gee, 2015); and more. Montana’s Literacy Practices are unique but
share similarities with the Common Core student descriptors and Montana’s Mathematical Practices linked above, as well as with frameworks that may
be referred to within other states or contexts as graduate profiles, profiles of a learner, 21st century skills, literacy goals, lifelong practices, etc.
Montana’s Literacy Practices are intended to serve similar—although not identical—instructional purposes as Montana’s Mathematical Practices, which
focus on the processes and practices students use to apply their mathematical knowledge flexibly, accurately, and efficiently across a variety of
applications and scenarios (Harbin Miles & Williams, 2016). The Literacy Practices, while less instructionally prescriptive, also identify broad practices
and purposes for which students should apply literacy knowledge across educational, interpersonal, job-related, and civic contexts. Teachers and
designers of curricula, assessments, and professional development should connect Montana’s Literacy Practices to the English Language Arts and
                                                                                                                                                      7


Literacy Content Standards, as embedding literacy practices within instructional activities can enhance and enrich ELA learning. The Literacy Practices
support learning and often directly connect to specific ELA standards. They are also written with the intent of skill and knowledge transfer; educators
are encouraged to make explicit connections to other contexts–both across content areas and related to students’ own interests and career goals. As
K-12 practices, Literacy Practices provide a guidepost for horizontal and vertical instructional coherence. Spaced repetition, or coming back to the
practices in different learning contexts, is a learning practice that will improve retention of broad skills and help students transfer knowledge.
In addition to stating Montana’s eight Literacy Practices, the following section provides a rationale for each of the practices, broad embedded skills
that may be used to further define the practices, and a description of associated ELA content standards. Grade-level guidance documents will include
specific suggestions for how educators and families might support students in engaging in the individual practices. Appendix C illustrates correlations
between the ELA standards and Literacy Practices that are most apparently exemplified by or associated with the standard; however, these are
intended to be utilized as a starting point and are not prescriptive, nor do the suggested associations rule out unlisted Literacy Practices from being
paired with a given standard.



Literacy Practice 1 – Broad Literacy Engagement
Literate students routinely read a broad range of literary and informational texts for a variety of purposes.
Rationale

Routinely reading a wide range of literary and informational texts for varied purposes strengthens students’ overall comprehension and ability to
navigate complex ideas. Because the ways we read differ depending on genre and purpose, exposing students to diverse texts helps them learn how
to appropriately shift their reading strategies. For example, we know to approach a poem differently than we do a lab report, or an opinion piece
differently than a historical narrative. Making purpose explicit to students by providing them with short, clear purpose statements before reading
further deepens comprehension by helping them focus on what matters most in a given text.

Broad reading across subject areas also builds background knowledge and vocabulary, two of the most powerful contributors to reading
comprehension and learning. Through encountering many genres, structures, and styles, students develop a stronger rhetorical awareness of how
texts work—how an author’s choices about genre, text structure, audience, and purpose shape meaning. Regular reading across disciplines also
supports writing development, as students study the features, language, and organizational patterns of the texts they encounter. By engaging widely
and purposefully with texts, students become more flexible, informed, and confident readers and writers.
                                                                                                                                                         8


Broad reading includes attention by educators to text complexity, text accessibility, and knowledge-building coherence—not simply independent
reading volume and variety. Educators should be careful to incorporate logical knowledge-building scope and sequences through the texts they teach
in the classroom and include informational texts. Additionally, educators should attend to accessibility, providing supports for diverse learners within a
Multi-Tiered System of Supports model and including texts by and about Montana Indigenous Peoples across grade bands that are not limited to
specific units.

Embedded Skills
This literacy practice is comprised of the following broad skills, which students should be encouraged to demonstrate in developmentally appropriate
ways:
   ✓   Adjust reading strategies based on genre and purpose.
   ✓   Read broadly across subject areas to increase background knowledge and vocabulary.
   ✓   Make connections and comparisons across texts and disciplines.
   ✓   Independently select and read texts in a range of genres suited to their interests.

Literacy Practice 2 – Collaboration
Literate students collaborate with others using active communication skills.

Rationale

Practicing collaboration in literacy helps students become effective communicators who engage thoughtfully with peers to build understanding, co-
construct meaning, and refine ideas. Collaborative literacy experiences—such as peer discussions, group analysis, co-writing, and peer feedback—
support social competence, self-confidence, and positive classroom engagement, while allowing students opportunities to articulate their ideas, listen
actively, and respond respectfully to others.

Collaboration allows students to develop Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) skills, particularly those identified in the CASEL competencies of Social
Awareness, Relationship Skills, and Responsible Decision-Making and in the related competencies of the Montana Whole Child Skill Development
Competencies framework. Students develop social awareness by considering multiple perspectives, strengthen relationship skills through effective
communication and using teamwork to problem solve, and practice responsible decision-making as they evaluate information and make choices
together. By embedding collaborative literacy routines into instruction, students not only deepen comprehension and writing skills, but also develop
essential interpersonal skills needed for academic, personal, and civic success.

Embedded Skills
This literacy practice is comprised of the following broad skills, which students should be encouraged to demonstrate in developmentally appropriate
ways:
   ✓   Provide and utilize peer feedback when engaging in a recursive process of writing.
   ✓   Participate in collaborative peer discussions.
   ✓   Negotiate with peers to solve problems, co-write, or reach consensus.
   ✓   Work together with partners and small groups to develop proficiency across ELA standards.

Literacy Practice 3 – Creativity
Literate students engage creatively to express their thoughts, deepen their understanding, and make connections.
Rationale
Practicing creativity allows students to synthesize their learning by making connections across texts, ideas, and experiences while expressing
understanding in varied and authentic ways. Creative literacy experiences—such as inquiry-based projects, multimodal compositions, choice-driven
writing, and interpretive responses—increase student ownership of learning and support intrinsic motivation by giving students opportunities to
explore their voice and practice agency.
A focus on creativity also aligns with project- and inquiry-based learning and provides students with opportunities to experience productive struggle
through decision making, which strengthens resilience and supports emotional development. Engaging in creative problem-solving encourages
persistence, flexibility, and reflection, while also supporting mental well-being for both students and teachers. By embedding creativity into literacy
instruction, educators deepen comprehension and communication skills while fostering motivated, resilient learners prepared for complex academic
and real-world tasks.

Literacy Practice 4 – Critical Thinking
Literate students analyze, reason, problem solve, and make decisions.
Rationale
Practicing critical thinking in literacy helps students move beyond surface-level comprehension to analyze ideas, evaluate evidence, and make
meaning from complex texts. Through experiences such as close reading, questioning, discussion, and argumentation, students learn to examine
                                                                                                                                                          12


multiple perspectives, identify claims and evidence, and draw reasoned conclusions. These practices strengthen comprehension, support deeper
understanding, and help students apply literacy skills across disciplines and real-world contexts.
Critical thinking also supports responsible decision-making as students learn to assess the credibility of sources, recognize bias, and justify their
thinking. Engaging in analysis and reasoning requires productive struggle, encouraging students to persist, revise their thinking, and reflect on their
learning. By embedding critical thinking into literacy instruction, educators prepare students to navigate complex information, communicate
thoughtfully, and participate effectively in academic, civic, and personal contexts.

Embedded Skills
This literacy practice is comprised of the following broad skills, which students should be encouraged to demonstrate in developmentally appropriate
ways:

   ✓   Use evidence and reasoning to support interpretations, claims, or solutions.
   ✓   Evaluate multiple perspectives, sources, or possible solutions to inform decisions.
   ✓   Reflect on reasoning processes and revise thinking based on new information or feedback.

Literacy Practice 5 – Cultural Considerations
Literate students acknowledge and respect diverse cultural perspectives, including those of Montana Indigenous Peoples.
Rationale
Practicing cultural considerations in literacy helps students understand that texts are shaped by cultural identity, history, and perspectives. Through
reading, writing, discussion, and inquiry, students learn to recognize multiple ways of knowing, value diverse voices, and engage respectfully with
experiences different from their own. Centering cultural perspectives deepens comprehension, strengthens empathy, and supports more meaningful
and inclusive literacy learning.
In Montana, this practice is grounded in the state’s constitutional mandate for Indian Education for All (IEFA), which affirms the responsibility of
schools to teach about the distinct and unique heritage of Montana’s Indigenous Peoples. Integrating IEFA into literacy instruction ensures that Native
histories, cultures, and contemporary experiences are accurately represented and thoughtfully examined. By embedding cultural considerations into
literacy learning, educators support social awareness and critical reflection, while preparing students to participate respectfully in a diverse and
interconnected society.

Embedded Skills
This literacy practice is comprised of the following broad skills, which students should be encouraged to demonstrate in developmentally appropriate
ways:

   ✓   Reflect on their own perspectives and consider how learning about other cultures deepens understanding.
   ✓   Engage respectfully with perspectives, traditions, and knowledge systems, including those of Montana Indigenous Peoples
   ✓   Analyze how culture, history, and identity influence authors’ perspectives, purposes, and meanings.
   ✓   Use culturally responsive language and practices when discussing, writing about, or responding to texts.

Literacy Practice 7 – Strategic Technology Use
Literate students effectively and responsibly use technology to access and evaluate information to express ideas.
Rationale
Technology shapes how information is created, shared, and interpreted. To be fully literate, students must do more than operate devices; they should
understand how to strategically use technology to locate credible information, evaluate sources for accuracy and bias, and communicate ideas
effectively to varied audiences. These skills align with the International Society for Technology in Education (ISTE) Standards, which emphasize
digital citizenship, knowledge construction, creative communication, and educator responsibility in designing purposeful learning experiences using
technology. Instruction should focus not only on how to use tools, but also on when, why, and whether technology enhances students’ learning.
Ongoing conversations with students about authorship, artificial intelligence, collaboration norms, and digital footprints are crucial to help students
develop thoughtful practices and agency.
Strategic technology use also requires attention to student data privacy and ethical practices. Montana laws such as Online Protections for Pupils 20-
7-1325, MCA and Pupil Records—Online Privacy Protection 20-7-1326, MCA establish safeguards to protect student information and limit the use of
student data. Educators must carefully vet digital tools and teach students how their data is collected and shared. When technology integration is
intentional, privacy-conscious, and grounded in critical thinking, it strengthens students’ research skills, communication, and civic readiness.

Embedded Skills
This literacy practice is comprised of the following broad skills, which students should be encouraged to demonstrate in developmentally appropriate
ways:
                                                                                                                                                 17


   ✓   Use digital tools and platforms strategically to access information, texts, and media for a defined purpose.
   ✓   Use technology to express ideas clearly and effectively through written, oral, visual, or multimedia formats.
   ✓   Evaluate the credibility, relevance, and bias of digital sources using developmentally appropriate criteria.
   ✓   Synthesize information from multiple digital sources to build understanding or develop ideas.
   ✓   Practice responsible, ethical, and safe technology use, including proper citation and digital citizenship.

Literacy Practice 8 – Student Agency
Literate students take an active role in their learning, using guidance and feedback to connect their skills and knowledge to
practical applications, college readiness, and career development.
Rationale
Taking an active role in learning empowers students to become purposeful, reflective, and independent learners. When students set goals, monitor
their progress, and use guidance and feedback to improve their work, they strengthen both academic skills and self-direction. Connecting literacy
learning to practical applications helps students see the relevance of their skills beyond the classroom and supports deeper engagement.
This practice also prepares students for college, career, and civic contexts by fostering habits such as persistence, adaptability, and reflection. As
students apply literacy skills to authentic tasks—such as researching career pathways, establishing goals, communicating within CTE courses and
career experiences, or solving real-world problems—they develop the ability to transfer knowledge across settings. Embedding active learning into
literacy instruction supports readiness for postsecondary success by helping students understand how their skills connect to future opportunities and
responsibilities.

Embedded Skills
This literacy practice is comprised of the following broad skills, which students should be encouraged to demonstrate in developmentally appropriate
ways:
   ✓ Set goals, monitor progress, and reflect on learning to guide improvement.
   ✓ Apply literacy skills to authentic, real-world, academic, or career-related tasks.
   ✓ Make connections between current learning and future college, career, or civic goals.

What is an English Language Arts and Literacy Content Standard?
A content standard in ELA is a specific statement that defines the knowledge, skills, and understandings that students are expected to achieve at a
particular grade level or within a course of study (REL Southeast, 2020). Content standards articulate the what of student learning, providing clear
expectations for the concepts and skills that should be taught by teachers, and in which students should demonstrate proficiency by the end of grade
level.

Proficiency in the English Language Arts and Literacy Content Standards
The Administrative Rules of Montana (item 10.55.603) states “Local school districts shall develop and implement a proficiency-based learning model
that includes curriculum aligned to all content standards and appropriate learning progressions.” As a supportive resource, this section provides a
sample proficiency scale that illustrates how the ELA and literacy content standards can be assessed. This example is not mandatory and is intended
solely to guide districts in designing their own proficiency-based systems.
When assessing students on content standards, educators should consider multiple forms of evidence, including classroom observations, student
self-assessments, projects or performance tasks, formative and summative assessments, and student portfolios. Using clear rubrics and proficiency
scales can help educators consistently and accurately measure students’ progress and proficiency in ELA.

Notes on Proficiency:
   •   Proficiency is defined by clearly articulated learning objectives or standards. It is the ability to consistently demonstrate a sufficient level of
       knowledge, skill, or understanding to meet all specific standards or expectations.
   •   These targets are often broken into measurable criteria that describe what mastery looks like for a particular skill or concept.
   •   Proficiency is demonstrated through evidence of learning, such as assessments, projects, or performances that align directly with the
       standard.
   •   There are differing views on the quality versus quantity of evidence for mastery; some resources argue that a single demonstration of mastery
       suffices, while others argue that mastery should be established through multiple assessments. Districts are encouraged to explore and adopt a
       consistent methodology across classrooms that aligns with their pedagogical philosophy and instructional practice. Engaging in discussions at
       the local level about district and educator preferences greatly benefits students, educators, and families.

Montana English Language Arts and Literacy Content Standards Glossary
Automaticity - the ability to read words quickly and effortlessly
Connotative - describes implied ideas or associations beyond the denotative meaning of a word
Decoding - the ability to apply sound-symbol relationships to read words
Encoding - the translation of spoken word or sounds into writing, or spelling
Etymology - the study of the origin of words and the historical development of their meaning
Figurative - describes non-literal or metaphorical use of language
Fluency - the ability to read a text accurately, with appropriate pace and expression; fluency includes the skills of accuracy, automaticity, and prosody
Grapheme - a letter or group of letters that represents a single phoneme
Morpheme - the smallest meaningful unit of language that cannot be further divided, including affixes, prefixes, suffixes, and roots
Morphology - the study of morphemes and how they are combined
Morphological awareness - the recognition, understanding, and use of morphemes that strengthens word recognition and spelling
Phoneme - an individual sound; English is made up of 44 phonemes
Phoneme-grapheme correspondence - the ability to match a phoneme (single sound) to a grapheme (written representation) and vice versa
Phonemic awareness - the ability to recognize and manipulate individual phonemes in spoken language
Phonological awareness - the ability to recognize and manipulate various sounds in spoken language, including word parts, syllables, rhymes, and
phonemes
Prosody - reading with appropriate expression and intonation, including pitch, tone, volume, and word emphasis
Rhetoric - the art of effective speaking or writing
Syllabication - the division of words into syllables

Appendix A: K-12 Correspondence with Common Core State Standards
An Important Note
The Montana State Standards are distinct from the Common Core State Standards (CCSS). While some Montana standards remain similar to their
CCSS counterparts, many have been significantly revised or newly developed. This correspondence information is provided as a reference tool to help
educators understand where conceptual connections may exist between the two sets of standards.
It is important to note that this correspondence does not imply equivalence. Educators should not assume that a CCSS-aligned resource
automatically addresses the expectations and requirements of the Montana ELA standards. Rather, this tool is intended to support educators in the
process of evaluating existing materials, identifying areas of overlap or deviation, and making informed decisions about curriculum and instruction
alignment to ensure that they are meeting the expectations of the Montana State Standards.
For the Montana Literacy Practices, no equivalent CCSS standards existed, unlike the 2025 Montana Mathematical Practices, which were based upon
the CCSS Mathematical Practices and the 2011 Montana Mathematical Practices.
Because many available curricular resources are built around CCSS, the following charts may serve as a starting point for alignment work in districts
and classrooms. However, each Montana English Language Arts and Literacy Standard must be reviewed individually to promote accurate delivery
during instruction. This is especially true for standards that reference Montana’s Indigenous Peoples and local communities, which are unique to our
state.

Appendix D: K-12 Science of Reading and Standards Correspondences
During the research stage of the ELA standards revision process, opportunities for enhancements to Montana’s content standards were identified. One
such goal was to update the standards to include the most recent research and evidence on effective English language arts and literacy teaching and
learning, integrating research on the science of reading and evidence-based literacy instruction at both the elementary and secondary levels.
The science of reading is defined by The Reading League as a “vast, interdisciplinary body of scientifically-based research about reading and issues
related to reading and writing” that has “culminated in a preponderance of evidence to inform how proficient reading and writing develop; why some
have difficulty; and how we can most effectively assess and teach and therefor, improve student outcomes through prevention of and intervention for
reading difficulties” (The Reading League, 2022).
This appendix aims to highlight reading components undergirding individual content standards, presented in the tables below. For the purposes of
identifying the intersections between foundational science of reading components and Montana’s ELA content standards, knowledge and skills from
the National Reading Panel’s (NRP) 2000 report and foundational literacy frameworks—including The Simple View of Reading (Gough & Tunmer 1986;
Hoover & Tunmer 2022), Scarborough’s Reading Rope (2001), and Duke and Cartwright’s Active View of Reading Model (2021)—are used to
constitute the following correspondence components:

Appendix E: Indian Education for All (IEFA) Integration Guidelines
The Montana English Language Arts and Literacy Standards also reflect the constitutional mandate to provide instruction that includes the distinct and
unique heritage and contemporary contributions of American Indians in a culturally responsive manner (Montana Constitution Article X Section 1(2)
and statutes §20-1-501 and §20-9-309 2(c), MCA). This allows students to recognize and respect historical and contemporary knowledge across the
unique cultures of Montana’s 12 federally recognized tribes, ensuring that the teaching of ELA in Montana integrates cultural understanding, respect,
and relevance for all Montana students.
This section includes general guidelines for integrating Indian Education for All across the K-12 continuum and identifies high-quality resources for
educators.

Requirements
Specific standards within Montana’s English Language Arts and Literacy Content Standards reference Montana’s Indigenous Peoples, although
educators are encouraged to incorporate culturally responsive content across standards. This reflects the state’s commitment to Indian Education for
All (IEFA) and is an essential component of student proficiency for those standards. Educators are expected to align instruction and assessment for
these standards with content that reflects the cultures, histories, and contributions of Montana’s 12 federally recognized tribes.
Instructional materials and classroom activities should be consistent with the Indian Education for All Framework, which provides detailed guidance
for integrating the Essential Understandings Regarding Montana Indians and recommended multicultural education approaches. This alignment
supports the intent of the standards and Montana’s constitutional commitment to preserving and honoring the cultural integrity of its Indigenous
communities.

Resources
There are many resources available to support educators in integrating IEFA into ELA. Some examples include:

   •   The OPI Indian Education For All webpage provides classroom resources, videos, and lesson plans.
           o OPI’s Indian Education for All program also offers Language Arts resources organized by grade level. However, keep in mind that
               resources from other subject areas may also work for integration within ELA lessons.
           o Class Sets of Books to borrow are available, along with full IEFA unit plans for commonly taught novels aligning. Note that while all of
               these texts highlight Indigenous knowledge, they do not all center upon Montana Indigenous peoples. Care should be taken to ensure
               that resources featuring Montana Indigenous peoples are integrated into unit plans.
   •   National Indian Education Association Digital Resources for Schools, Teachers, Parents, and Students. This resource provides professional
       learning, digital lessons and tasks, projects, and parent resources.
                                                                                                                                                   136


   •   Confederated Salish Kootenai Tribes (CSKT) educational resources although not content-specific, this resource includes lessons and
       activities that integrate many content areas, such as science, history, land management, and climate, through the perspective of the CSKT.
   •   Little Shell Tribe Our Culture shares many traditional teachings, cultural stories, and lessons. Although not content-specific, this resource
       provides an opportunity for educators to better understand the cultural practices and stories of the Little Shell People.
   •   PBS Learning Media – Montana PBS provides multiple videos, documents, lessons, and images relating to American Indians all over the US,
       including many of the 12 tribes of Montana.
   •   Directory of Indian Education Programs in Montana: This directory includes contact information for all Tribal governments, universities and
       colleges, Tribal education specialists, etc.
   •   Native Knowledge 360°: This website from the Smithsonian Museum of the American Indian is designed for educators and contains
       interactive lessons and resources to teach about Indigenous histories and cultures.

Appendix F: Assessment
This section aims to provide general guidance on statewide assessments. This can aid in understanding educator, school, and district responsibilities
related to assessing the Montana English Language Art and Literacy Content Standards.

State Assessment
State testing measures student proficiency and progress on state content standards over time. The participation requirement to administer statewide
assessments is required under federal and state law including:

   •   ESSA Section 1111(b)(2) Academic Assessments
          o (A) IN GENERAL.—Each State plan shall demonstrate that the State educational agency, in consultation with local educational agencies,
             has implemented a set of high-quality student academic assessments in mathematics, reading or language arts, and science. The State
             retains the right to implement such assessments in any other subject chosen by the State.
          o (B) REQUIREMENTS.—The assessments under subparagraph (A) shall— (i) except as provided in subparagraph (D), be— (I) the same
             academic assessments used to measure the achievement of all public elementary school and secondary school students in the State;
             and (II) administered to all public elementary school and secondary school students in the State; (ii) be aligned with the challenging
             State academic standards, and provide coherent and timely information about student attainment of such standards and whether the
             student is performing at the student’s grade level.

   •   ARM 10.56.101 Statewide Assessment
         o (1) By the authority of 20-2-121(12), MCA and ARM 10.55.603, the Board of Public Education adopts rules for state-level assessment
             in the public schools and those private schools seeking accreditation.
         o (2) The Board of Public Education (board) recognizes that the primary purpose of assessment is to serve learning. A balanced
             assessment system including formative, interim, and summative assessments aligned to state content standards, will provide an
             integrated approach to meeting both classroom learning needs and school and state level information needs. A balanced assessment
             system is structured to continuously improve teaching and learning and to inform education policy.
         o (3) may not be construed to require a school district to provide these assessments if the state does not have a current contract with
             test vendors for provision of these assessments to Montana school districts.
         o (4) The Superintendent of Public Instruction shall recommend in writing to the Board of Public Education any modifications to the
             single system of statewide assessments as set forth in (2). The Board of Public Education may consider recommended modifications
             as an information item on an agenda at a Board of Public Education meeting. At that meeting, the Board of Public Education may vote
             to list the recommendations as an action item on the agenda of a subsequent Board of Public Education meeting. Unless approved by
                                                                                                                                           138


         the Board of Public Education, no recommended modifications are effective, and no accredited schools may implement the
         recommended modifications.
       o (5) When developing a recommendation to the Board of Public Education for adopting statewide assessments the Superintendent of
         Public Instruction will include implications including alignment to content standards.
       o (6) The Superintendent of Public Instruction shall: (a) ensure Montana educators participate in the process; (b) ensure that all
         statewide test items are field tested before being used to determine proficiency; and (c) request approval from the Board of Public
         Education to allow for census field testing before determining proficiency.

•   ARM 10.56.102 Participation
      o (1) By the authority of 20-2-121(11), MCA, and ARM 10.55.603, the Board of Public of Education (board) adopts rules for statewide
          assessment in all public and accredited nonpublic schools.
      o (2) Statewide assessments approved by the Board shall be administered in all public and accredited nonpublic schools.
      o (3) School districts shall annually administer statewide assessments to all students in accordance with state and federal laws and
          regulations that meet or exceed the following specifications:
              ▪ (a) English language arts and mathematics assessments shall be aligned to Montana content standards in English language arts
                  and mathematics and administered in grades 3-8 and 11;
              ▪ (b) Science assessments shall be aligned to Montana content standards for science and administered in grades 5, 8, and 11;
                  and
              ▪ (c) Assessments shall be administered in the spring of each year.
      o (4) State-level assessments aligned to Montana-English language proficiency standards shall be administered to all students identified
          as English Learners (EL) in grades (K-12). These assessments shall be administered mid-school year.
